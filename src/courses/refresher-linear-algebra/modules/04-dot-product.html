04

Dot product, length and orthogonality

lecture 1, slides 30–40 · one number that carries all the geometry
READ FIRST Where geometry enters— ⊡ ✕

So far a matrix has been an array of numbers with rules for combining. Nothing has had a length, an angle, or a notion of two things being perpendicular. This module introduces all three at once, and remarkably they all come from a single quantity: the dot product.

The plan is short:

  1. Define the dot product as the special case of matrix multiplication where a row meets a column (nothing new needed).
  2. Dot a vector with itself and take a square root — that is its length.
  3. Dot two different vectors — Theorem 3.1 says the result equals $\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$, so it carries the angle.
  4. Set that to zero — that is orthogonality, which module 07 shows is a strong form of independence.
Why finance cares
Almost every quantity you meet later is a dot product wearing a costume: portfolio return $\mathbf{w}'\mathbf{r}$, the budget constraint $\mathbf{p}'\mathbf{n}=w_0$, an expected value $\sum_s p_s x_s$, a covariance. Orthogonality is the algebraic form of "uncorrelated", and it is what makes regression residuals behave.
DEF Dot product (scalar product)— ⊡ ✕

Take a row $(1\times k)$-vector $\mathbf{a}$ and a column $(k\times1)$-vector $\mathbf{b}$. By the multiplication rule of module 03 the shapes give a $(1\times1)$ result — a plain number:

$$\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}a_1&a_2&\dots&a_k\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\\ \vdots\\b_k\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a_\ell b_\ell\in\mathbb{R}$$

This is the dot product, also called the scalar product precisely because the output is a scalar. Example: $(1,2,3)\cdot(4,0,-1)=4+0-3=1$.

The transpose convention

Since our default is that vectors are columns, we need a transpose somewhere to make the shapes fit. The lecture states both conventions explicitly (slide 31):

two row vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$$\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}'=\sum_{\ell}a_\ell b_\ell$
two column vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$$\mathbf{a}'\mathbf{b}=\sum_{\ell}a_\ell b_\ell$

Both give the same number. The transpose is only there to satisfy the shape condition; the arithmetic is always "multiply matching entries, add them up", for any $n$-tuples of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Trap
$\mathbf{a}'\mathbf{b}$ is a number; $\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}'$ is an $(n\times n)$ matrix (the outer product from module 03). One misplaced prime turns a scalar into a matrix. Check the shapes: $(1\times n)(n\times1)=1\times1$ versus $(n\times1)(1\times n)=n\times n$.

Rules

Symmetric$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{a}$
Distributes over $+$$\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}$
Scalars pull out$(c\,\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathbf{b}=c\,(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})$

DEF Length of a vector— ⊡ ✕

Dot a vector with itself and you get the sum of its squared entries — always non-negative, so a square root is available. That square root is the length (Euclidean norm) of $\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^n$, written $\|\mathbf{u}\|$ or $|\mathbf{u}|$:

$$\|\mathbf{u}\|=|\mathbf{u}|:=\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}u_\ell^2}=\sqrt{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}$$

In $\mathbb{R}^2$ this is literally Pythagoras: the vector $(u_1,u_2)$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $u_1$ and $u_2$, so its length is $\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2}$. The formula above says: keep doing that in any number of dimensions, where you can no longer draw the triangle but the algebra is unchanged.

Two easy consequences worth stating: $\|c\mathbf{u}\|=|c|\,\|\mathbf{u}\|$ (scaling scales the length, and a sign flip does not shorten anything), and $\|\mathbf{u}\|=0$ only for the zero vector.

Check yourself: $\|(3,4)\|$, $\|(1,1,1)\|$, $\|(0,0,0,3)\|$. (Answers: $5$, $\sqrt3\approx1.73$, $3$. The last one shows that extra zero coordinates cost nothing — a vector's length only sees where it actually points. Full solutions in module 14, Exercise 2.)

THM 3.1 The dot product measures the angle— ⊡ ✕

Any two vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ determine a plane — even if $n$ is 50, the two arrows together span at most a 2-dimensional slice — and in that plane there is an honest angle $\theta$ between them. The theorem states the link:

$$\boxed{\;\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta\;}$$

where $\theta$ is measured in radians (or degrees, as long as you are consistent). Rearranged, this is a formula for the angle itself:

$$\cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}$$

Since $\|\mathbf{u}\|$ and $\|\mathbf{v}\|$ are never negative, the sign of the dot product is the sign of $\cos\theta$. That gives the three cases of slide 39:

same direction, $\theta=0$$\cos0=1$, so $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|>0$
perpendicular, $\theta=90^\circ$$\cos90^\circ=0$, so $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$
opposite directions, $\theta=180^\circ$$\cos180^\circ=-1$, so $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=-\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|<0$

More loosely: a positive dot product means the vectors broadly agree, a negative one means they broadly disagree, and zero means they carry no common component at all.

It is worth recalling why $\cos$ appears. On the unit circle of radius 1, the point at angle $\theta$ has coordinates $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$: the cosine is the horizontal coordinate, i.e. exactly "how far along the reference direction you have got". The dot product asks the same question about $\mathbf{v}$ relative to $\mathbf{u}$, then rescales by the two lengths.

Watch the sign change exactly as $\mathbf{v}$ crosses the vertical: that crossing is $\theta=90^\circ$, where the projection has zero length. Everything to the right of the vertical gives a positive dot product, everything to the left a negative one.

Notice also what the length slider does not change: the angle. Scaling $\mathbf{v}$ scales the dot product proportionally but leaves $\cos\theta$ untouched, because the extra length cancels in the ratio. Direction and magnitude are separate pieces of information, and the dot product mixes them in a very specific way.

DEF Orthogonality— ⊡ ✕

Theorem 3.1 justifies turning the perpendicular case into a definition that needs no geometry at all. Two vectors $\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are orthogonal if

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}u_\ell v_\ell=0$$

This is the payoff of the whole module. "Perpendicular" is a picture that only exists in 2 or 3 dimensions; "dot product equals zero" is an arithmetic test that works in $\mathbb{R}^{500}$ and can be checked in seconds.

Example: $(2,1)$ and $(-1,2)$ have dot product $-2+2=0$, so they are orthogonal — sketch them and you will see one is the other rotated by a quarter turn. Example in higher dimensions: $(1,0,0,7)$ and $(0,3,-2,0)$ have dot product $0$, and no drawing was required.

Two uses coming up
Module 07: a set of non-zero mutually orthogonal vectors is automatically linearly independent — orthogonality is a strong, easily checked sufficient condition for independence (the converse fails: independent vectors need not be orthogonal). Statistics: two mean-zero return series are uncorrelated exactly when their vectors of deviations are orthogonal, which is why "orthogonal" and "uncorrelated" get used interchangeably in econometrics.

Check yourself: for which $k$ are $(k,2,-1)$ and $(1,k,3)$ orthogonal? (Set $k+2k-3=0$, so $k=1$.)
