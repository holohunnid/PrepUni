06

Linear combinations, subspaces and span

lecture 2, slides 13–26 · everything you can build from what you already hold
READ FIRST One idea, three names— ⊡ ✕

This module has a single idea in it, dressed three ways:

  • a linear combination is one recipe: scale some vectors, add the results;
  • a subspace is a subset that is closed under such recipes — a vector space living inside a bigger one;
  • the span of a set $S$ is the collection of all recipes you can write using the vectors of $S$. It is automatically a subspace, and it is the smallest one containing $S$.

Slide 13 calls the linear combination a "very important concept" and gives the reason directly: portfolio construction is linear combination, and in asset pricing, "can I replicate this payoff?" means literally "is this vector a linear combination of the ones I have?". Module 03 already showed that $A\mathbf{x}$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$; this module gives that observation its proper vocabulary.

Translation table, keep it handy
"is $\mathbf{b}$ in $\text{span}(\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_k)$?" $\;=\;$ "does $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ have a solution?" $\;=\;$ "can this payoff be replicated by the traded assets?" Three phrasings of one question, and you will meet all three.
DEF Linear combination— ⊡ ✕

Let $V$ be a vector space and $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\in V$. A linear combination of them is any sum of the form

$$\alpha_1\mathbf{v}_1+\alpha_2\mathbf{v}_2+\dots+\alpha_n\mathbf{v}_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\mathbf{v}_i\in V,\qquad \alpha_i\in\mathbb{R}$$

The $\alpha_i$ are the coefficients or weights. They may be any reals: positive, negative, or zero. Because a vector space is closed under both operations, the result is always back inside $V$ — combining never takes you outside.

Reading it in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Addition is the parallelogram rule: place $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ tail to tail, complete the parallelogram, and $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$ is the diagonal. Subtraction is the same construction applied to $\mathbf{u}$ and $-\mathbf{v}$, which is why $\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}$ is the arrow running from the tip of $\mathbf{v}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{u}$.

Finance reading
If $\mathbf{r}_i$ is the payoff vector of asset $i$ across states of the world and $\alpha_i$ is the number of units you hold, then $\sum_i\alpha_i\mathbf{r}_i$ is your portfolio's payoff vector. Negative $\alpha_i$ is a short position. So "the set of achievable payoffs" is a set of linear combinations — which is exactly what a span is.
DEF Subspace— ⊡ ✕

Let $V$ be a vector space. A set $W$ is a subspace of $V$ if

  1. $W$ is a subset of $V$, and
  2. $W$ is closed under linear combinations of its own elements: whenever $\mathbf{w}_1,\mathbf{w}_2\in W$ and $\alpha_1,\alpha_2\in\mathbb{R}$, we have $\alpha_1\mathbf{w}_1+\alpha_2\mathbf{w}_2\in W$.

Condition 2 is the same closure requirement as in module 05, compressed into one statement (take $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=1$ for addition, $\alpha_2=0$ for scaling). So a subspace is simply a vector space that happens to sit inside a larger one.

Every subspace contains $\mathbf{0}$
Why? Take any $\mathbf{w}\in W$ and $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=0$. Then $\mathbf{0}\in W$. Use this first, always. If a candidate set does not contain the origin, you are done in one line — as with $W=\{x_1+x_2+x_3=1\}$ in module 05.

All the subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^2$ (slide 20)

There are exactly three kinds, and it is worth knowing the complete list:

  • $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, the origin alone — trivially closed;
  • any line through the origin, i.e. $\{\alpha\mathbf{v}:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}$ for a fixed $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ — scaling stays on the line, adding two points of the line stays on the line;
  • the whole plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Everything else in the slide's figure fails:

a line not through the originfails — does not contain $\mathbf{0}$
the first quadrant (a shaded corner)fails — closed under addition, but $\lambda<0$ leaves it
a rectangle or any bounded regionfails — scaling by a large $\lambda$ escapes it
a single point $\neq\mathbf{0}$fails — no $\mathbf{0}$, and not closed under anything
the union of two distinct lines through $\mathbf{0}$fails — each line is fine alone, but adding one vector from each gives a point on neither

The same classification in $\mathbb{R}^3$ gives four kinds: $\{\mathbf{0}\}$, lines through the origin, planes through the origin, and $\mathbb{R}^3$. The pattern is by dimension, which is module 08.

Why it matters (slide 19)
The set of payoffs achievable by trading the available securities is a subspace of the space of all conceivable payoffs. Whether that subspace is the whole space is exactly the definition of market completeness.
DEF The subspace spanned by a set— ⊡ ✕

Let $S$ be a subset of a vector space $V$. The subspace generated (spanned) by $S$, written $[S]$ or $\text{span}(S)$, is the set of all linear combinations of vectors in $S$:

$$\text{span}(S)=[S]:=\Big\{\textstyle\sum_{s_i\in S}\alpha_i s_i,\ \ \alpha_i\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}$$

It is a subspace (any combination of combinations is again a combination), and it is the smallest subspace containing $S$: any subspace that holds all of $S$ must, by closure, hold every combination of them too.

Two examples in $\mathbb{R}^2$ (slide 23)

  1. $S_1=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ gives $[S_1]=\left\{\alpha\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}\right\}$ — the line through the origin in the direction $(1,2)$: the points $(1,2)$, $(2,4)$, $(-1,-2)$, $(0,0)$, and everything between. One vector spans a line.
  2. $S_2=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ gives $[S_2]=\left\{\alpha_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}+\alpha_2\begin{bmatrix}5\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$. The two directions are genuinely different, so between them they reach the whole plane: $[S_2]=\mathbb{R}^2$. (Formally: $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&5\\2&1\end{pmatrix}=1-10=-9\neq0$, which module 10 will show is exactly the test.)

Drawing them: $[S_1]$ is a single line at slope 2 through the origin; $[S_2]$ is the entire shaded plane. The jump from one vector to two independent ones is a jump from a 1-dimensional to a 2-dimensional object — and adding a third vector in $\mathbb{R}^2$ would add nothing at all, which is the subject of module 07.

The general pattern
In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the span of $k$ vectors is at most $k$-dimensional, and exactly $k$-dimensional when none of them is redundant. Fewer vectors than $n$ can never span $\mathbb{R}^n$: you cannot reach a plane with a single arrow, no matter how you scale it.
EXERCISE (slide 24) · spanned payoffs versus all possible payoffs

Three states of the economy (Boom, Flat, Recession), so security payoffs live in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Two securities are traded: $\mathbf{a}=(1,1,1)$ (a bond) and $\mathbf{b}=(2,1,0)$ (a stock).

a) Can $\mathbf{c}=(3,1,-1)$ be replicated? And $\mathbf{c}=(3,1,0)$?

"Replicate" means: find $\alpha,\beta$ with $\alpha\mathbf{a}+\beta\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{c}$. Writing it out state by state gives a linear system.

  1. For $\mathbf{c}=(3,1,-1)$: $$\begin{cases}\alpha+2\beta=3&\text{(Boom)}\\ \alpha+\beta=1&\text{(Flat)}\\ \alpha+0\cdot\beta=-1&\text{(Recession)}\end{cases}$$ The third equation gives $\alpha=-1$ immediately. Substituting into the second: $-1+\beta=1$, so $\beta=2$. Now the first equation must be checked, not solved — it is an extra condition: $-1+2(2)=3$ ✓.
  2. So yes: $\mathbf{c}=-\mathbf{a}+2\mathbf{b}$. Economically, short one bond and buy two stocks.
  3. For $\mathbf{c}=(3,1,0)$: the third equation gives $\alpha=0$, the second gives $\beta=1$, and the first then requires $0+2(1)=3$, i.e. $2=3$. Contradiction, so this payoff cannot be replicated — no.

b) Is $\text{span}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$? Is it all of $\mathbb{R}^3$? Describe it geometrically.

  1. It is a subspace by construction — every span is. It contains $\mathbf{0}$ (take $\alpha=\beta=0$) and any combination of two of its elements is again a combination of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.
  2. It is not all of $\mathbb{R}^3$: part (a) exhibited $(3,1,0)$ as unreachable. One counterexample settles it.
  3. Geometrically, $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are two non-parallel vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, so their span is a plane through the origin — a 2-dimensional subspace sitting inside 3-dimensional space.
The economic name for this
Two securities, three states: the market is incomplete. The achievable payoffs form a plane, and the payoffs off that plane — such as $(3,1,0)$ — simply cannot be manufactured. Module 08 answers the natural follow-up: how many more securities do you need, and which ones?
EXERCISE 6 (slide 25) · four candidate subspaces

1. Show $W=\{(a-b,\;3a-b,\;2a+b):a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

The efficient route is to recognise it as a span. Separate the $a$ and $b$ parts:

$$(a-b,\;3a-b,\;2a+b)=a(1,3,2)+b(-1,-1,1)$$

so $W=\text{span}\{(1,3,2),(-1,-1,1)\}$, and every span is a subspace. Done.

Direct verification, if you prefer it: take $\mathbf{w}_1$ generated by $(a_1,b_1)$ and $\mathbf{w}_2$ by $(a_2,b_2)$. Then $\alpha\mathbf{w}_1+\beta\mathbf{w}_2$ is generated by the parameters $(\alpha a_1+\beta a_2,\ \alpha b_1+\beta b_2)$, which are again real numbers — so the result is in $W$. Geometrically $W$ is a plane through the origin.

2. Show $W=\{(a-b,\;3a-b,\;2a+b-1):a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}$ is not a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Use the zero test. For $\mathbf{0}\in W$ we would need all three coordinates to vanish simultaneously:

$$a-b=0\ \Rightarrow\ a=b;\qquad 3a-b=0\ \Rightarrow\ 3a=b$$

Together $a=b$ and $3a=b$ force $a=b=0$. But then the third coordinate is $2(0)+0-1=-1\neq0$. So $\mathbf{0}\notin W$ and $W$ is not a subspace.

The structural reason: this $W$ is the plane of part 1 shifted by the constant vector $(0,0,-1)$. A shifted plane (an affine set) is not a subspace unless the shift happens to be zero — the same $1$-versus-$0$ distinction as fully-invested versus self-financing portfolios in module 05.

3. Is $W=\{(x,y,0):x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$?

Yes. It contains $\mathbf{0}=(0,0,0)$. And

$$\alpha(x_1,y_1,0)+\beta(x_2,y_2,0)=(\alpha x_1+\beta x_2,\ \alpha y_1+\beta y_2,\ 0)$$

whose third coordinate is still $0$, so the result is in $W$. This is the $xy$-plane: $W=\text{span}\{(1,0,0),(0,1,0)\}$, a 2-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

4. Is $W=\{(a,b):ab\ge0\}$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$?

No. The set is the union of the first and third quadrants (including the axes). It contains $\mathbf{0}$ and it is closed under scalar multiplication — multiplying both coordinates by $\lambda$ multiplies the product by $\lambda^2\ge0$, so the sign of $ab$ never changes. But addition breaks it:

$$(1,0)\in W\ \ (\text{since }1\cdot0=0\ge0),\qquad (0,-1)\in W\ \ (\text{since }0\cdot(-1)=0\ge0)$$ $$(1,0)+(0,-1)=(1,-1),\qquad 1\cdot(-1)=-1<0\ \Rightarrow\ (1,-1)\notin W$$

One counterexample is enough.

Method summary
Part 4 is the instructive one: it shows the two closure conditions are genuinely independent. A set can pass scaling and fail addition. Always check both — and remember that the union of two subspaces is almost never a subspace, for exactly this reason.
