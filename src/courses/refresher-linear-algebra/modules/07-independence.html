07

Linear dependence and independence

lecture 2, slides 28–40 · which vectors are genuinely new, and which are redundant
READ FIRST The single most useful concept in the course— ⊡ ✕

Module 06 asked what a set of vectors can build. This module asks the complementary question: is any of them redundant? A vector is redundant — linearly dependent on the others — if it is already reachable from them, so adding it enlarges nothing.

The reason this dominates everything that follows is that dependence is the hidden cause of nearly every difficulty you will meet:

  • a system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has no solution or infinitely many exactly when the columns of $A$ are dependent (module 12);
  • a square matrix has no inverse exactly when its columns are dependent (module 11);
  • $\det A=0$ is exactly the arithmetic test for dependence (module 10);
  • in finance, a dependent security is redundant — either it adds nothing to the market, or it is mispriced and there is an arbitrage.
Two equivalent definitions — know both
The lecture gives the definition first in the "$\mathbf{v}\in[S]$" form (slide 28) and then in the far more usable Theorem 4.1 form (slide 33). The second is what you compute with; the first is what it means.
DEF Dependence as reachability— ⊡ ✕

Let $S$ be a set of vectors in a vector space.

  • A vector $\mathbf{v}$ is linearly dependent on $S$ if it can be written as a linear combination of vectors of $S$, i.e. if $$\mathbf{v}\in[S]=\Big\{\textstyle\sum_{s_i\in S}\alpha_i s_i,\ \alpha_i\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}$$
  • A vector $\mathbf{v}$ is linearly independent of $S$ if it cannot, i.e. $\mathbf{v}\notin[S]$.

So "dependent" means "already inside the span" and "independent" means "outside the span" — genuinely new information, a direction the existing vectors cannot reach.

Example (Exercise 7, slide 29): with $[S_1]=\{\alpha(1,2):\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}$, the line of slope 2 through the origin —

  • $(2,4)$ is dependent on $[S_1]$: it equals $2\cdot(1,2)$, it lies on the line;
  • $(1,0)$ is independent: no multiple of $(1,2)$ has second coordinate 0 unless the first is 0 too, so $(1,0)$ is off the line.

Any vector off that line works, e.g. $(0,1)$ or $(3,1)$. Any other point on it, e.g. $(-1,-2)$ or $(0,0)$, is dependent.

Careful with the zero vector
$\mathbf{0}$ is dependent on every set (take all $\alpha_i=0$). So any set containing $\mathbf{0}$ is automatically a dependent set — a useful instant check.
THM 4.1 The test you actually use— ⊡ ✕

Let $V$ be a vector space and $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\in V$. Then $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ are linearly independent if and only if

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\mathbf{v}_i=\mathbf{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha_i=0\ \text{ for all }i=1,\dots,n$$

In words: the only way to combine them into the zero vector is the trivial way — with every coefficient zero. If some combination with at least one $\alpha_i\neq0$ produces $\mathbf{0}$, the set is dependent.

Why the two definitions agree: if some $\alpha_j\neq0$, you can divide by it and solve for $\mathbf{v}_j$,

$$\mathbf{v}_j=-\frac{1}{\alpha_j}\sum_{i\neq j}\alpha_i\mathbf{v}_i$$

exhibiting $\mathbf{v}_j$ as a combination of the others — dependence in the first sense. Conversely, if $\mathbf{v}_j$ is a combination of the others, moving it to the other side gives a non-trivial vanishing combination.

The two lecture examples (slide 34)

  1. $\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}$: from $\alpha_1\begin{bmatrix}2\\0\end{bmatrix}+\alpha_2\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\alpha_1\\ \alpha_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}$ we read off $\alpha_1=0$ and $\alpha_2=0$. Only the trivial combination works, so they are independent.
  2. $\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$: take $\alpha_1=1,\alpha_2=-2$ — then $\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}-2\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}$ with non-zero coefficients. So they are dependent. (Or $\alpha_1=1/2,\alpha_2=-1$; once one non-trivial combination exists, infinitely many do.)
How this becomes a calculation
Stack the vectors as the columns of a matrix $A$. Then $\sum_i\alpha_i\mathbf{v}_i=A\boldsymbol\alpha$ (module 03), and Theorem 4.1 reads: the columns are independent $\iff$ $A\boldsymbol\alpha=\mathbf{0}$ has only the solution $\boldsymbol\alpha=\mathbf{0}$. Independence is a statement about a homogeneous system — which is why modules 09–12 can settle it with rank and determinant.
IDEA What dependence looks like— ⊡ ✕

Geometrically, dependence is collapse: the vectors fail to open up as many dimensions as there are vectors.

2 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$dependent $\iff$ they lie on one line through the origin (one is a multiple of the other)
3 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$dependent $\iff$ they lie in one plane through the origin
$k$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $k>n$always dependent — you cannot fit more independent directions than the space has

Slide 31 makes the third-vector case concrete. Take $\mathbf{u}=(u_1,u_2,0)$ and $\mathbf{v}=(v_1,v_2,0)$, both lying in the horizontal plane. Then:

  • $\mathbf{w}=(0,0,w_3)$ with $w_3\neq0$ cannot be written as $\alpha_1\mathbf{u}+\alpha_2\mathbf{v}$ — every such combination has third coordinate $0$, and $w_3\neq0$. Independent, and the picture is an arrow sticking out of the plane.
  • $\mathbf{w}=(w_1,w_2,0)$ can: solving the two equations $\alpha_1u_1+\alpha_2v_1=w_1$, $\alpha_1u_2+\alpha_2v_2=w_2$ gives $$\alpha_1=\frac{w_1v_2-v_1w_2}{u_1v_2-v_1u_2},\qquad \alpha_2=\frac{u_1w_2-w_1u_2}{u_1v_2-v_1u_2}$$ provided the common denominator $u_1v_2-v_1u_2$ is non-zero. Dependent, and the picture is three arrows lying flat in one plane.

That denominator is worth staring at: it is the determinant of $\begin{pmatrix}u_1&v_1\\u_2&v_2\end{pmatrix}$. When it vanishes, $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are themselves dependent and the formulas break down — the first hint of the connection developed in module 10. (Check the sanity case: with $u=(1,0)$ and $v=(0,1)$ the denominator is 1 and the formulas give $\alpha_1=w_1$, $\alpha_2=w_2$, as they must.)

Drag $\mathbf{v}$ onto the blue line — at $(3,1.5)$, or $(-2,-1)$, or $(1,0.5)$ — and the shaded plane collapses to that line. The number $u_1v_2-u_2v_1$ shown in the readout hits exactly zero at that moment, and stays zero along the whole line.

That collapse is the entire phenomenon. When it happens: the span loses a dimension, the pair stops being a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$, the matrix $[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{v}]$ loses rank and stops being invertible, and the system $[\mathbf{u}\ \mathbf{v}]\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ stops having a solution for most $\mathbf{b}$. Six statements, one event.

COR 4.2 Independence gives uniqueness— ⊡ ✕

If $\mathbf{v}_1,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n$ are linearly independent and

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\mathbf{v}_i=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_i\mathbf{v}_i$$

then $\alpha_i=\beta_i$ for every $i$. In words: when a vector can be written in terms of independent vectors, the coefficients are unique — there is only one recipe.

Proof (Exercise 8, slide 35). Suppose not, so some index $\tilde\imath$ has $\alpha_{\tilde\imath}-\beta_{\tilde\imath}\neq0$. Subtract the two sums:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_i-\beta_i)\mathbf{v}_i=\mathbf{0}$$

This is a vanishing linear combination with at least one non-zero coefficient, which contradicts Theorem 4.1 (independence allows only the trivial combination). Hence no such $\tilde\imath$ exists and $\alpha_i=\beta_i$ for all $i$. $\;\square$

Why this is the whole point
Uniqueness of the coefficients is uniqueness of the solution to $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$. In finance it is uniqueness of the replicating portfolio: if the traded securities are independent, there is exactly one way to manufacture a given payoff — which is what makes its price well defined. If they are dependent, there are infinitely many replicating portfolios, and requiring them all to cost the same is precisely the no-arbitrage condition.
TRAP Pairwise checks do not generalise— ⊡ ✕

Exercise 7 asks: is it correct to say two vectors are linearly independent if they are not scalar multiples of each other?

For exactly two vectors, yes. If $\alpha_1\mathbf{v}_1+\alpha_2\mathbf{v}_2=\mathbf{0}$ with, say, $\alpha_1\neq0$, then $\mathbf{v}_1=-(\alpha_2/\alpha_1)\mathbf{v}_2$, making one a multiple of the other. So "neither is a multiple of the other" $\iff$ "independent". (Note this correctly handles the zero vector: $\mathbf{0}=0\cdot\mathbf{v}$ is a scalar multiple of anything, so a pair containing $\mathbf{0}$ counts as dependent, as it should.)

For three or more vectors, no — and this is the trap. Consider

$$\mathbf{v}_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{v}_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{v}_3=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

No two of them are multiples of each other, yet $\mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2-\mathbf{v}_3=\mathbf{0}$: a non-trivial vanishing combination, so the set is dependent. Dependence is a property of the whole set, not of its pairs.

Rule of thumb
Any $k>n$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ are dependent — three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$, four in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and so on. If you spot that before computing, you have your answer for free.
EX 10 Orthogonal implies independent — but not the reverse— ⊡ ✕

1. Orthogonal (non-zero) vectors are independent. Show it for two vectors.

Let $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ with $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$. Suppose $\alpha\mathbf{u}+\beta\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$. Take the dot product of both sides with $\mathbf{u}$ and use bilinearity (module 04):

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot(\alpha\mathbf{u}+\beta\mathbf{v})=\alpha\,(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u})+\beta\,(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})=\alpha\|\mathbf{u}\|^2+0=\alpha\|\mathbf{u}\|^2$$

and the right-hand side is $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{0}=0$. Since $\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}$ we have $\|\mathbf{u}\|^2>0$, hence $\alpha=0$. Dotting instead with $\mathbf{v}$ gives $\beta\|\mathbf{v}\|^2=0$, hence $\beta=0$. Only the trivial combination vanishes, so by Theorem 4.1 the vectors are independent. $\;\square$

The argument extends verbatim to any number of mutually orthogonal non-zero vectors: dot the vanishing combination with $\mathbf{v}_j$ and every term dies except $\alpha_j\|\mathbf{v}_j\|^2$.

2. Show the reverse is not true: independent vectors are in general not orthogonal.

A single counterexample suffices. Take

$$\mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{v}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$$

They are independent (neither is a multiple of the other: any multiple of $\mathbf{u}$ has second coordinate 0). But

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=1\cdot1+0\cdot1=1\neq0$$

so they are not orthogonal. The angle between them is $45^\circ$ — different enough to be independent, not different enough to be perpendicular.

The logic, stated cleanly
Orthogonality is strictly stronger than independence: it is a sufficient but not necessary condition. That makes it a very convenient shortcut — spotting mutual orthogonality proves independence with no system to solve — but the absence of orthogonality proves nothing. This is also why orthogonal bases (module 08) are prized in practice: they give independence for free and make coefficients easy to compute.
