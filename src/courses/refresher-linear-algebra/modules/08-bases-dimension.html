08

Bases and dimension

lecture 2, slides 42–47 · the smallest set that still builds everything
READ FIRST Putting the two halves together— ⊡ ✕

Module 06 gave one requirement — span everything — and module 07 gave the other — waste nothing. A basis is a set of vectors that satisfies both at once: large enough to reach every point of the space, small enough that no member is redundant.

Bases are the reason coordinates exist. Once you fix a basis, every vector in the space has exactly one list of coefficients relative to it, and that list is the vector's coordinates. The number of vectors in a basis turns out not to depend on which basis you choose, and that invariant number is the dimension of the space — a single integer summarising how much room there is.

The finance version
A basis of the payoff space is a set of securities that can replicate every conceivable payoff with no redundancy. Its size — the dimension — is the number of securities you need. Whether the traded securities form a basis of the whole payoff space is exactly the question of market completeness, and the exercise at the end of this module answers it in a three-state economy.
DEF Basis— ⊡ ✕

A basis for a vector space $V$ is a subset $\mathcal{B}$ of vectors of $V$ which is

  1. linearly independent, and
  2. generating: every vector of $V$ can be expressed as a linear combination of vectors from $\mathcal{B}$, i.e. $$[\mathcal{B}]=\text{span}(\mathcal{B})=V$$

We say "$\mathcal{B}$ generates $V$". Drop condition 1 and you have a spanning set with fat in it; drop condition 2 and you have an independent set that misses part of the space. A basis is the exact balance point.

A basis is not unique

Slide 43 makes the point with two different bases of $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-2\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\end{bmatrix}\right\}$$

Both are independent and both span $\mathbb{R}^2$. The first is the standard (canonical) basis $\{\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2\}$, convenient because the coefficients of a vector in that basis are just its usual coordinates. The second is equally valid and would give the same vectors different coordinate lists — like measuring the same city with axes rotated.

Coordinates are unique once the basis is fixed

Given a basis, each vector of $V$ has exactly one representation as a combination of the basis vectors. This is Corollary 4.2 from module 07: the basis vectors are independent, so two representations of the same vector must have identical coefficients. Existence comes from condition 2, uniqueness from condition 1 — one condition each, which is why a basis needs both.

Why we insist on independence
Without it, coordinates would be ambiguous: the same vector would have many equally valid coordinate lists, and "the" representation would not exist. Redundancy is not just wasteful, it destroys well-definedness.
DEF Dimension— ⊡ ✕

A vector space is finite dimensional if the number of vectors forming a basis is finite; otherwise it is infinite dimensional (the space of all polynomials, for instance, or of all continuous functions).

Theorem 5.1. Any two bases of a finite-dimensional vector space contain the same number of elements.

Sketch of the proof. Suppose $\mathcal{B}=\{\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_m\}$ and $\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{c}_1,\dots,\mathbf{c}_p\}$ are both bases, with $p>m$. Every $\mathbf{c}_j$ lies in $V=[\mathcal{B}]$, so each is a combination of the $m$ vectors of $\mathcal{B}$; collect those coefficients as the columns of an $(m\times p)$ matrix $M$, so that $\mathbf{c}_j$ corresponds to column $j$. A vanishing combination $\sum_j\alpha_j\mathbf{c}_j=\mathbf{0}$ then amounts to $M\boldsymbol\alpha=\mathbf{0}$ — a homogeneous system with more unknowns ($p$) than equations ($m$), which always has a non-zero solution (module 12, case $k>n$). That would make $\mathcal{C}$ dependent, contradicting that it is a basis. So $p\le m$; swapping the roles gives $m\le p$, hence $m=p$. $\;\square$

Because the count is basis-independent, it deserves a name. A vector space is an $n$-dimensional vector space if it is finite dimensional with a basis of $n$ elements; we write $\dim V=n$.

The reference cases (Exercise 11)

$\mathbb{R}$basis $\{1\}$  (or $\{c\}$ for any $c\neq0$)$\dim=1$
$\mathbb{R}^2$basis $\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$$\dim=2$
$\mathbb{R}^3$basis $\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$$\dim=3$

In general $\dim\mathbb{R}^n=n$, using the standard basis $\{\mathbf{e}_1,\dots,\mathbf{e}_n\}$ where $\mathbf{e}_i$ has a 1 in position $i$ and zeros elsewhere. These vectors are visibly independent (a combination equals its own coefficient list) and visibly generating (any $\mathbf{x}$ equals $\sum_i x_i\mathbf{e}_i$).

Two shortcuts worth memorising
In an $n$-dimensional space: (i) any set of more than $n$ vectors is dependent; (ii) any set of exactly $n$ independent vectors is automatically a basis — you get "spanning" for free and never have to check it. Shortcut (ii) saves real time in exams.
EXERCISE 12 (slide 47) · spans, bases and dimensions

Let $S_1=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\right\}$ and $S_2=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

1. Do $[S_2]=\text{span}(S_2)$ and $[S_1]=\text{span}(S_1)$ coincide?

  1. $\text{span}(S_2)=\{(x,y,0):x,y\in\mathbb{R}\}$ — the $xy$-plane, a 2-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  2. For $\text{span}(S_1)$, test independence of the three vectors by putting them in a matrix and taking the determinant (module 10, expanding along the last row, which has two zeros): $$\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\0&1&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}=1\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=1\cdot(0-1)=-1\neq0$$ so the three vectors are independent.
  3. Three independent vectors in the 3-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ form a basis (shortcut (ii) above), so $\text{span}(S_1)=\mathbb{R}^3$.
  4. No, they do not coincide: $\text{span}(S_1)=\mathbb{R}^3$ while $\text{span}(S_2)$ is only a plane inside it. Concretely, $(0,1,1)\in\text{span}(S_1)$ but $(0,1,1)\notin\text{span}(S_2)$, since everything in $\text{span}(S_2)$ has third coordinate 0.

2. Find a basis for $[S_1]$.

$S_1$ itself is a basis: we just checked it is independent, and it generates $[S_1]$ by definition of span. So $\left\{(1,0,0),(0,1,1),(2,1,0)\right\}$ works, and $\dim[S_1]=3$.

3. Is a basis for $[S_1]$ also a basis for $\mathbb{R}^3$?

Yes, because $[S_1]=\mathbb{R}^3$ by part 1 — the two spaces are the same, so a basis of one is a basis of the other. (Had $S_1$ spanned only a proper subspace, the answer would have been no: a basis of a plane inside $\mathbb{R}^3$ has two elements and cannot generate $\mathbb{R}^3$.)

Find $\dim[S]=\dim\text{span}(S)$ for:

  1. $S=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\right\}$: one non-zero vector, so it is independent on its own and $\dim=\mathbf{1}$. The span is a line through the origin.
  2. $S=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}\right\}$: neither is a multiple of the other ($\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}=1-4=-3\neq0$), so both are needed and $\dim=\mathbf{2}$. The span is all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  3. $S=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix}\right\}$: three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ must be dependent, and indeed $(3,3)=(1,2)+(2,1)$. The third adds nothing, so $\dim=\mathbf{2}$ again — the span is still $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Insight
Part 3 is the crucial one: the number of vectors in $S$ is not the dimension of its span. Dimension counts only the independent ones — which is precisely the definition of rank in module 09. Note also that dimension can never exceed the ambient space: no set of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ has a span of dimension 3.
EXERCISE (slide 46) · how many securities to complete the market?

Three states of the world and two securities, $\mathbf{a}=(1,1,1)$ and $\mathbf{b}=(2,1,0)$ (the same pair as module 06).

a) What is $\dim(\text{span}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}))$?

The two vectors are independent — no scalar $\lambda$ satisfies $(2,1,0)=\lambda(1,1,1)$, since the first coordinate demands $\lambda=2$ and the third demands $\lambda=0$. Two independent vectors span a 2-dimensional subspace, so

$$\dim(\text{span}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}))=2$$

which is the plane through the origin found in module 06.

b) Minimum number of additional securities needed to complete the market, and why?

  1. Payoffs live in $\mathbb{R}^3$, since there are three states, and $\dim\mathbb{R}^3=3$.
  2. The market is complete when every conceivable payoff is replicable, i.e. when the traded securities span all of $\mathbb{R}^3$. That requires at least 3 independent securities.
  3. We currently have 2 independent ones, so the minimum number of additional securities is $3-2=\mathbf{1}$.
  4. But it must be the right one: any new security lying inside the existing plane — for example $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}=(3,2,1)$ — is redundant and raises the dimension by nothing. The new security must be linearly independent of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, i.e. off the plane.

c) Propose an extra security $\mathbf{d}$ so that $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{d}$ form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, and argue why it works.

Take $\mathbf{d}=(0,0,1)$ — an Arrow–Debreu security paying 1 unit in the third state and nothing otherwise. Stack the three as columns and take the determinant, expanding along the last column (two zeros):

$$\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\1&1&0\\1&0&1\end{pmatrix}=1\cdot(-1)^{3+3}\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=1\cdot(1-2)=-1\neq0$$

so the three are independent (module 10). Three independent vectors in the 3-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$ form a basis, hence they span $\mathbb{R}^3$ and every payoff is replicable — the market is complete, and by Corollary 4.2 the replicating portfolio of any payoff is unique.

Many other choices work; the only requirement is $\mathbf{d}\notin\text{span}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})$. The payoff $(3,1,0)$ shown to be unreplicable in module 06 is, by that very fact, another valid choice.

The dictionary
dimension of the payoff space = number of states. Rank of the payoff matrix = number of independent securities. Complete market = those two numbers are equal. Redundant security = a linearly dependent column. Unique replication (hence a unique price) = independence, via Corollary 4.2. Every finance statement here is a linear algebra statement in disguise.
