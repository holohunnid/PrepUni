08

Deriving OLS

lecture 2, slides 2–9 · the derivation you must be able to write from memory
READ FIRST Fit the line that makes the squared vertical misses smallest— ⊡ ✕

We want a line through the cloud that passes "as close as possible" to the points. OLS measures closeness by the sum of squared vertical distances and picks $b$ to make it as small as possible. Thanks to matrix algebra the solution is one formula. The matrix algebra is only a tool: "nothing conceptual", the slide says.

STEP 1 The criterion— ⊡ ✕

Univariate:

$$\min_{\{b_1,b_2\}}\sum_{i=1}^n\big(\underbrace{y_i-(b_1+b_2x_{i2})}_{\text{vertical distance}}\big)^2$$

Matrix form, for any $K$:

$$\min_b\ SSE=(y-Xb)'(y-Xb)=y'y-2y'Xb+b'X'Xb$$

How the product expands. $(y'-b'X')(y-Xb)=y'y-y'Xb-b'X'y+b'X'Xb$. The two middle terms are scalars and transposes of each other, hence equal: $b'X'y=(y'Xb)'=y'Xb$. So they add to $-2y'Xb$.

Slide question · dimensions

$SSE$ is $1\times1$. $(y-Xb)'$ is $1\times n$. Check: $(1\times n)(n\times1)=1\times1$ ✓.

STEP 2 F.O.C., normal equations, estimator, S.O.C.— ⊡ ✕
  1. F.O.C. Differentiate with the two rules of module 04 and set to zero: $\dfrac{\partial SSE}{\partial b}=0-2X'y+2X'Xb=0$.
  2. Normal equations ($K$ equations in $K$ unknowns): $X'Xb=X'y$.
  3. Solve, using A1b (so $(X'X)^{-1}$ exists): $$\boxed{\;b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y\;}$$
  4. S.O.C. $\dfrac{\partial^2SSE}{\partial b\,\partial b'}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial b}(-2X'y+2X'Xb)=2X'X$, positive definite by A1b. So it is a minimum (and the only one, since $SSE$ is a quadratic bowl).
  5. Scalar case $K=1$: $\dfrac{\partial^2SSE}{\partial b^2}=2\sum x_i^2>0$, the familiar condition.
The normal equations say $X'e=0$

$X'y-X'Xb=0\Leftrightarrow X'(y-Xb)=0\Leftrightarrow X'e=0$. This one line gives: residuals orthogonal to every regressor; with a constant, residuals sum to zero; and the whole geometry of lecture 3.

INTERACTIVE Find the minimum yourself— ⊡ ✕

Try this. Move the line by hand and watch the total red area. Try to beat $SSE=1.90$: you can't. "One gradient step" moves downhill along $-\partial SSE/\partial b$; the closed form jumps to the bottom in one go. The valley is long and tilted because $\bar x\neq0$: raising the slope and lowering the intercept almost cancel out. That is the negative covariance between $b_1$ and $b_2$ that you will see in module 17.

RUNNING EXAMPLE · OLS by hand in four lines
  1. $X'X=\begin{bmatrix}5&15\\15&55\end{bmatrix}$, $X'y=\begin{bmatrix}2+3+5+4+6\\1\cdot2+2\cdot3+3\cdot5+4\cdot4+5\cdot6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}20\\69\end{bmatrix}$
  2. $\det=275-225=50$, $(X'X)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1.1&-0.3\\-0.3&0.1\end{bmatrix}$
  1. $b=\begin{bmatrix}1.1\cdot20-0.3\cdot69\\-0.3\cdot20+0.1\cdot69\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}22-20.7\\-6+6.9\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1.3\\0.9\end{bmatrix}$
  2. Check the normal equations: $X'Xb=\begin{bmatrix}6.5+13.5\\19.5+49.5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}20\\69\end{bmatrix}=X'y$ ✓
W3 · EX 1 (from Greene) · OLS beats any other c, exactly by how much

For any $K\times1$ vector $c$, prove $(y-Xc)'(y-Xc)-(y-Xb)'(y-Xb)=(c-b)'X'X(c-b)\ge0$.

  1. Write $c=b+(c-b)$. Then $y-Xc=(y-Xb)-X(c-b)=e-X(c-b)$.
  2. Expand: $(y-Xc)'(y-Xc)=e'e-2(c-b)'X'e+(c-b)'X'X(c-b)$.
  3. The middle term is zero because $X'e=0$ (normal equations).
  4. Therefore $(y-Xc)'(y-Xc)-e'e=(c-b)'X'X(c-b)=d'd=\sum d_i^2\ge0$ with $d=X(c-b)$.
  5. It is zero only if $X(c-b)=0$, which under A1b means $c=b$. So $b$ is the unique minimiser. No calculus was needed.
A slip in the solution sheet

The last displayed equation of the TA's typed solution drops the term $-(y-Xb)'(y-Xb)$ on the left. The statement above is the correct one. With the running example: $c=(0,1)'$ gives $c-b=(-1.3,0.1)'$ and $(c-b)'X'X(c-b)=5.1$; directly, $SSE(c)=1+1+4+0+1=7=1.9+5.1$ ✓.

?●●practiceSolve a 2×2 system

A regression with constant and one regressor has $n=10$, $\sum x=20$, $\sum x^2=60$, $\sum y=50$, $\sum xy=130$. Find $b$.

Solution

$X'X=\begin{bmatrix}10&20\\20&60\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=200$, $(X'X)^{-1}=\frac1{200}\begin{bmatrix}60&-20\\-20&10\end{bmatrix}$. $b=\frac1{200}\begin{bmatrix}3000-2600\\-1000+1300\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1.5\end{bmatrix}$.

?●●practiceChange of units

You multiply regressor $x_2$ by 100 (decimals to percent). What happens to $b_2$, $\hat y$, $e$ and $R^2$?

Solution

$b_2$ is divided by 100; $\hat y$, $e$ and $R^2$ don't change. The column space of $X$ is the same, so the projection is the same; only the weight on the rescaled column adjusts. In general, replacing $X$ by $XA$ with $A$ invertible gives $A^{-1}b$.

?●practiceThe smallest regressions

(a) Regress $y$ on a constant only. (b) Regress $y$ on one regressor $x$ without a constant.

Solution

(a) $X=\mathbf 1$: $X'X=n$, $X'y=\sum y_i$, so $b=\bar y$. (b) $b=\frac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2}$. Two formulas worth knowing by heart.
