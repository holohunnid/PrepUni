The t-statistic and the t-test

lecture 7, slides 9–10, 17–18
READ FIRST Replace σ² with s² and pay with fatter tails— ⊡ ✕

In practice $\sigma^2$ is unknown. Put $s^2$ in its place. The statistic is no longer normal, because the denominator is now random too. It follows a Student-$t$ with $n-K$ degrees of freedom: like a normal, but with fatter tails to account for the uncertainty about $\sigma^2$.

DEF The t-statistic— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{t=\frac{b_k-q}{\sqrt{s^2(X'X)^{-1}_{k,k}}}=\frac{\text{estimate}-\text{hypothesised value}}{\text{standard error}}\ \overset{H_0}{\sim}\ t_{n-K}}$$

General single restriction ($J=1$): $t=\dfrac{Rb-q}{\sqrt{s^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'}}\sim t_{n-K}$.

Reported by software for $q=0$:

$$t=\frac{b_k}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbb V}[b_k\mid X]}}=\frac{b_k}{\text{se}(b_k)}$$

Reject $H_0:\beta_k=q$ against $\neq$ at 5% if $\lvert t\rvert>t_{n-K,\,0.975}$.

DERIVATION Where the t comes from (slide 17)— ⊡ ✕
$$t=\frac{b_1}{\sqrt{s^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'}}=\frac{\dfrac{b_1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'}}}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s^2}{\sigma^2}}}\ \overset D=\ \frac{N(0,1)}{\sqrt{\dfrac{\chi^2_{n-K}}{n-K}}}\ \overset D=\ t_{n-K}$$
  1. Multiply and divide by $\sigma$: numerator becomes the $z$ of module 20, $N(0,1)$ under $H_0$.
  2. Denominator: $s^2/\sigma^2$ is an average chi-square (module 22, step 1).
  3. They are independent (module 22, step 2: $b$ and $s^2$ independent).
  4. A standard normal over the square root of an independent average chi-square is, by definition, Student-$t$. $\blacksquare$
Notice what disappeared

$\sigma^2$ cancels: the distribution of $t$ depends on nothing unknown. That is why it can be used as a test statistic (it is a pivot).

Slide 18 says "more peaked at zero and fatter tails". Compared with $N(0,1)$ its tails are fatter, and the peak is slightly lower ($t_3$: 0.368 against 0.399); it looks more peaked only when both are scaled to the same variance. As $n-K\to\infty$, $s^2\to\sigma^2$ and $t\to N(0,1)$.

RUNNING EXAMPLE · two t-tests (n − K = 3, critical value 3.182)

$H_0:\beta_2=0$ vs $\neq0$: $t=\dfrac{0.9}{0.2517}=3.576>3.182$ ⇒ reject at 5% (p-value 0.037). At 1% the critical value is 5.841: do not reject.

$H_0:\beta_1=0$: $t=\dfrac{1.3}{0.8347}=1.557<3.182$ ⇒ do not reject.

$H_0:\beta_2=1$: $t=\dfrac{0.9-1}{0.2517}=-0.397$ ⇒ do not reject.

With the (wrong) normal critical value 1.96 the conclusions would be the same here, but a $t$ of 2.5 with 3 d.f. would be wrongly called significant.

ES_TESTING SHEET · test whether the sum of the two slopes is 1

$\hat y=4+0.4x_1+0.9x_2$, $N=29$, $e'e=520$, $(X'X)^{-1}=\frac1{3900}\begin{bmatrix}3900/29&0&0\\0&80&-10\\0&-10&50\end{bmatrix}$.

  1. Hypotheses. $H_0:\beta_2+\beta_3=1$ vs $H_A:\beta_2+\beta_3\neq1$. $R=[0\ 1\ 1]$, $q=1$, $J=1$.
  2. Which statistic? One restriction, $\sigma^2$ unknown ⇒ $t_{n-K}$ with $n-K=29-3=26$.
  3. Numerator. $Rb-q=0.4+0.9-1=0.3$.
  4. $s^2=520/26=20$.
  1. Denominator. $R(X'X)^{-1}R'=\frac{80-10-10+50}{3900}=\frac{110}{3900}=0.02821$; times 20: $0.5641$; root: $0.7511$.
  2. Statistic. $t=0.3/0.7511=\mathbf{0.399}$.
  3. Decision. $t_{26,0.975}=2.056$. $0.399<2.056$ ⇒ do not reject: the data are compatible with the slopes summing to one (p-value 0.69).
TRAP · the covariance

$\mathbb V[b_2+b_3]=\mathbb V[b_2]+\mathbb V[b_3]+2\text{Cov}$. The sum of all four entries of the block is $80-10-10+50=110$. Using $80+50=130$ forgets the covariance.

Using ES_Testing data, test $H_0:\beta_2=\beta_3$ at 5%.

Solution

$R=[0\ 1\ {-1}]$, $m=0.4-0.9=-0.5$. $R(X'X)^{-1}R'=\frac{80+10+10+50}{3900}=\frac{150}{3900}=0.03846$; $\times20=0.7692$; root $0.8771$. $t=-0.57$ ⇒ do not reject. (With a negative covariance, the variance of a difference is larger than that of the sum.)

Replacing $\sigma^2$ by the random $s^2$ fattens the tails, for any $n$.
2The independence of $b$ and $s^2$ is needed
The definition of $t$ requires an independent chi-square in the denominator.
3$n=60$, $K=4$. The d.f. of the $t$-statistic are
$n-K$.
4As $n-K\to\infty$ the $t$ distribution
$s^2\to\sigma^2$.
