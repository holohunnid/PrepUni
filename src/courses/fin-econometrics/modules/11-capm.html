The CAPM: beta of IBM and the market-neutral trade

lecture 2, slides 12–19
READ FIRST From a regression to a trading strategy— ⊡ ✕

The CAPM regression is the course's main application. It is the linear model with excess returns. Its slope $\beta$ is the quantity of market risk, its intercept $\alpha$ the part of the return the market does not explain. If you have a view on $\alpha$, the estimated $\beta$ tells you how much market to short.

DEF The CAPM as a linear regression— ⊡ ✕

Daily simple return: $r_t=\dfrac{P_t-P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}}$. The CAPM postulates

$$r_t-r_t^f=\alpha^{IBM}+\beta^{IBM}(r_t^m-r_t^f)+\varepsilon_t^{IBM},\qquad t=1,\dots,T$$

Take expectations:

$$\mathbb E[r_t-r_t^f]=\alpha^{IBM}+\beta^{IBM}\,\mathbb E[r_t^m-r_t^f]$$

Write it as $y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ with $r_t^{ex}=r_t-r_t^f$ and $r_t^{m,ex}=r_t^m-r_t^f$:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}r_1^{ex}\\\vdots\\r_T^{ex}\end{bmatrix}}_{y\ (T\times1)}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&r_1^{m,ex}\\\vdots&\vdots\\1&r_T^{m,ex}\end{bmatrix}}_{X\ (T\times2)}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{IBM}\\\beta^{IBM}\end{bmatrix}}_{\beta\ (2\times1)}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_1^{IBM}\\\vdots\\\varepsilon_T^{IBM}\end{bmatrix}}_{\varepsilon\ (T\times1)}$$

Then $b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y=\begin{bmatrix}\hat\alpha^{IBM}\\\hat\beta^{IBM}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.05\\0.74\end{bmatrix}$ on the course's daily data.

Key remark of slide 16

$\alpha^{IBM},\beta^{IBM}$ are unobserved true values. $\hat\alpha^{IBM},\hat\beta^{IBM}$ are estimates computed from a sample. Another sample gives other estimates.

READ THE NUMBERS What $\hat\beta=0.74$ and $\hat\alpha=0.05$ mean— ⊡ ✕

$\hat\beta=0.74$: when the market's excess return is 1% higher, IBM's is 0.74% higher on average. IBM is defensive ($\beta\lt1$): it moves less than the market in both directions. $\beta>1$ would be aggressive, $\beta=1$ like the market.

$\hat\alpha=0.05$: average daily return not explained by the market, in the units of the data (percent per day if returns are in percent). Whether it is different from zero is a test, not something you can read off (module 23).

Alpha is what is left: $\hat\alpha=\bar r^{ex}-\hat\beta\,\bar r^{m,ex}$ (module 09's $b_1=\bar y-b_2\bar x$).

Beta is covariance over variance:

$$\hat\beta=\frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}[r^{ex},r^{m,ex}]}{\hat{\mathbb V}[r^{m,ex}]}$$

Portfolio beta is the weighted average: $\beta_p=\sum w_j\beta_j$, because covariance is linear.

STRATEGY Executing a view: the market-neutral portfolio— ⊡ ✕

The view: IBM will do well relative to the market: if the market rises, IBM rises more than its beta suggests; if the market falls, IBM falls less. In the CAPM this is $\alpha^{IBM}>0$.

  1. Why not simply buy IBM? With $r^f=0$: $r_t=\alpha^{IBM}+\beta^{IBM}r_t^m+\varepsilon_t^{IBM}$. If the market falls 10%, you lose about $7.4\%$, even if your view is right.
  2. Hedge the market part. Buy 1 of IBM and sell short $\beta^{IBM}$ of the market (futures, ETFs).
  3. What you hold: $r_t-\beta^{IBM}r_t^m=\alpha^{IBM}+\varepsilon_t^{IBM}$.
  4. Expected return $\alpha^{IBM}>0$; market risk gone. What remains is the idiosyncratic shock $\varepsilon_t^{IBM}$.
  5. You need a good $\hat\beta$. With $\hat\beta\neq\beta$ you keep exposure $(\beta-\hat\beta)r_t^m$.

Dollars

Long 1,000,000 of IBM with $\hat\beta=0.74$: short 740,000 of an S&P 500 future. The trade is not free of risk. It is free of market risk, on average, if beta is right and stable.

INTERACTIVE Hedge it and see— ⊡ ✕

Try this. Press "crash market": the stock falls with it, the hedged portfolio hardly notices. Set the hedge ratio to 0: the hedged line becomes the stock. Set $\alpha=0$: the hedged portfolio drifts nowhere. Raise the idiosyncratic $\sigma$: the hedge is still market-neutral but no longer calm, and $\hat\alpha$ becomes very hard to estimate. That is the motivation for lecture 5.

?●●practiceHedge a portfolio

You hold 600,000 of stock A ($\beta=1.1$) and 400,000 of stock B ($\beta=0.6$). How much market do you short to be neutral? After you short it, the market falls 5% and idiosyncratic returns are zero, $\alpha=0$, $r^f=0$. Your P&L?

Solution

Exposure: $600{,}000\cdot1.1+400{,}000\cdot0.6=900{,}000$. Short 900,000. Stocks lose $45{,}000$, the short gains $45{,}000$: P&L $=0$.

?●●practiceHow wrong can the hedge be?

True $\beta=0.9$, you hedge with $\hat\beta=0.74$. Market return $-10\%$, $\alpha=\varepsilon=0$. What is the return of the "neutral" portfolio?

Solution

$(0.9-0.74)\cdot(-10\%)=-1.6\%$. Residual market exposure is $\beta-\hat\beta$. "Better this estimate be accurate!" says slide 19. How accurate it is, is the subject of lecture 4.
