Expected shortfall and GARCH extensions

GARCH deck, slides 23–26
READ FIRST If it goes wrong, how wrong?— ⊡ ✕

VaR tells you the threshold of the worst 1% of days but not how bad those days are. Expected shortfall (ES, or conditional VaR) is their average. Then: three ways in which GARCH is extended to fit returns better.

DEF Expected shortfall— ⊡ ✕

The major problem with VaR (slide 23's question): it ignores the size of losses beyond it. Two portfolios can have the same VaR and very different disasters.

$$\begin{aligned}-ES_{t,t+1}&=\mathbb E[r_{t+1}\mid r_{t+1}<-VaR_{t,t+1},\mathcal F_t]\\&=\mathbb E[\sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}\mid\sigma_{t+1}z_{t+1}<\sigma_{t+1}z_{0.01}]\\&=\sigma_{t+1}\,\mathbb E[z_{t+1}\mid z_{t+1}<z_{0.01}]\end{aligned}$$
$$\boxed{ES_{t,t+1}=-\sigma_{t+1}\,\mathbb E[z\mid z<z_{0.01}]}$$

$\mathbb E[z\mid z<z_{0.01}]$ is a number that depends only on the distribution of $z$. Estimate it by the average of the $\hat z_t$ below the 1% quantile (empirical counterpart), or from a model.

Normal $z$: $\mathbb E[z\mid z<z_\alpha]=-\dfrac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}\alpha$, e.g. $-\dfrac{0.02665}{0.01}=-2.665$.

Required by regulators (e.g. the Swiss Solvency Test). Holds for any $\sigma_{t+1}\in\mathcal F_t$.

PROOF Where $-\varphi(z_\alpha)/\alpha$ comes from— ⊡ ✕
$$\mathbb E[z\mid z<z_\alpha]=\frac1{P(z<z_\alpha)}\int_{-\infty}^{z_\alpha}x\varphi(x)\,dx=\frac1\alpha\big[-\varphi(x)\big]_{-\infty}^{z_\alpha}=-\frac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}\alpha$$

using $\varphi'(x)=-x\varphi(x)$.

EXAMPLE · same VaR, different ES (why VaR can mislead)

Portfolio A loses 5 or more with probability 1%, and when it does it loses 6 on average. Portfolio B has the same 1% threshold of 5, but loses 40 on average beyond it. VaR: both 5. ES: 6 versus 40.

VaR can punish diversification. Two independent bonds, each loses 100 with probability 0.7%, else 0. Each alone has 1% VaR $=0$. Together $P(\text{loss}\ge100)=1-0.993^2=1.4\%$, so the portfolio's 1% VaR is $100>0+0$. ES of each is $0.7\%\cdot100/1\%=70$, and the portfolio's ES is about 100.5, less than 140.

EXTENSION 1 Non-normal innovations— ⊡ ✕

Keep the GARCH variance, change the distribution of $z_t$: e.g. Student-$t$ (Bollerslev 1987), semiparametric (Engle and Gonzalez-Rivera 1991). Motivated by the diagnostics: $\hat z_t$ has values near $-10$. Effect: larger 1% VaR and much larger ES.

EXTENSION 2 Asymmetric GARCH (GJR, 1993): the leverage effect— ⊡ ✕
$$\sigma_t^2=\beta_0+\beta_1\sigma_{t-1}^2+\beta_2r_{t-1}^2+\beta_3I_{t-1}r_{t-1}^2$$

$I_{t-1}=1$ if $r_{t-1}\lt0$, and 0 otherwise.

yesterdayeffect of $r_{t-1}^2$
$r_{t-1}\ge0$$\beta_2$
$r_{t-1}\lt0$$\beta_2+\beta_3$

With $\beta_3>0$ a fall raises volatility more than an equal rise: matches slopes −2.25 vs 0.24 of slide 10. Testing $\beta_3=0$ tests for the leverage effect.

Unconditional variance for symmetric $z$ ($\mathbb E[I_{t-1}r_{t-1}^2]=\frac12\bar\sigma^2$):

$$\bar\sigma^2=\frac{\beta_0}{1-\beta_1-\beta_2-\beta_3/2}$$

Example: $\beta=(0.02,0.90,0.02,0.12)$, $\sigma_{t-1}^2=1$. After $r=-2$: $0.02+0.9+0.08+0.48=1.48$. After $r=+2$: $0.02+0.9+0.08=1.00$. Same size, different sign, 48% higher variance.

INTERACTIVE News impact curve— ⊡ ✕

Symmetric GARCH is a parabola centred at 0. With $\beta_3>0$ the left branch is steeper. The kink at zero is smooth for $\sigma^2$ (the slope is 0 from both sides), but the curvature jumps.

EXTENSION 3 Long-run and short-run components (Christoffersen et al. 2008)— ⊡ ✕
$$\sigma_t^2=q_t+\tilde\beta_1(\sigma_{t-1}^2-q_{t-1})+\tilde\beta_2\,\eta_{1,t-1}$$ $$q_t=\gamma_0+\gamma_1q_{t-1}+\gamma_2\,\eta_{2,t-1}$$

$\eta_{1},\eta_2$ are zero-mean innovations.

In GARCH(1,1) volatility reverts to a constant $\bar\sigma^2$. Here it reverts to a moving level $q_t$ that itself follows an AR(1)-type process: $q_t$ is the slow, long-run component; $\sigma_t^2-q_t$ the fast one. It captures the fact that some decades are calm and others are not.

Today: GARCH is widely used to estimate and forecast conditional variances, usually with non-normal innovations, for risk management and derivative pricing (Barone-Adesi, Engle and Mancini 2008).

?●●practiceEmpirical ES

500 innovations; the five smallest are $-2.4,-2.6,-2.9,-3.3,-4.8$, and we take the 1% tail to be these five. Estimate $\mathbb E[z\mid z<z_{0.01}]$ and the ES for $\sigma_{t+1}=2\%$.

Solution

Average $=-16/5=-3.2$. ES $=3.2\cdot2\%=6.4\%$.

?●●practiceGJR by hand

$\sigma_t^2=0.05+0.88\sigma_{t-1}^2+0.03r_{t-1}^2+0.10I_{t-1}r_{t-1}^2$. Long-run variance? $\sigma_{t-1}^2=1$: $\sigma_t^2$ after $-3$ and after $+3$? 1% normal VaR in both cases?

Solution

$\bar\sigma^2=0.05/(1-0.88-0.03-0.05)=0.05/0.04=1.25$. After $-3$: $0.05+0.88+0.13\cdot9=2.10$; after $+3$: $0.05+0.88+0.27=1.20$. VaR: $2.326\sqrt{2.10}=3.37\%$ and $2.326\sqrt{1.20}=2.55\%$.
