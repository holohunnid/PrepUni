χ², independence, and the distribution of s²

lecture 7, slides 11–16
READ FIRST Two ingredients for the t distribution— ⊡ ✕

To get the distribution of $t=b_1/\sqrt{s^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'}$ we need (1) the distribution of $s^2$, which is an "average chi-square", and (2) the fact that $b$ and $s^2$ are independent. Both follow from properties of $M$.

DEF The chi-square in one panel— ⊡ ✕

$Z\sim N(0,1)\Rightarrow Z^2\sim\chi^2_1$. If $Z_1,\dots,Z_k$ are i.i.d. $N(0,1)$, then $Z'Z=\sum Z_i^2\sim\chi^2_k$.

Check (slide 12): $\mathbb E[Z'Z]=k$ and $\mathbb V[Z'Z]=2k$.

  1. $\mathbb E[Z_i^2]=\mathbb V[Z_i]+0=1$, so $\mathbb E[Z'Z]=k$.
  2. $\mathbb V[Z_i^2]=\mathbb E[Z_i^4]-1=3-1=2$, and independence gives $\mathbb V[Z'Z]=2k$.

"Average chi-square" $\chi^2_k/k$: mean 1, variance $2/k\to0$.

STEP 1 $s^2/\sigma^2$ is an average chi-square— ⊡ ✕
  1. $\dfrac{e'e}{\sigma^2}=\dfrac{\varepsilon'M\varepsilon}{\sigma^2}=\Big(\dfrac\varepsilon\sigma\Big)'M\Big(\dfrac\varepsilon\sigma\Big)$ with $\varepsilon/\sigma\sim N(0,I_n)$.
  2. Without $M$: $(\varepsilon/\sigma)'(\varepsilon/\sigma)\sim\chi^2_n$.
  3. With $M$, symmetric idempotent of rank $n-K$ (S3): $\dfrac{e'e}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-K}$.
  4. Therefore $$\boxed{\frac{s^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{e'e}{\sigma^2(n-K)}\sim\frac{\chi^2_{n-K}}{n-K}}$$

Intuition. $M$ "contracts" the space: $\varepsilon/\sigma$ lives in $n$ dimensions, $M(\varepsilon/\sigma)$ in $n-K$. So only $n-K$ independent squared normals remain.

Sanity checks. Mean: $\mathbb E[s^2]/\sigma^2=\frac{n-K}{n-K}=1$ ✓ unbiased. Variance: $\mathbb V[s^2\mid X]=\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n-K}\to0$: $s^2$ concentrates on $\sigma^2$.

STEP 2 $b$ and $s^2$ are independent— ⊡ ✕
  1. $\text{Cov}[b,e\mid X]=\mathbb E[(b-\beta)(M\varepsilon-0)'\mid X]$
  2. $=\mathbb E[(X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon\varepsilon'M'\mid X]$ using $b-\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'\varepsilon$
  3. $=(X'X)^{-1}X'(\sigma^2I_n)M'=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}X'M=0$ because $X'M=0$
  4. $b$ and $e$ are jointly normal (both linear in $\varepsilon$), so zero covariance ⇒ independent
  5. $s^2=e'e/(n-K)$ is a function of $e$ ⇒ $b$ and $s^2$ independent $\blacksquare$

Dimension check: $\text{Cov}[b,e\mid X]$ is $K\times n$.

Geometry: $b$ depends on the part of $\varepsilon$ on the floor, $e$ on the part perpendicular to it. For a spherical normal, perpendicular parts are independent.

Needed facts

Independence ⇒ zero covariance, always. Zero covariance ⇒ independence, only for (jointly) normal variables. If $Y_1,Y_2$ are independent so are $h(Y_1),g(Y_2)$ for any functions.

INTERACTIVE Zero covariance does not imply independence— ⊡ ✕

Knowing $Y_1$ tells you $Y_2$ almost exactly in the last three, yet the covariance is zero: covariance sees only linear dependence. Proof for $Y_2=Y_1^2$ with $Y_1\sim N(0,1)$: $\text{Cov}=\mathbb E[Y_1^3]-\mathbb E[Y_1]\mathbb E[Y_1^2]=0-0=0$.

?●●practiceDistribution of e′e

$n=25$, $K=5$, $\sigma^2=4$. What are the distribution, mean and variance of $e'e$? Of $s^2$?

Solution

$e'e/4\sim\chi^2_{20}$, so $e'e\sim4\chi^2_{20}$: mean 80, variance $16\cdot40=640$. $s^2=e'e/20$: mean 4, variance $640/400=1.6=2\sigma^4/(n-K)=32/20$ ✓.

?●●practiceIs it chi-square?

$Z\sim N(0,I_3)$. Which are chi-square, with how many d.f.? (i) $Z_1^2+Z_2^2$; (ii) $(Z_1+Z_2)^2$; (iii) $(Z_1+Z_2)^2/2$; (iv) $\sum(Z_i-\bar Z)^2$.

Solution

(i) $\chi^2_2$. (ii) No: $Z_1+Z_2\sim N(0,2)$, so it is $2\chi^2_1$. (iii) $\chi^2_1$. (iv) $Z'MZ$ with $M=I-\frac13\mathbf1\mathbf1'$, rank 2: $\chi^2_2$.
