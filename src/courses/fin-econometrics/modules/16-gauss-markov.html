Gauss–Markov: OLS is BLUE

lecture 4, slides 6–9 · W8 questions 2 and 3
READ FIRST Best in its class— ⊡ ✕

There are many estimators of $\beta$. Among those that are linear in $y$ and unbiased, OLS has the smallest variance. That is the Gauss–Markov theorem, also stated as "OLS is BLUE": Best Linear Unbiased Estimator. It uses A1b, A2a, A2b and not normality.

DEF What "best" means when variances are matrices— ⊡ ✕

For a scalar parameter, best means $\mathbb V[\tilde b\mid X]\ge\mathbb V[b\mid X]$. For $K$ parameters both are $K\times K$ matrices, so we say

$$\mathbb V[\tilde b\mid X]-\mathbb V[b\mid X]=\text{non-negative definite}.$$

Equivalent meaning: for every linear combination $c'\beta$,

$$\mathbb V[c'\tilde b\mid X]-\mathbb V[c'b\mid X]=c'\big(\mathbb V[\tilde b\mid X]-\mathbb V[b\mid X]\big)c\ge0 .$$

So OLS is best for each coefficient, for sums, for differences, for predictions $x_0'\beta$.

PROOF The proof in five lines (learn it)— ⊡ ✕
  1. A general rival. Any linear estimator is $\tilde b=\tilde Ay$. Then $\mathbb E[\tilde b\mid X]=\tilde AX\beta$ and $\mathbb V[\tilde b\mid X]=\sigma^2\tilde A\tilde A'$.
  2. Unbiased for every $\beta$ $\Leftrightarrow\tilde AX=I_K$.
  3. Compare with OLS: write $\tilde A=A+D$, with $A=(X'X)^{-1}X'$. Then $I_K=\tilde AX=AX+DX=I_K+DX\Rightarrow\boxed{DX=0}$.
  4. Cross terms vanish: $AD'=(X'X)^{-1}X'D'=(X'X)^{-1}(DX)'=0$, and $DA'=(AD')'=0$.
  5. Variance: $$\mathbb V[\tilde b\mid X]=\sigma^2(A+D)(A+D)'=\underbrace{\sigma^2AA'}_{\mathbb V[b\mid X]}+\sigma^2DD'$$ and $DD'$ is non-negative definite: $x'DD'x=z'z=\sum z_j^2\ge0$ with $z=D'x$. $\blacksquare$
How to remember it

Any rival = OLS + something ($D$). Unbiasedness forces that something to be orthogonal to $X$ ($DX=0$). Orthogonal pieces add variance but no information. It is Pythagoras again.

Where each assumption is used

A1b: $A$ exists. A2a: $\mathbb E[y\mid X]=X\beta$. A2b: $\mathbb V[y\mid X]=\sigma^2I$, so $\sigma^2$ factors out of $\tilde A\tilde A'$. Normality: nowhere.

What Gauss–Markov does not say

It does not say OLS beats biased or non-linear estimators. A biased estimator with a tiny variance can have a smaller MSE. And it fails if A2b fails.

INTERACTIVE OLS against unbiased rivals— ⊡ ✕

Try this. All three rivals are linear in $y$ and unbiased: both histograms are centred at 1, but the amber one is always wider. Then choose "always 0.9": variance zero! It beats OLS on variance but it is biased, so Gauss–Markov does not apply to it. With $\sigma$ large it even has a lower mean squared error.

EXAMPLE · the endpoints estimator, worked out

$\tilde b_2=\dfrac{y_n-y_1}{x_n-x_1}$. Linear? Yes, weights $-\frac1{x_n-x_1}$ on $y_1$ and $+\frac1{x_n-x_1}$ on $y_n$, 0 elsewhere. Unbiased? $\mathbb E[\tilde b_2\mid X]=\frac{(\beta_1+\beta_2x_n)-(\beta_1+\beta_2x_1)}{x_n-x_1}=\beta_2$ ✓. Variance: $\frac{2\sigma^2}{(x_n-x_1)^2}$. With $x=1,\dots,5$: $2\sigma^2/16=0.125\sigma^2$, against OLS $\sigma^2/10=0.1\sigma^2$. OLS is better by 20%, because it also uses the middle points.

W8 PART 1, QUESTIONS 2 and 3 · model answers

Q2. What is the BLUE of $\beta$? Explain without proof.

The OLS estimator $\hat\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$. It is linear in $y$ ($\hat\beta=Ay$), unbiased ($\mathbb E[\hat\beta]=\beta$, because $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$), and best: by the Gauss–Markov theorem, since the errors are i.i.d. with mean zero and constant variance $\sigma^2$, independent of the regressors, and $X$ has full column rank, its covariance matrix is the smallest among all linear unbiased estimators: for any other one $\tilde b$, $\mathbb V[\tilde b\mid X]-\mathbb V[\hat\beta\mid X]$ is non-negative definite.

Q3. If $\varepsilon_t$ were normal rather than unknown, would the answer change?

NO. Gauss–Markov needs only $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, $\mathbb V[\varepsilon\mid X]=\sigma^2I$ and full rank; it holds for any distribution with those moments, including the normal. Normality would add the exact distribution of $\hat\beta$, which is needed for hypothesis testing, not for BLUE.

One detail worth a sentence

Independence "for each $t$" gives $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_t\mid I_t,r_t]=0$. Together with i.i.d. errors independent of all regressors it gives $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, which is what the theorem uses.

?●●practiceIs it BLUE?

$y_i=\beta x_i+\varepsilon_i$ (no constant). Rival: $\tilde b=\bar y/\bar x$. Linear? Unbiased? Compare variances.

Solution

Linear: $\tilde b=\sum y_i/\sum x_i$ ✓. Unbiased: $\sum\beta x_i/\sum x_i=\beta$ ✓. Variance: $\frac{n\sigma^2}{(\sum x_i)^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\bar x^2}$, against OLS $\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum x_i^2}$. Since $\sum x_i^2=n\bar x^2+\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\ge n\bar x^2$, OLS has the smaller variance ✓.

?●●practiceThe mean is BLUE

In $y_i=\mu+\varepsilon_i$, show that among $\sum w_iy_i$ with $\sum w_i=1$, equal weights are best.

Solution

$\mathbb V=\sigma^2\sum w_i^2$. Write $w_i=\frac1n+d_i$, $\sum d_i=0$: $\sum w_i^2=\frac1n+\sum d_i^2\ge\frac1n$. Here $d$ plays the role of $D$ and $\sum d_i=0$ is $DX=0$.
