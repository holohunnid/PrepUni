The residual maker and s²

lecture 4, slides 10–17
READ FIRST Why divide by n − K— ⊡ ✕

$\mathbb V[b\mid X]=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$ needs $\sigma^2$, which is unknown. We estimate it from the residuals. The obvious estimator $\frac1n\sum e_i^2$ is almost right but too small on average, because OLS chose $b$ precisely to make the residuals small. The correction is $n-K$ in place of $n$, and the matrix $M$ shows why.

INTUITION Three steps to the estimator— ⊡ ✕
  1. If the errors were observed, use their sample variance: $\frac1n\sum\varepsilon_i^2$ (remember $\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i]=0$). Its mean is exactly $\sigma^2$.
  2. They are not. Replace $\varepsilon_i$ with $e_i=y_i-x_i'b$: $\frac1n\sum e_i^2$. (With a constant, residuals have mean zero: $0=e'\mathbf 1=\sum e_i$.)
  3. But $\sum e_i^2\le\sum\varepsilon_i^2$ in every sample, since $b$ minimises the sum of squares and $\beta$ does not. So $\frac1n\sum e_i^2$ is too small on average. By how much? Exactly the factor $\frac{n-K}n$.
DEF The residual maker matrix— ⊡ ✕
$$e=y-X(X'X)^{-1}X'y=My,\qquad\boxed{M=I_n-X(X'X)^{-1}X'}\ (n\times n)$$
  1. Symmetric: $M'=M$ (transpose each piece).
  2. $MX=0$: $X-X(X'X)^{-1}X'X=X-X=0$. Also $X'M=0$.
  3. Idempotent: $MM=M(I-P)=M-MX(X'X)^{-1}X'=M$.
  4. $\text{rank}(M)=\text{trace}(M)=n-K$ (A4 and the cyclic trace):
$$\text{tr}(M)=\text{tr}(I_n)-\text{tr}\big(X(X'X)^{-1}X'\big)=n-\text{tr}\big(X'X(X'X)^{-1}\big)=n-K$$

Consequences

$$e=My=M(X\beta+\varepsilon)=M\varepsilon$$ $$e'e=\varepsilon'M'M\varepsilon=\varepsilon'MM\varepsilon=\varepsilon'M\varepsilon$$

an idempotent quadratic form (S3 in the Memorandum).

RUNNING EXAMPLE · M in full
$$M=\begin{bmatrix}0.4&-0.4&-0.2&0&0.2\\-0.4&0.7&-0.2&-0.1&0\\-0.2&-0.2&0.8&-0.2&-0.2\\0&-0.1&-0.2&0.7&-0.4\\0.2&0&-0.2&-0.4&0.4\end{bmatrix}$$

Trace $=0.4+0.7+0.8+0.7+0.4=3=5-2$ ✓. Each row sums to 0 ($M\mathbf1=0$) ✓. Row 1 times $y$: $0.8-1.2-1.0+0+1.2=-0.2=e_1$ ✓.

PROOF $\mathbb E[e'e\mid X]=\sigma^2(n-K)$— ⊡ ✕
  1. $\mathbb E[e'e\mid X]=\mathbb E[\varepsilon'M\varepsilon\mid X]$
  2. $=\mathbb E[\text{tr}(\varepsilon'M\varepsilon)\mid X]$ — a scalar equals its trace (the slide's "why?")
  3. $=\mathbb E[\text{tr}(\varepsilon\varepsilon'M)\mid X]$ — cyclic property
  4. $=\text{tr}\big(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\varepsilon'\mid X]M\big)$ — trace is linear, $M$ is a function of $X$
  5. $=\text{tr}(\sigma^2I_nM)=\sigma^2\text{tr}(M)=\sigma^2(n-K)$ $\blacksquare$

Dimension of $e'e$: $1\times1$. By the tower property $\mathbb E[e'e]=\sigma^2(n-K)$. So the unbiased estimator is

$$\boxed{\;s^2=\frac{e'e}{n-K},\qquad\mathbb E[s^2]=\sigma^2\;}$$

Degrees of freedom. $n$ data points, but $K$ of them are "used up" estimating $\beta$ before $e$ can be computed. The residual vector lives in an $(n-K)$-dimensional space: once $n-K$ residuals are known, $X'e=0$ determines the other $K$.

INTERACTIVE The bias of e′e/n— ⊡ ✕

Try this. With $n=10$, $K=4$ the naive estimator is 40% too small on average. Let $K$ approach $n$: it goes to 0 (perfect fit by overfitting). With $n$ much larger than $K$ the two are the same. Note that $s^2$ is right on average but it is itself random, quite widely for small $n-K$. That randomness is why the $t$ distribution replaces the normal in lecture 7.

RECIPE Everything you need after a regression (slide 17)— ⊡ ✕
  1. $b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$
  2. $e=y-Xb$
  3. $s^2=e'e/(n-K)$
  4. $\hat{\mathbb V}[b\mid X]=s^2(X'X)^{-1}$, unbiased since $\mathbb E[s^2(X'X)^{-1}\mid X]=\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$
  5. Standard error of $b_k$: $\sqrt{s^2(X'X)^{-1}_{k,k}}$. Covariance of $b_i,b_j$: $s^2(X'X)^{-1}_{i,j}$.
TRAP · order of operations

First compute $X'X$, then invert the whole matrix, then read the element. $(X'X)^{-1}_{k,k}$ is not $1/(X'X)_{k,k}$. In the running example $(X'X)_{2,2}=55$ but $(X'X)^{-1}_{2,2}=0.1$, not $1/55=0.018$.

RUNNING EXAMPLE · the full output

$e'e=1.90$, $n-K=3$, $s^2=0.6333$.

$$\hat{\mathbb V}[b\mid X]=0.6333\begin{bmatrix}1.1&-0.3\\-0.3&0.1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.6967&-0.19\\-0.19&0.0633\end{bmatrix}$$
estimatestd. error

?●practiceStandard errors

$n=29$, $K=3$, $e'e=520$ and $(X'X)^{-1}=\frac1{3900}\begin{bmatrix}3900/29&0&0\\0&80&-10\\0&-10&50\end{bmatrix}$. Find $s^2$ and the standard error of each slope.

Solution

$s^2=520/26=20$. $\text{se}(b_2)=\sqrt{20\cdot80/3900}=\sqrt{0.4103}=0.6405$. $\text{se}(b_3)=\sqrt{20\cdot50/3900}=\sqrt{0.2564}=0.5064$. (Data of the ES_Testing sheet, continued in module 25.)

?●●practiceM for the simplest model

$X=\mathbf1$ ($K=1$). Write $M$, $My$ and $s^2$.

Solution

$M=I-\frac1n\mathbf1\mathbf1'$; $My=y-\bar y$ (de-meaning); $s^2=\frac{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}{n-1}$: the sample variance with its familiar $n-1$. So $n-1$ is a special case of $n-K$.

?●●●practiceCov of two residuals

Show $\mathbb V[e\mid X]=\sigma^2M$. In the running example, which residual has the largest variance, and why?

Solution

$e=M\varepsilon$, so $\mathbb V[e\mid X]=M\sigma^2IM'=\sigma^2MM=\sigma^2M$. Diagonal: $0.4,0.7,0.8,0.7,0.4$ (times $\sigma^2$). The middle observation has the largest residual variance because it has no leverage on the slope; the extremes pull the line towards themselves, so their residuals are small.
