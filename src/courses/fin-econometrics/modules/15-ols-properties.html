b is random: linear, unbiased, and its variance

lecture 4, slides 2–5
READ FIRST One sample gives one b. What would the others give?— ⊡ ✕

$b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$ is a number vector after the sample, but before it is a random vector, because $\varepsilon$ is. Lecture 4 studies it as a random vector: its mean (property 2), its variance (property 3), and how it compares with rivals (property 4). Every property is a two-line proof with $A=(X'X)^{-1}X'$.

PROPERTY 1 Linear in y— ⊡ ✕
$$b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y=Ay,\qquad A=(X'X)^{-1}X'\ (K\times n)$$

Two facts about $A$, used in everything below:

$$AX=(X'X)^{-1}X'X=I_K$$ $$AA'=(X'X)^{-1}X'X(X'X)^{-1}=(X'X)^{-1}$$

Implication. If $y$ is (multivariate) normal, $b$ is normal, because a linear map of a normal is normal. Compact form of the dependence on the errors:

$$\boxed{b=A(X\beta+\varepsilon)=\beta+A\varepsilon}$$

Estimator = truth + a weighted sum of the errors. Memorise this line: it gives properties 2 and 3 at once.

PROPERTY 2 Unbiased— ⊡ ✕
  1. $\mathbb E[b\mid X]=\mathbb E[Ay\mid X]=A\,\mathbb E[y\mid X]$ ($A$ depends only on $X$)
  2. $=AX\beta$ (by A2a, $\mathbb E[y\mid X]=X\beta$)
  3. $=\beta$ (because $AX=I_K$)
  4. $\mathbb E[b]=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[b\mid X]]=\mathbb E[\beta]=\beta$ (tower property) $\blacksquare$

Uses: A1b and A2a. Does not use: A2b or normality.

Meaning. Over many samples, the realizations of $b$ are centred at $\beta$. It is a statement about the method, not about your one estimate: $\hat\beta=0.74$ is not "unbiased" or "biased", it is just a number that may be off.

PROPERTY 3 Variance— ⊡ ✕
  1. $\mathbb V[y\mid X]=\mathbb V[X\beta+\varepsilon\mid X]=\mathbb V[\varepsilon\mid X]=\sigma^2I_n$ ($X\beta$ is constant given $X$)
  2. $\mathbb V[b\mid X]=\mathbb V[Ay\mid X]=A\,\mathbb V[y\mid X]\,A'$
  3. $=\sigma^2AA'$ (by A2b)
  4. $=\boxed{\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}}$ $\blacksquare$

A $K\times K$ matrix: variances on the diagonal, covariances off it. It depends on the unknown $\sigma^2$, so it must be estimated (module 17).

Univariate slope: $\mathbb V[b_2\mid X]=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\,\hat{\mathbb V}[x]}$

Univariate intercept: $\mathbb V[b_1\mid X]=\sigma^2\Big(\frac1n+\frac{\bar x^2}{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}\Big)$

What makes the slope precise

Less noise ($\sigma^2\downarrow$), more data ($n\uparrow$), more variation in $x$ ($\hat{\mathbb V}[x]\uparrow$). And, with several regressors, less correlation between them.

INTERACTIVE The sampling distribution of b₂— ⊡ ✕

Try this. One sample at a time, then a thousand: the mean settles at 1 (unbiased) and the standard deviation matches the formula. Change the errors to exponential or $t_3$: still centred, still the right variance, because properties 2 and 3 don't use normality. Only the shape is no longer exactly normal for small $n$. Quadruple $n$: the spread halves.

W8 PART 1, QUESTION 4 · compute E[e]

$e=y-X\hat\beta$ with $\hat\beta=b$ the OLS estimator.

  1. $\mathbb E[e\mid X]=\mathbb E[y\mid X]-X\,\mathbb E[b\mid X]=X\beta-X\beta=0$ ($T\times1$ vector of zeros).
  2. Tower property: $\mathbb E[e]=\mathbb E[\mathbb E[e\mid X]]=0$.
  3. Alternative: $e=M\varepsilon$, so $\mathbb E[e\mid X]=M\,\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$.

Note the two different statements: $\mathbb E[e_i]=0$ for each $i$ is a property across samples. $\sum e_i=0$ is true within each sample when there is a constant.

?●●practiceVariance of a fitted value

Find $\mathbb E[\hat y\mid X]$ and $\mathbb V[\hat y\mid X]$.

Solution

$\hat y=Xb$: mean $X\beta$; variance $X\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}X'=\sigma^2P$. Similarly $\mathbb V[e\mid X]=\sigma^2MM'=\sigma^2M$. Residuals are not homoskedastic or uncorrelated even though errors are.

?●●practiceEstimate with half the sample

You use only the first half of the data. Is the estimator unbiased? What happens to its variance (if $x$ has the same spread in both halves)?

Solution

Still unbiased. $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$ roughly halves, so the variance doubles and the standard deviation rises by $\sqrt2$. Unbiased is not enough: we want small variance.

?●●●practiceDesign the experiment

You may choose ten values of $x$ in $[0,1]$ to estimate the slope as precisely as possible, in a world where the model is surely linear. Which values? Why might you not do it in practice?

Solution

Maximise $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$: five at 0 and five at 1. In practice you could not check linearity with only two distinct values of $x$.

?●●practiceRead the running example's matrix

With $\sigma^2=1$ known, write $\mathbb V[b\mid X]$ for the running example. What is $\text{Corr}(b_1,b_2)$? Explain the sign.

Solution

$\begin{bmatrix}1.1&-0.3\\-0.3&0.1\end{bmatrix}$. Corr $=-0.3/\sqrt{0.11}=-0.905$. $\bar x>0$: the line passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$, so a steeper slope forces a lower intercept.
