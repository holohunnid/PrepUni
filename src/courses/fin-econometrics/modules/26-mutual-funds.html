Mutual funds: skill or luck?

lecture 8, slides 8–13
READ FIRST 1000 tests, some will reject by pure chance— ⊡ ✕

A fund of funds tests the alpha of 1000 mutual funds and wants to buy the ones with the best t-statistics. The course's punch line: the smart manager buys none of them. The reason is the meaning of the size of a test.

SET-UP One regression per fund— ⊡ ✕

For each fund $i=1,\dots,1000$, daily data 2011–2012 ($T\approx500$):

$$r^{ex}_{i,t}=\alpha_i+\beta_ir^{ex}_{m,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}$$

Test $H_0:\alpha_i=0$ vs $H_A:\alpha_i>0$. Idea: invest if $\hat\alpha_i$ is "large and positive", i.e. $H_0$ is rejected. The $t$-statistics follow $t_{498}$ if $\alpha_i=0$; the 0.99 quantile is 2.33.

Why risk-adjust? A fund can outperform just by having $\beta>1$ in a rising market. On slide 9, MF1 doubles while MF2 and MF3 lag the S&P 500, but performance charts mix alpha, beta and luck.

Results: 7 funds are "outstanding" ($t>$ 0.99 quantile); 55 are "very bad" ($t<$ 0.05 quantile).

ANSWER Why would smart managers invest in none of them?— ⊡ ✕
  1. What the size means. At 1% size, the test rejects a true $H_0$ with probability 1%.
  2. So if all 1000 alphas were exactly zero, we would expect $1000\cdot1\%=\mathbf{10}$ funds above the 0.99 quantile, by luck alone.
  3. We found 7, even fewer than luck predicts. The 7 "outstanding" funds are what Type I errors look like. No evidence of skill.
  4. The left tail says the same: $1000\cdot5\%=50$ expected below the 0.05 quantile, 55 found. Nothing unusual either.
  5. The whole histogram of 1000 $t$-statistics lies on the $t_{498}$ density: exactly the picture of $\alpha_i=0$ for all $i$.
How unusual are 7 and 55?

If the funds were independent, the count is Binomial: mean 10, sd $\sqrt{1000\cdot0.01\cdot0.99}=3.1$; and mean 50, sd 6.9. So 7 and 55 are both within one standard deviation of what pure luck produces. (Funds share risks, so the true uncertainty is larger still.)

INTERACTIVE Create 1000 funds and look for skill— ⊡ ✕

Try this. With 0% skill the histogram matches the density and about 10 funds still pass. Press follow the winners: their t-statistics in the next two years are again centred at zero, because they were lucky. Now set 10% skilled with shift 3: the right tail gets heavy and most winners are genuine. With Bonferroni, false discoveries almost disappear, at the price of missing many skilled funds (power!).

LESSON The multiple-testing problem— ⊡ ✕

If you run $N$ independent tests of true nulls at size $\alpha$:

$$\mathbb E[\#\text{false rejections}]=N\alpha,\qquad P(\text{at least one})=1-(1-\alpha)^N$$

The same trap: testing 100 trading rules and publishing the best; trying 50 regressors and reporting the significant ones ("data snooping"). A single test's size is valid for one test fixed in advance.

Fixes (beyond the slides): compare the number of rejections with $N\alpha$; use a stricter size, e.g. Bonferroni $\alpha/N$; check out of sample.

?●●practiceIs there skill here?

Out of 2000 funds tested at 1% one-sided, 85 have $t>2.33$. What do you conclude? Roughly how many of them are likely genuine?

Solution

Expected by luck: 20, sd $\sqrt{19.8}=4.4$. 85 is far above: some skill exists. Roughly $85-20=65$ genuine, but you can't tell which: about a quarter of the 85 are lucky.

?●●practiceExplain to an investor

"This fund has a t-stat of 2.5, so there is only a 0.6% chance it is luck." Correct the statement.

Solution

0.6% is $P(t>2.5\mid\alpha=0)$ for one fund chosen in advance. This one was picked because it was the best of 1000: the maximum of 1000 $t$-statistics is above 2.5 almost surely even if all alphas are zero. And $P(\text{data}\mid\text{luck})$ is not $P(\text{luck}\mid\text{data})$.
