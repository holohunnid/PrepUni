ARCH and GARCH

GARCH deck, slides 11–13
READ FIRST Give the variance a memory— ⊡ ✕

ARCH/GARCH models keep the mean of returns at zero and make the conditional variance depend on the past. In ARCH it depends on yesterday's squared return. In GARCH it also depends on yesterday's variance, which makes it smooth and persistent with only three parameters. Engle (1982), Bollerslev (1986).

DEF The two models— ⊡ ✕

ARCH(1) ("for teaching purposes")

$$r_t=\sigma_tz_t,\quad z_t\ \text{i.i.d. }D(0,1)$$ $$\boxed{\sigma_t^2=\alpha_0+\alpha_1r_{t-1}^2}$$

$\sigma_t^2=\mathbb V[r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]$ is the conditional variance. Positive if $\alpha_0>0,\alpha_1\ge0$; stationary if $\alpha_1<1$.

GARCH(1,1) ("largely used in practice")

$$r_t=\sigma_tz_t,\quad z_t\ \text{i.i.d. }D(0,1)$$ $$\boxed{\sigma_t^2=\beta_0+\beta_1\sigma_{t-1}^2+\beta_2r_{t-1}^2}$$

Positive if $\beta_0>0,\beta_1,\beta_2\ge0$; stationary if $\beta_1+\beta_2<1$. Typically $\beta_1\approx0.9$.

TRAP · the order of the betas

In this deck $\beta_1$ multiplies the lagged variance and $\beta_2$ the lagged squared return. Textbooks and software usually write $\sigma_t^2=\omega+\alpha r_{t-1}^2+\beta\sigma_{t-1}^2$: $\omega=\beta_0$, $\alpha=\beta_2$, $\beta=\beta_1$. And these betas have nothing to do with regression betas.

KEY Four properties to be able to derive— ⊡ ✕

1. $\mathbb E[r_t]=0$. $\mathbb E[r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=\sigma_t\mathbb E[z_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=0$ ($\sigma_t$ is known at $t-1$, $z_t$ independent of the past). Then the tower property.

2. $\mathbb V[r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]=\sigma_t^2$. $\sigma_t^2\,\mathbb E[z_t^2]=\sigma_t^2\cdot1$.

3. No autocorrelation. $\mathbb E[r_tr_{t-h}]=\mathbb E\big[r_{t-h}\,\mathbb E[r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]\big]=0$.

4. Unconditional variance. Let $\bar\sigma^2=\mathbb E[\sigma_t^2]$. Since $\mathbb E[r_{t-1}^2]=\mathbb E[\sigma_{t-1}^2]=\bar\sigma^2$:

$$\bar\sigma^2=\beta_0+\beta_1\bar\sigma^2+\beta_2\bar\sigma^2\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\bar\sigma^2=\frac{\beta_0}{1-\beta_1-\beta_2}}$$

ARCH(1): $\dfrac{\alpha_0}{1-\alpha_1}$. Same trick as the AR(1) mean.

$\beta_1+\beta_2$ is the persistence of variance, the analogue of $\phi_1$.

"$\sigma_t$ is known at time $t-1$"

$\mathcal F_{t-1}$ is the information set at $t-1$: all returns up to then. $\sigma_t^2$ is a function of $r_{t-1},\sigma_{t-1}$, so at $t-1$ it is a number. At time $t-1$ the only thing unknown about $r_t$ is $z_t$. At time $t$, $\sigma_{t+1}$ is already known: that is what makes VaR easy (module 34).

INTERACTIVE Simulate GARCH(1,1)— ⊡ ✕

Try this. i.i.d.: flat volatility. ARCH(1) with 0.5: volatility spikes for one day and forgets. Typical GARCH: spikes decay slowly, because $\beta_1\approx0.9$ carries the variance forward. Add the crash: volatility jumps the next day and takes weeks to return. The half-life is $\ln0.5/\ln(\beta_1+\beta_2)$. With $\beta_1+\beta_2\to1$ it hardly ever returns.

LINK GARCH is a smoothed average of past squared returns— ⊡ ✕

Substitute backwards, as for the Wold form:

$$\sigma_t^2=\frac{\beta_0}{1-\beta_1}+\beta_2\sum_{j=0}^\infty\beta_1^j\,r_{t-1-j}^2$$

So GARCH(1,1) is an ARCH($\infty$) with geometrically declining weights: three parameters instead of many. ARCH(1) uses only $r_{t-1}^2$.

Forecast of variance. As for an AR(1), with persistence $\beta_1+\beta_2$:

$$\mathbb E[\sigma_{t+h}^2\mid\mathcal F_t]=\bar\sigma^2+(\beta_1+\beta_2)^{h-1}\big(\sigma_{t+1}^2-\bar\sigma^2\big)$$

For $h=1$ it is $\sigma_{t+1}^2$ itself (known); for $h\to\infty$ it is $\bar\sigma^2$: volatility mean-reverts.

EXAMPLE · one step of GARCH by hand

$\beta_0=0.02,\ \beta_1=0.90,\ \beta_2=0.08$ (returns in percent). Long-run variance $\frac{0.02}{1-0.98}=1$, so 1% per day.

  1. Today $\sigma_t^2=1$ and the market falls 3%: $r_t=-3$.
  2. $\sigma_{t+1}^2=0.02+0.90\cdot1+0.08\cdot9=1.64$, $\sigma_{t+1}=1.28\%$.
  3. Expected variance in two days: $1+0.98(1.64-1)=1.627$; in 20 days ($h=20$): $1+0.98^{19}\cdot0.64=1.436$.
  1. Had the return been $+3\%$, the result would be identical: GARCH ignores signs.
  2. Had it been 0: $\sigma_{t+1}^2=0.92$: volatility falls a little.
  3. Half-life: $\ln0.5/\ln0.98=34$ trading days.
  4. Implied $z_t=r_t/\sigma_t=-3$.
?●practiceMoments

$\sigma_t^2=0.1+0.85\sigma_{t-1}^2+0.10r_{t-1}^2$. Long-run variance, persistence, half-life. $\sigma_{t}^2=3$, $r_t=1$: $\sigma_{t+1}^2$?

Solution

$0.1/0.05=2$; persistence $0.95$; half-life 13.5 days. $0.1+2.55+0.1=2.75$: variance falls although it is above the mean, because the return was small.

?●●practiceARCH(1)

$\sigma_t^2=0.5+0.5r_{t-1}^2$, $r_{t-1}=2$, $z_t\sim N(0,1)$. Find $\mathbb V[r_t\mid\mathcal F_{t-1}]$, $\mathbb V[r_t]$, and $P(r_t\lt-3\mid\mathcal F_{t-1})$.

Solution

Conditional variance $0.5+2=2.5$; unconditional $0.5/0.5=1$. $P(Z\lt-3/1.581)=P(Z\lt-1.897)=0.029$. Unconditionally such a day is far rarer.

?●●●practiceWhy tails get fat

$r=\sigma z$, $z\sim N(0,1)$ independent of $\sigma$, $\sigma$ random. Show that the kurtosis $\mathbb E[r^4]/\mathbb E[r^2]^2\ge3$.

Solution

$\mathbb E[r^4]=\mathbb E[\sigma^4]\cdot3$, $\mathbb E[r^2]=\mathbb E[\sigma^2]$. Ratio $=3\,\mathbb E[\sigma^4]/\mathbb E[\sigma^2]^2\ge3$, since $\mathbb E[\sigma^4]-\mathbb E[\sigma^2]^2=\mathbb V[\sigma^2]\ge0$. Equality only if $\sigma$ is constant. Mixing calm and turbulent days creates heavy tails.
