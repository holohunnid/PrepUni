The F-test: J restrictions at once

lecture 8, slides 2–7
READ FIRST From one restriction to many— ⊡ ✕

With $J$ restrictions, $m$ is a vector and we use the quadratic form. If $\sigma^2$ is known it is $\chi^2_J$. If it is unknown, put $s^2$ and divide by $J$: an $F_{J,n-K}$. Large values mean $m$ is far from zero, so we reject only in the right tail.

DEF The statistic— ⊡ ✕

σ² known:

$$(Rb-q)'\big(\sigma^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'\big)^{-1}(Rb-q)\ \overset{H_0}{\sim}\ \chi^2_J$$

σ² unknown:

$$\boxed{F=\frac{(Rb-q)'\big(s^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'\big)^{-1}(Rb-q)}J\ \overset{H_0}{\sim}\ F_{J,\,n-K}}$$

Reject $H_0:R\beta=q$ if $F>\bar F$, the upper 5% quantile of $F_{J,n-K}$.

Why $F$: divide numerator and denominator by $\sigma^2$:

$$F=\frac{(Rb-q)'(\sigma^2R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(Rb-q)/J}{\dfrac{e'e}{\sigma^2}\Big/(n-K)}\overset D=\frac{\chi^2_J/J}{\chi^2_{n-K}/(n-K)}$$

Numerator: function of $b$. Denominator: function of $s^2$. Independent (module 22). Ratio of independent average chi-squares = $F_{J,n-K}$. The unknown $\sigma^2$ cancels.

LINK $F$ and $t$: for $J=1$, $F=t^2$— ⊡ ✕

With one restriction, $F=\dfrac{(Rb-q)^2}{s^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'}=t^2$ and $F_{1,n-K}=(t_{n-K})^2$. The critical values match: $3.182^2=10.13=\bar F_{1,3}$. So a two-sided $t$-test and the $F$-test give the same answer. The $t$ keeps the sign, so use it for one-sided alternatives.

Why not $J$ separate t-tests? (1) Each has size 5%, so the chance that at least one rejects a true joint null exceeds 5%. (2) They ignore the correlation among the $b_k$: with collinear regressors each $t$ can be small while $F$ is huge.

INTERACTIVE The F distribution and its right tail— ⊡ ✕

Try this. $F$ is never negative: a squared distance. Its mean is near 1 under $H_0$. As $n-K$ grows, $J\cdot F$ approaches $\chi^2_J$: e.g. $2\cdot\bar F_{2,\infty}=5.99$. With $J=1$ compare $\sqrt{\bar F}$ with the $t$ critical value.

RUNNING EXAMPLE · joint test of β₁ = 0 and β₂ = 1

$R=I_2$, $q=(0,1)'$, $m=(1.3,-0.1)'$, $J=2$, $n-K=3$, $s^2=0.6333$. $(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}=X'X$, so

$$F=\frac{m'X'Xm}{J\,s^2}=\frac{5.1}{2\cdot0.6333}=\mathbf{4.03}<\bar F_{2,3}=9.55\ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{do not reject.}$$

(Module 19 computed $m'X'Xm=5.1$.)

ES_TESTING DATA · are both slopes zero?

$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $q=0$, $m=(0.4,0.9)'$, $J=2$, $n-K=26$, $s^2=20$.

$R(X'X)^{-1}R'=\frac1{3900}\begin{bmatrix}80&-10\\-10&50\end{bmatrix}$. Its inverse is $\begin{bmatrix}50&10\\10&80\end{bmatrix}$ (determinant of the block $=80\cdot50-100=3900$; divide and the 3900s cancel).

$$m'\begin{bmatrix}50&10\\10&80\end{bmatrix}m=[0.4\ 0.9]\begin{bmatrix}29\\76\end{bmatrix}=11.6+68.4=80$$ $$F=\frac{80}{2\cdot20}=\mathbf{2.00}<\bar F_{2,26}=3.37\ \Rightarrow\ \text{do not reject.}$$

Individual $t$'s: $0.4/0.6405=0.62$ and $0.9/0.5064=1.78$, also insignificant.

FIGURE What to draw in the exam (W8 Part 2, question 3)— ⊡ ✕
?●●practiceSet up and decide

$n=50$, $K=4$. $H_0:\beta_3=0,\beta_4=0$. $m=(0.6,-0.4)'$, $s^2R(X'X)^{-1}R'=\begin{bmatrix}0.10&0.02\\0.02&0.05\end{bmatrix}$. Test at 5% ($\bar F_{2,46}=3.20$).

Solution

$\det=0.005-0.0004=0.0046$; inverse $=\frac1{0.0046}\begin{bmatrix}0.05&-0.02\\-0.02&0.10\end{bmatrix}$. $m'(\cdot)m=\frac{0.05\cdot0.36+2\cdot0.02\cdot0.24+0.10\cdot0.16}{0.0046}=\frac{0.018+0.0096+0.016}{0.0046}=9.478$. $F=4.74>3.20$ ⇒ reject.

?●●practiceFrom R²

$n=120$, $K=4$ (constant + 3), $R^2=0.09$. Test that all slopes are zero at 5% ($\bar F_{3,116}=2.68$).

Solution

$F=\frac{0.09/3}{0.91/116}=\frac{0.03}{0.007845}=3.82>2.68$ ⇒ reject. A small $R^2$ can still be significant when $n$ is large.
