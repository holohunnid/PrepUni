14

Goodness of fit: R²

lecture 3, slides 12–15
READ FIRST The share of the variance of y that X explains— ⊡ ✕

$R^2$ is one number between 0 and 1 that says how close $y$ is to the floor. Its main drawback is that it can only go up when you add regressors, even useless ones.

DEF Definition and derivation— ⊡ ✕

Assume $y,X_1,\dots,X_K$ have mean zero (or de-mean them). Then $\frac1ny'y=\frac1n\sum y_i^2$ is the sample variance of $y$. From $y=\hat y+e$ and $\hat y'e=0$:

$$\underbrace{\tfrac1ny'y}_{\text{variance of }y}=\underbrace{\tfrac1n\hat y'\hat y}_{\text{variance of fitted}}+\underbrace{\tfrac1ne'e}_{\text{variance of residuals}}$$ $$\boxed{R^2=\frac{\hat y'\hat y}{y'y}=1-\frac{e'e}{y'y}\qquad 0\le R^2\le1}$$

Why between 0 and 1? All three terms are sums of squares, so $0\le\hat y'\hat y\le y'y$.

If data are not de-meaned but there is a constant, use deviations from the mean:

$$R^2=1-\frac{e'e}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}=\frac{\sum(\hat y_i-\bar y)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}$$
Angle interpretation

$R^2=\cos^2\theta$, with $\theta$ the angle between $y$ and the floor. Perfect fit: $\theta=0°$. Worst: $90°$. With one regressor plus constant, $R^2=\hat\rho_{xy}^2$.

Routine in any software: (1) estimate $b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$; (2) compute $R^2$.

TRAP · what a low R² does not mean

A low $R^2$ does not mean OLS is biased, nor that $\beta_2=0$. Daily single-stock CAPM regressions often have $R^2$ around 0.3 and a very precisely estimated beta. $R^2$ measures noise relative to signal, not whether the model is right. And a high $R^2$ does not prove causality.

RUNNING EXAMPLE

$\bar y=4$: $\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2=4+1+1+0+4=10$. $e'e=1.90$. $R^2=1-0.19=\mathbf{0.81}$. Check: $\hat\rho=\dfrac{9}{\sqrt{10\cdot10}}=0.9$ and $0.9^2=0.81$ ✓. (Here $S_{xx}=S_{yy}=10$, which is why $b_2=\hat\rho$.)

Using $\frac{\hat y'\hat y}{y'y}=\frac{88.1}{90}=0.979$ without de-meaning would be wrong: it credits the model for explaining the mean.

DRAWBACK $R^2$ mechanically increases with more regressors— ⊡ ✕

Go from $K$ to $K+L$ columns: $[X_1\cdots X_K\ X_{K+1}\cdots X_{K+L}]$. The column space gets larger, so the closest point in it cannot be farther away:

$$\min_{b\in\mathbb R^{K+L}}\lVert y-X_{new}b\rVert^2\ \le\ \min_{b\in\mathbb R^K}\lVert y-Xb\rVert^2$$

because you can always set the extra coefficients to zero. So $e'e$ falls (weakly) and $R^2$ rises (weakly), even if the new regressors have little to do with $y$.

Extreme case: with $K=n$ independent columns, the column space is all of $\mathbb R^n$. Then $\hat y=y$, $e=0$, $R^2=1$ whatever the regressors are. The model has memorised the sample and $s^2=e'e/(n-K)$ is $0/0$.

Beyond the slides · adjusted $R^2$

Software reports $\bar R^2=1-\dfrac{e'e/(n-K)}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2/(n-1)}$, which can fall when a useless regressor is added. Running example: $1-\frac{0.6333}{2.5}=0.747$.

INTERACTIVE Add useless regressors and watch R² rise— ⊡ ✕

Try this. The blue line never goes down, and it reaches 1 when $K=n$. With $n=10$ and pure noise you get a "good" $R^2$ after a handful of random columns. Larger $n$ makes the climb slower. The amber line does not reward noise on average.

Solution

$R^2=1-0.25=0.75$. $\hat y'\hat y=150$. $\lVert\hat y\rVert=\sqrt{150}=12.25$.

?●●practiceBoth at once

Model A has $K=2$, $R^2=0.40$; model B adds 10 regressors, $R^2=0.45$, $n=60$. Does B explain $y$ better? What would you do?

Solution

The rise alone proves nothing: it is mechanical. Test $H_0$: the 10 extra coefficients are zero, with an F-test (module 24): $F=\frac{(0.45-0.40)/10}{(1-0.45)/(60-12)}=\frac{0.005}{0.01146}=0.44$, far below the critical value of about 2.0. Do not reject: the extra regressors add nothing.

?●●lecture 5, slide 6 questionWhat is R² under the hypothesis β₂ = β₃ = β₄ = 0?

In $y_i=\beta_1+\beta_2x_{i2}+\beta_3x_{i3}+\beta_4x_{i4}+\varepsilon_i$.

Solution

Under the hypothesis the regressors explain nothing of the variation of $y$: the population $R^2$ is 0. The sample $R^2$ is close to 0 but positive, because $b$ is not exactly zero (and $R^2$ is mechanically non-negative). Its expected value under the null is about $(K-1)/(n-1)$.
