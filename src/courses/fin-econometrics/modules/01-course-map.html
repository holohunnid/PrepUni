READ FIRST The whole course in six sentences— ⊡ ✕
  1. We want to explain a variable $y$ (a stock's return) with variables $X$ (the market's return). We assume the linear model $y=X\beta+\varepsilon$ (lecture 1).
  2. $\beta$ is a fixed number we never see. We estimate it with OLS, $b=(X'X)^{-1}X'y$ (lecture 2).
  3. OLS is a projection: it splits $y$ into a part that $X$ explains, $\hat y=Xb$, and a part it does not, $e\perp X$. The share explained is $R^2$ (lecture 3).
  4. Before we see the data, $b$ is random. It is linear, unbiased, has variance $\sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}$, and is the best linear unbiased estimator. $\sigma^2$ is estimated by $s^2=e'e/(n-K)$ (lecture 4).
  5. Because $b$ is random, $b_1=0.005$ does not mean $\beta_1\neq0$. Hypothesis testing decides, using $m=Rb-q$ and the distributions $N$, $\chi^2$, $t$, $F$ (lectures 5–8).
  6. Data that come in time order need models with memory: AR(1) for the level of a series, GARCH for its volatility, and from GARCH the risk measures VaR and ES (time series and GARCH decks).

TRAP · Greek versus Latin

$\beta$ and $b$ are different objects, and so are $\varepsilon$ and $e$, $\sigma^2$ and $s^2$. Greek letters belong to the data generating process (fixed and unobserved, or random and unobserved). Latin letters are computed from the sample. The exercise sheets also write $\hat\beta$ for $b$ and $N$ or $T$ for $n$; they mean the same thing.
