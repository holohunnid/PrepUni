09

OLS with one regressor, by hand

lecture 3, slide 6 exercise · W6 tutorial, ex. 1 and 2
READ FIRST Slope = covariance over variance— ⊡ ✕

With a constant and one regressor the matrix formula collapses into two formulas you should be able to derive and use without matrices:

$$\boxed{b_2=\frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}[y_i,x_{i2}]}{\hat{\mathbb V}[x_{i2}]}\qquad b_1=\bar y-b_2\bar x}$$

This is an explicit exercise on slide 6 of lecture 3 and was solved in the W6 tutorial.

DEF The sample moments— ⊡ ✕
$$\bar y=\frac1n\sum y_i,\quad\bar x=\frac1n\sum x_{i2},\quad\widehat{\text{Cov}}[y_i,x_{i2}]=\frac1n\sum(y_i-\bar y)(x_{i2}-\bar x)$$

and $\hat{\mathbb V}[x_{i2}]=\widehat{\text{Cov}}[x_{i2},x_{i2}]$. Dividing by $n$ or $n-1$ does not matter for $b_2$: it cancels.

The shortcut used in the proof (expand the product):

$$\frac1n\sum(y_i-\bar y)(x_{i2}-\bar x)=\frac1n\sum x_{i2}y_i-\bar x\bar y$$

because $\sum x_{i2}=n\bar x$ and $\sum y_i=n\bar y$: the four terms give $\frac1n(\sum x_{i2}y_i-n\bar x\bar y-n\bar x\bar y+n\bar x\bar y)$.

LECTURE 3, SLIDE 6 · W6 EX 2 · the proof

Write out $X'y-X'Xb=0$:

$$\begin{bmatrix}\sum y_i\\\sum x_{i2}y_i\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}nb_1+b_2\sum x_{i2}\\b_1\sum x_{i2}+b_2\sum x_{i2}^2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}$$

Row 1. $n\bar y-nb_1-b_2n\bar x=0$. Divide by $n$:

$$b_1=\bar y-b_2\bar x$$

Row 2. Substitute $b_1$:

$$\sum x_{i2}y_i-(\bar y-b_2\bar x)\sum x_{i2}-b_2\sum x_{i2}^2=0$$

Divide by $n$, with $\frac1n\sum x_{i2}=\bar x$, and collect $b_2$:

$$\frac1n\sum x_{i2}y_i-\bar y\bar x=b_2\Big(\frac1n\sum x_{i2}^2-\bar x^2\Big)$$ $$b_2=\frac{\frac1n\sum x_{i2}y_i-\bar x\bar y}{\frac1n\sum x_{i2}^2-\bar x^2}=\frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}[x_{i2},y_i]}{\hat{\mathbb V}[x_{i2}]}\quad\blacksquare$$
W6 EX 1 (from Greene) · same result from the residuals, $y=a+bx+e$
  1. a) $\sum e_i=0$ and $\sum x_ie_i=0$. $X'e=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1\\x_1&\cdots&x_n\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}e_1\\\vdots\\e_n\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum e_i\\\sum x_ie_i\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}$. Row 1 comes from the constant, row 2 from $x$.
  2. b) $a=\bar y-b\bar x$. Each row says $y_i-bx_i=a+e_i$. Average over $i$: $\bar y-b\bar x=a+\frac1n\sum e_i=a+0$.
  3. c) $b=\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)/\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$. Subtract the averages: $y_i-\bar y=b(x_i-\bar x)+e_i$. Multiply by $(x_i-\bar x)$ and sum: $\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)=b\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2+\underbrace{\sum x_ie_i}_{0}-\bar x\underbrace{\sum e_i}_{0}$. Divide. $\blacksquare$
Reading the handwritten sheet

Part c) of the W6 notes writes a sum inside the bracket, $\sum(x_i-\bar x)\big[\sum b(x_i-\bar x)-\sum(y_i-\bar y)\big]$, which is not the same expression. The argument works term by term, as above. The notes also write $\varepsilon$ where the sample quantity $e$ is meant: $X'e=0$ is true by construction, $X'\varepsilon=0$ is not.

KEY What the formulas tell you— ⊡ ✕
  • The line passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$: plug $x=\bar x$ into $b_1+b_2x$ to get $\bar y$.
  • Sign of slope = sign of covariance.
  • Slope vs correlation: $b_2=\hat\rho_{xy}\,\dfrac{\hat\sigma_y}{\hat\sigma_x}$. They are equal only if $x$ and $y$ have the same standard deviation.
  • Need variation in $x$: if all $x_i$ are equal, $\hat{\mathbb V}[x]=0$ and $b_2$ is undefined (A1b fails).

?●practiceBy hand

$x=(0,1,2,3)$, $y=(1,3,2,6)$. Find $b_1$, $b_2$, residuals, and check $\sum e_i=0$ and $\sum x_ie_i=0$.

Solution

$\bar x=1.5$, $\bar y=3$. $S_{xy}=(-1.5)(-2)+(-0.5)(0)+(0.5)(-1)+(1.5)(3)=3+0-0.5+4.5=7$. $S_{xx}=2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25=5$. $b_2=1.4$, $b_1=3-2.1=0.9$. $\hat y=(0.9,2.3,3.7,5.1)$, $e=(0.1,0.7,-1.7,0.9)$. Sum $=0$ ✓; $\sum xe=0.7-3.4+2.7=0$ ✓.

?●●practiceFrom summary statistics to beta

Monthly data: $\hat\sigma_{IBM}=6\%$, $\hat\sigma_m=4\%$, correlation $0.5$, $\bar r_{IBM}=0.9\%$, $\bar r_m=0.6\%$ (excess returns). Compute $\hat\beta$ and $\hat\alpha$.

Solution

$\hat\beta=0.5\cdot6/4=0.75$. $\hat\alpha=0.9-0.75\cdot0.6=0.45\%$ per month.

?●●●practiceSwap the roles

You regress $y$ on $x$ (slope $b$) and then $x$ on $y$ (slope $d$). Show $b\cdot d=\hat\rho^2$, and that in general $d\neq1/b$.

Solution

$b=\frac{\text{Cov}}{\mathbb V[x]}$, $d=\frac{\text{Cov}}{\mathbb V[y]}$, so $bd=\frac{\text{Cov}^2}{\mathbb V[x]\mathbb V[y]}=\hat\rho^2\le1$. It equals 1 (so $d=1/b$) only with a perfect fit. Vertical and horizontal distances are different criteria, so the direction $X\Rightarrow y$ matters even numerically. In module 14: $\hat\rho^2=R^2$.
