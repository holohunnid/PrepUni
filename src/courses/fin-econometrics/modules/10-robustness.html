Outliers & the missing intercept

lecture 2, slides 10–11 · the two slide questions
READ FIRST Two ways to ruin a regression line— ⊡ ✕

OLS squares the misses. A miss of 10 counts 100 times more than a miss of 1, so one wild point can drag the line a long way. And if you leave out the constant, you force the line through the origin whether the data like it or not.

INTERACTIVE Move one point, drop the constant— ⊡ ✕

Slide 10's question: what happens to the OLS line? It tilts towards the outlier. With $y_5=0$ instead of 6 the slope goes from $0.9$ to $-0.3$: one observation out of five flips the sign of the effect.

Slide 11's question: where does the line go without the constant? Through the origin. The slope becomes $\frac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2}=\frac{69}{55}=1.25$ instead of $0.9$.

WHY Why outliers pull so hard— ⊡ ✕

$b_2$ is a weighted sum of the $y_i$:

$$b_2=\sum_{i=1}^n w_iy_i,\qquad w_i=\frac{x_i-\bar x}{\sum_j(x_j-\bar x)^2}$$

Two things make point $i$ influential: $y_i$ far from the others, and $x_i$ far from $\bar x$ (a large weight, "leverage"). In the running example the weights are $(-0.2,-0.1,0,0.1,0.2)$, so moving $y_5$ by $-6$ moves $b_2$ by $-6\cdot0.2=-1.2$ ✓.

The middle point $x_3=\bar x$ has weight 0: move it as far as you like and the slope does not change (only the intercept and $s^2$ do).

In finance

The 1987 crash day is a log-return of −0.23, about 20 standard deviations (module 31). Whether such a day is in the sample can change an estimated beta visibly. OLS is BLUE under the assumptions, but it is not robust.

WHY Why dropping the intercept hurts— ⊡ ✕

Suppose the truth is $y_i=\beta_1+\beta_2x_i+\varepsilon_i$ with $\beta_1\neq0$ and you fit $y_i=\tilde b_2x_i+\text{residual}$. Then

$$\mathbb E[\tilde b_2\mid X]=\frac{\sum x_i\,\mathbb E[y_i\mid X]}{\sum x_i^2}=\beta_2+\beta_1\frac{\sum x_i}{\sum x_i^2}$$

Biased, unless $\beta_1=0$ or $\bar x=0$. It is an omitted-variable bias: the omitted regressor is the column of ones.

You also lose:

  • $\sum e_i=0$ (no column of ones to enforce it): in the example the residuals sum to $1.18$;
  • the usual $R^2$, which can become negative when computed as $1-e'e/\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$;
  • $\alpha$ itself. In the CAPM the intercept is what you want to know.

Rule: keep the constant. If it is truly zero, the cost of including it is small (a little variance). If it is not zero, the cost of omitting it is bias.

?●●practiceLeverage

$x=(1,2,3,4,10)$. Which observation has the largest weight in $b_2$? By how much does $b_2$ move if its $y$ rises by 1?

Solution

$\bar x=4$, deviations $(-3,-2,-1,0,6)$, $S_{xx}=9+4+1+0+36=50$. Weights $(-0.06,-0.04,-0.02,0,0.12)$. The last has the largest: $b_2$ rises by $0.12$. The fourth point has no effect on the slope.

?●●practiceBias through the origin

True model $y_i=2+0.5x_i+\varepsilon_i$ with $x=(1,2,3)$. What is $\mathbb E$ of the no-intercept slope?

Solution

$0.5+2\cdot\frac{6}{14}=0.5+0.857=1.357$. It is nearly three times the truth.
