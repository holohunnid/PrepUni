06

The linear regression model

lecture 1, slides 3–6, 11
READ FIRST A sentence about a conditional mean— ⊡ ✕

The linear model says: on average, given the regressors, $y$ is a linear function of them, and whatever is left over is noise with mean zero. The "workhorse" of the course is one line, $y_i=x_i'\beta+\varepsilon_i$. Reading it correctly is half of lecture 1.

DEF The model, three ways— ⊡ ✕

One observation, written out: for $i=1,\dots,n$

$$y_i=\beta_1x_{i1}+\beta_2x_{i2}+\dots+\beta_Kx_{iK}+\varepsilon_i$$

One observation, compact: with $x_i=(x_{i1},\dots,x_{iK})'$ and $\beta=(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_K)'$, both $K\times1$,

$$y_i=x_i'\beta+\varepsilon_i$$

All observations stacked:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\\vdots\\y_n\end{bmatrix}}_{n\times1}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_1'\\\vdots\\x_n'\end{bmatrix}}_{n\times K}\underbrace{\beta}_{K\times1}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_1\\\vdots\\\varepsilon_n\end{bmatrix}}_{n\times1}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{y=X\beta+\varepsilon}$$
objectmeaningobserved
$y_i$dependent variable for "individual" $i$yes
$x_{ik}$regressor ("factor", explanatory variable) $k$ for $i$yes
$\beta_k$coefficient, a constant to be estimatedno
$\varepsilon_i$error term: everything else that moves $y_i$no

"Individual" $i$ can be a person, a firm, or a month: in time series we write $t$ and $T$ instead.

Goal: use $x$ to explain $y$. Direction of causality: $x\Rightarrow y$.

Constant term: almost always $x_{i1}=1$ for all $i$, so the first column of $X$ is ones and $\beta_1$ is the intercept. Including it is "very useful and very important"; module 10 shows what goes wrong without it.

LECTURE 1 · the CAPM example (risk-free rate = 0)

$y_i$ = IBM return in month $i$; $x_{i1}=1$; $x_{i2}$ = S&P 500 return in month $i$:

$$y_i=\beta_1+\beta_2x_{i2}+\varepsilon_i$$

What are the values of $\beta_1,\beta_2$? Unknown. We estimate them (lecture 2).

Is $\beta_2=0$? Why does it matter? If $\beta_2=0$ the market has no effect on the expected IBM return: IBM carries no market risk and cannot be used as a hedge. We need a formal test (lectures 5–7), because an estimate is never exactly 0.

What if $y_i$ is our portfolio return and $\beta_2\gg0$? Our portfolio is heavily exposed to the market. A market fall of 10% costs us about $10\beta_2\%$. If we don't want that risk, we must hedge it (module 11).

Slide 11. Suppose the estimates are $\beta_1=0$, $\beta_2=0.5$, so $\mathbb E[y_i\mid X]=0.5\,x_{i2}$. If the market goes up by 2%, the expected IBM return is $0.5\cdot2\%=1\%$.

Slide question · are we predicting next month's IBM return?

No. $y_i$ and $x_{i2}$ are from the same month. The model says what IBM does on average given this month's market return. To predict next month you would need next month's market return, which you don't know, or a regressor known today (as in the AR(1) of module 27, where the regressor is $Y_{t-1}$).

KEY Reading the coefficients— ⊡ ✕

Under $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$ (module 07), $\mathbb E[y_i\mid X]=x_i'\beta$, so

$$\frac{\partial\,\mathbb E[y_i\mid X]}{\partial x_{ik}}=\beta_k .$$

$\beta_k$ is the change in the conditional mean of $y_i$ when $x_{ik}$ rises by one unit, holding the other regressors fixed. $\beta_1$ is the conditional mean when all other regressors are zero.

"Linear" means linear in the parameters. These are all linear models:

  • $y_i=\beta_1+\beta_2x_i+\beta_3x_i^2+\varepsilon_i$ (define a regressor $x_i^2$);
  • $\log Y_j=\log c+\gamma\log K_j+\theta\log L_j+\varepsilon_j$ (Cobb–Douglas after logs, lecture 5).

$y_i=\beta_1x_i^{\beta_2}+\varepsilon_i$ is not.

EXAM FORMAT · W8 Part 1, question 1 · write the model as y = Xβ + ε

$C_t=\gamma_0+\gamma_1I_t+\gamma_2r_t+\varepsilon_t$, $t=1,\dots,T$. "Define the content and dimension of $y,X,\beta,\varepsilon$."

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}C_1\\\vdots\\C_T\end{bmatrix}}_{y\ (T\times1)}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&I_1&r_1\\\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\1&I_T&r_T\end{bmatrix}}_{X\ (T\times3)}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_0\\\gamma_1\\\gamma_2\end{bmatrix}}_{\beta\ (3\times1)}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon_1\\\vdots\\\varepsilon_T\end{bmatrix}}_{\varepsilon\ (T\times1)}$$

Say it in words too: $y$ stacks consumption, $X$ has a column of ones, income and the interest rate, $\beta$ the three unknown parameters, $\varepsilon$ the unobserved errors. Here $K=3$.

?●practiceCobb–Douglas in matrix form

$Y_j=cK_j^\gamma L_j^\theta$ for firms $j=1,\dots,n$, estimated in logs with an error. Write $y,X,\beta$ with dimensions.

Solution

$y=(\log Y_1,\dots,\log Y_n)'$ is $n\times1$. $X$ is $n\times3$ with rows $(1,\log K_j,\log L_j)$. $\beta=(\log c,\gamma,\theta)'$ is $3\times1$. $\gamma$ is the output elasticity of capital: a 1% rise in $K$ raises expected output by about $\gamma$%.

?●●practiceWhat does the intercept mean?

$r_t-r_t^f=\alpha+\beta(r_t^m-r_t^f)+\varepsilon_t$. Interpret $\alpha$. What would the intercept mean if you regressed raw returns on raw market returns with $r^f\neq0$?

Solution

$\alpha$ is the expected excess return when the market's excess return is zero: the risk-adjusted performance. With raw returns, $r_t=\alpha+(1-\beta)r_t^f+\beta r_t^m+\varepsilon_t$, so the intercept mixes $\alpha$ with $(1-\beta)r^f$. Use excess returns to read $\alpha$ directly.
