Linear transformations

what survives $g(X)=a+bX$, and what does not
THM The two rules— ⊡ ✕

$g(X)$ applies a function to the variable, producing a new random variable. When $g$ is linear, $g(x)=a+bx$, both moments transform by simple rules:

$$E[a+bX]=a+b\,E[X]$$ $$V[a+bX]=b^2\,V[X]\qquad \textit{(the constant }a\textit{ disappears)}$$

Why the constant behaves differently

Shifting every value by $a$ moves the centre but changes no distance between points, so the spread is untouched. Scaling by $b$ stretches all distances by $|b|$, and variance is measured in squared units — hence $b^2$, and hence the sign of $b$ vanishing.

Trap — linearity fails for nonlinear $g$
In general $E[g(X)]\ne g(E[X])$. The cleanest proof is variance itself: $E[X^2]\ne(E[X])^2$ unless $V[X]=0$. So knowing $E[X]$ alone is enough for $E[1+2X]$ and useless for $E[X^2]$ or $E[1/X]$.

Standardisation — the payoff

Combining both rules gives the $z$-score behind every normal-distribution question:

$$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\ \Longrightarrow\ E[Z]=0,\quad V[Z]=1$$

Read it as $a+bX$ with $a=-\mu/\sigma$ and $b=1/\sigma$. Mean: $(\mu-\mu)/\sigma=0$. Variance: $(1/\sigma)^2\sigma^2=1$.

Tip — read what you were given
If a question hands you $E[X]$ and nothing else, the function must be linear — otherwise the question would be unanswerable. "What am I given?" is often faster than "what am I asked?".
Finance
$b^2$ is the reason leverage is quadratic in risk. Doubling a position ($b=2$) doubles expected return but quadruples variance — so volatility doubles too. Return scales linearly, risk scales by $|b|$ in $\sigma$ terms.
