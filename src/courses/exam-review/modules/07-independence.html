Independent vs uncorrelated

the most heavily tested distinction in the paper
DEF One implication, and it runs one way only— ⊡ ✕
PropertyDefinitionStrength
Independent$P(X{=}x,\,Y{=}y)=P(X{=}x)P(Y{=}y)$ for every pairstrong
Uncorrelated$\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]=0$weak
$$\text{independent}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{uncorrelated},\qquad \text{uncorrelated}\ \not\Longrightarrow\ \text{independent}$$

Independence constrains the entire joint distribution — every value, every combination. Uncorrelatedness constrains one single number. Covariance sees only straight-line association, so any symmetric curved relationship is invisible to it.

Tip — the exception
For jointly normal variables, uncorrelated does imply independent. It is the only common case where the implication runs both ways, and questions later in the paper test exactly this.

The counterexample to memorise

Let $X$ take $-1, 0, 1$ with probability $1/3$ each, and set $Y=X^2$.

$E[X]=0$ and $E[XY]=0$, so $\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]=0-0\cdot E[Y]=0$: uncorrelated. Yet $Y$ is completely determined by $X$ — maximally dependent. The parabola is symmetric, so the positive and negative contributions cancel exactly.

What independence buys you

$$X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\ \Longrightarrow\ E\big[g(X)h(Y)\big]=E[g(X)]\,E[h(Y)]\ \ \text{for \textit{any} } g,h$$

Zero covariance only gives you the single case $g(X)=X$, $h(Y)=Y$. Nothing more — which is precisely what makes Q6 unanswerable and Q5 answerable.
