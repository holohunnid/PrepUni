Transformations & sums of random variables

what happens to distributions when you compute with them
DEF Functions of one RV — the CDF method (always works)— ⊡ ✕

Given the distribution of $X$, what is the distribution of $Y=g(X)$? The bulletproof recipe:

  1. Write $F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)=P(g(X)\le y)$.
  2. Solve the inequality $g(x)\le y$ for $x$ (careful with direction if $g$ is decreasing).
  3. Express the result through $F_X$; differentiate to get $f_Y=F_Y'$.

Worked in full — the lognormal, properly derived. $Y=e^{X}$, $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$:

$$F_Y(y)=P(e^X\le y)=P(X\le \ln y)=\Phi\Big(\frac{\ln y-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)$$

Differentiate (chain rule, $\Phi'=\varphi$ the standard normal pdf):

$$f_Y(y)=\varphi\Big(\frac{\ln y-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)\frac{1}{\sigma y}=\frac{1}{y\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(\ln y-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}},\ \ y\gt0$$

— exactly module 6's lognormal density. No memorisation needed: the CDF method rebuilds it in four lines.

Change-of-variable formula (monotone g)

If $g$ is strictly monotone with inverse $g^{-1}$:

$$f_Y(y)=f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\cdot\Big|\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\Big|$$

Read it as conservation of probability: the mass $f_X(x)dx$ lands on the interval $dy$, and $|d g^{-1}/dy|$ is the stretching factor. The absolute value keeps the density positive when $g$ decreases.

Standard cases

$Y=aX+b$$f_Y(y)=\frac{1}{|a|}f_X\big(\frac{y-b}{a}\big)$ — shift & stretch
$Y=e^X$$f_Y(y)=\frac1y f_X(\ln y)$, $y\gt0$
$Y=X^2$ (not monotone!)$F_Y(y)=P(-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y)=F_X(\sqrt y)-F_X(-\sqrt y)$, then differentiate: $f_Y(y)=\frac{f_X(\sqrt y)+f_X(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y}$
Trap
For non-monotone $g$ (like $x^2$) the one-line formula is invalid — you must go through the CDF and collect every $x$-region mapping below $y$. Applying $Z^2$ to a standard normal this way yields the $\chi^2_1$ density — a favourite exam derivation (W19).
THM Sums of independent RVs — three tools— ⊡ ✕

1 · Moments (no distribution needed)

$E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]$ always; $\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2$ under independence. Often the question only asks for these — don't derive a distribution you don't need.

2 · Convolution (the honest way)

$$f_{X+Y}(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,f_Y(s-x)\,dx$$

"All the ways to split s into x + (s−x)". Rarely computed by hand in exams beyond simple cases — recognise it, then reach for tool 3.

3 · MGFs (the fast way)

$$M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t)$$

Multiply the MGFs, recognise the product as a known MGF, conclude by uniqueness. Example: two independent Poissons, $e^{\lambda_1(e^t-1)}e^{\lambda_2(e^t-1)}=e^{(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(e^t-1)}$ — a Poisson MGF, so $X+Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$. Three lines, no convolution.

THM Max & min — order statistics, light version— ⊡ ✕

For $X_1,\dots,X_n$ iid with CDF $F$, the extremes have clean CDFs because "max ≤ y" means all ≤ y, and "min > y" means all > y:

$$F_{\max}(y)=\big[F(y)\big]^n\qquad\qquad P(\min\gt y)=\big[1-F(y)\big]^n$$ $$\text{so}\quad F_{\min}(y)=1-\big[1-F(y)\big]^n$$

Example — first default. $n$ loans with independent Exp(λ) default times: $P(\min\gt t)=(e^{-\lambda t})^n=e^{-n\lambda t}$, so the first default time is Exp(nλ) — mean time to first default $\frac{1}{n\lambda}$: ten times the loans, one tenth the wait.

Example — max of uniforms. $X_i\sim U(0,1)$: $F_{\max}(y)=y^n$, density $ny^{n-1}$, $E[\max]=\frac{n}{n+1}$ — the record creeps toward 1.

Finance
Worst-of / best-of structures: a "worst-of" note pays on the minimum of several asset returns; barrier and first-to-default products live entirely on min/max distributions.

Probability integral transform — why U(0,1) generates everything

If $X$ has continuous CDF $F$, then $F(X)\sim U(0,1)$. Reverse direction: for $U\sim U(0,1)$,

$$X=F^{-1}(U)\ \text{ has CDF } F$$

This is how simulation works: draw uniforms, push them through the inverse CDF. Exp(λ) example: $X=-\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1-U)$. Monte Carlo (modules 9–10) is built on this one line.

EX Transformation drill— ⊡ ✕

$X\sim U(0,1)$. Find the density of $Y=-2\ln X$, and identify the distribution.

  1. Range: $x\in(0,1)\Rightarrow \ln x\in(-\infty,0)\Rightarrow y\in(0,\infty)$.
  2. CDF method: $F_Y(y)=P(-2\ln X\le y)=P\big(\ln X\ge -\tfrac y2\big)=P\big(X\ge e^{-y/2}\big)=1-e^{-y/2}$ (using $F_X(x)=x$ on (0,1); note the inequality flipped when dividing by −2).
  3. Differentiate: $f_Y(y)=\tfrac12 e^{-y/2}$, $y\gt0$.
  4. Recognise: Exponential with λ = ½ — equivalently Gamma(1, ½) = $\chi^2_2$. So $-2\ln U\sim\chi^2_2$.
Insight
Two habits: track the support first (it tells you where the density can live and catches algebra slips), and watch inequality directions under decreasing transformations. The result itself is the engine of classic simulation methods for normals and chi-squares.
