Finance applications

everything above, pointed at markets
DEF Returns as random variables— ⊡ ✕

Price today $S_0$, price tomorrow $S_1$ (random). Two ways to measure the move:

Simple return$R=\dfrac{S_1-S_0}{S_0}$, so $S_1=S_0(1+R)$
Log return$r=\ln\dfrac{S_1}{S_0}=\ln(1+R)$, so $S_1=S_0e^{r}$

Why logs? They add over time: $r_{0\to T}=r_1+r_2+\dots+r_T$ (the product of growth factors becomes a sum of logs). Sums of iid random variables are exactly what modules 8–9 handle — so the whole limit-theorem machinery applies to log returns.

For small moves $r\approx R$ (e.g. R = 1% → r = 0.995%); they diverge for big moves (R = −50% → r = −69%).

Aggregation of iid log returns

Daily log returns iid with mean μ, variance σ². Over T days:

$$E[r_{0\to T}]=\mu T\qquad \operatorname{Var}(r_{0\to T})=\sigma^2T\qquad \sigma_{0\to T}=\sigma\sqrt T$$
The √T rule
Means scale with time, volatility with its square root: annualise daily vol with $\sqrt{252}$ (σ_annual ≈ 16 × σ_daily). A 1% daily vol is a ≈16% annual vol. This asymmetry (drift grows faster than noise) is why long horizons favour risky assets in expectation.
Trap
Never annualise by multiplying σ by 252, and never add simple returns across time (they compound, not add). Log returns add across time; simple returns add across a portfolio (with weights). Use each in its lane.
THM The lognormal price model— ⊡ ✕

If the cumulative log return is normal — which the CLT makes plausible, since it is a sum of many small shocks — then with $Z\sim N(0,1)$:

$$S_T=S_0\,e^{\mu T+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad S_T\ \text{is lognormal}$$

From module 6's lognormal formulas:

$$\text{median}(S_T)=S_0e^{\mu T}\qquad E[S_T]=S_0\,e^{\mu T+\sigma^2T/2}$$

The mean sits above the median by the Jensen term $e^{\sigma^2T/2}$: volatility itself lifts the expected price, while the typical (median) outcome is lower. Skewed distributions force you to say which "average" you mean.

Probability the asset beats a target

All such questions reduce to taking logs and standardising:

$$P(S_T\gt K)=P\Big(Z\gt\frac{\ln(K/S_0)-\mu T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\Big)$$

Example. $S_0=100$, μ = 8%/yr, σ = 20%/yr, T = 1: $P(S_1\gt120)=1-\Phi\big(\frac{\ln1.2-0.08}{0.2}\big)=1-\Phi(0.51)\approx \mathbf{30.5\%}$.

The same standardised quantity, with a $\sigma^2/2$ adjustment, is the $d_2$ inside Black–Scholes — you will meet it again in the MSc.

INTERACTIVE Diversification — the only free lunch— ⊡ ✕

Two assets: A with σ = 20%, B with σ = 30%, weights $w$ and $1-w$. Portfolio variance (module 7): $\sigma_P^2=w^2\sigma_A^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_B^2+2w(1-w)\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B$. Slide the correlation:

Read off the chart: the minimum of the curve is the minimum-variance portfolio; its weight is $w^*=\dfrac{\sigma_B^2-\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2-2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}$.

The whole magic is the cross term $2w(1-w)\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B$: with ρ < 1, risks partially cancel while expected returns simply average — risk falls with no cost in expectation.

n-asset version: $\sigma_P^2=\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ (LinAlg module 10). Idiosyncratic risk dies like 1/n; average covariance survives as the market-risk floor (module 9).

DEF Value-at-Risk — a quantile wearing a suit— ⊡ ✕

The α-VaR (say α = 95%) is the loss level exceeded with probability only $1-\alpha$: the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the P&L distribution, sign-flipped into a positive "amount at risk".

If the portfolio return over the horizon is $R\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $V$ is portfolio value:

$$\text{VaR}_\alpha=V\big(z_\alpha\sigma-\mu\big),\qquad z_{0.95}=1.645,\ z_{0.99}=2.326$$

Example. V = €10m, monthly μ = 0.5%, σ = 4%: $\text{VaR}_{95\%}=10\text{m}\times(1.645\times0.04-0.005)=\mathbf{€608{,}000}$ — the monthly loss exceeded only 1 month in 20.

Trap
VaR says nothing about how bad losses are beyond the threshold, and normal-based VaR inherits the thin tails of the normal — real returns (fat-tailed, module 4) breach it more often than advertised. State assumptions when you quote a VaR.
DEF Binomial tree & risk-neutral probability— ⊡ ✕

Each period the price moves up by factor $u$ or down by $d$. After $n$ periods with $k$ up-moves: $S_n=S_0u^kd^{\,n-k}$, and the number of up-moves is Binomial(n, p) — the whole tree is module 5 wearing prices.

For pricing, the market replaces the real p with the risk-neutral probability — the unique q making the expected growth equal the risk-free rate:

$$q=\frac{(1+r_f)-d}{u-d}\qquad\text{price}=\frac{E_q[\text{payoff}]}{(1+r_f)^n}$$

Example. u = 1.1, d = 0.909, r_f = 2%: $q=\frac{1.02-0.909}{1.1-0.909}\approx0.581$. One-period call, strike 100: payoff 10 up / 0 down. Price $=\frac{0.581\times10}{1.02}\approx\mathbf{5.70}$.

Note what q is not: nobody believes the up-move has probability 58% — q is a change of measure that absorbs risk aversion. Expected payoffs under q, discounted at r_f, give arbitrage-free prices; the E, Var, Binomial machinery is unchanged.

DEF Sharpe ratio — and an honesty check on the whole module— ⊡ ✕

Risk-adjusted performance = excess return per unit of volatility:

$$\text{Sharpe}=\frac{E[R]-r_f}{\sigma_R}$$

Annualisation (iid returns): multiply a daily Sharpe by $\sqrt{252}$ — the mean scales with T, σ with √T, so the ratio scales with √T. Comparing funds on Sharpe is comparing their entire (μ, σ) pairs in one number.

Example. Fund: 9% expected return, 15% vol; r_f = 1%: Sharpe = 0.53. Market at 7%/12%: Sharpe = 0.50 — the fund's extra return does (slightly) pay for its extra risk.

Model risk — the fine print
This module's clean formulas assume returns are iid and (log)normal. Real returns have negative skew, fat tails (kurtosis ≫ 3), and volatility clustering (calm and stormy periods — not iid). Consequences: normal VaR understates tail risk; Sharpe ignores skew (selling crash insurance looks great until the crash); √T-scaling misprices long-horizon risk. The formulas are the exam answer and the first-order model — knowing where they bend is what separates using them from believing them.
