Joint distributions, covariance & correlation

two random variables at once — where portfolios begin
DEF Joint, marginal, conditional — one table holds everything— ⊡ ✕

The joint PMF $p(x,y)=P(X=x,\ Y=y)$ gives the probability of each pair of values. Everything else is read off it:

Marginal of X$p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)$ — sum the row: "forget Y"
Marginal of Y$p_Y(y)=\sum_x p(x,y)$ — sum the column
Conditional$p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=\dfrac{p(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}$ — one column, rescaled to sum to 1
Continuoussame story with a joint density $f(x,y)$ and integrals; $P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f$

Independence

$X, Y$ independent $\iff$ the joint factorises into the marginals, for every pair:

$$p(x,y)=p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)\quad\text{— every cell = row total × column total}$$

One failing cell kills independence. Checking all cells (or spotting one violation) is a standard exam task.

Running example — stock ($X$) and bond ($Y$) yearly moves, in %:

$p(x,y)$$Y=-5$$Y=+5$$p_X$
$X=-10$0.100.200.30
$X=+20$0.350.350.70
$p_Y$0.450.551.00

Reading it: $P(X=20, Y=-5)=0.35$. Marginals in the margins (hence the name). Conditional: $P(X=20\mid Y=-5)=\frac{0.35}{0.45}\approx 0.78$ — higher than the unconditional 0.70, so a falling bond market makes the stock rally more likely here: the two are dependent.

Independence check, cell (−10, −5): row × column $=0.30\times0.45=0.135\ne 0.10$ — not independent, confirmed.

DEF Conditional expectation & the tower rule— ⊡ ✕

$E[X\mid Y=y]$ is the mean of $X$ computed with the conditional distribution — "the best guess of $X$ after seeing $Y=y$". As $y$ varies it defines a random variable $E[X\mid Y]$.

Law of iterated expectations (tower rule)

$$E\big[\,E[X\mid Y]\,\big]=E[X]$$

In words: average the scenario-averages, weighted by scenario probabilities, and you get the overall average. It is the expectation version of the law of total probability, and the standard trick for computing a mean in two stages ("first condition on the first draw…").

Example (table above): $E[X\mid Y=-5]=\frac{0.10(-10)+0.35(20)}{0.45}\approx 13.33$; $E[X\mid Y=5]=\frac{0.20(-10)+0.35(20)}{0.55}\approx 9.09$. Tower check: $0.45(13.33)+0.55(9.09)=11 = E[X]$ ✓ (indeed $E[X]=0.3(-10)+0.7(20)=11$).

Law of total variance

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\underbrace{E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)\big]}_{\text{within-scenario}}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\big(E[X\mid Y]\big)}_{\text{between-scenario}}$$

Total risk = average risk inside each scenario + risk from not knowing the scenario. Both pieces are ≥ 0, so conditioning never increases expected variance — information helps.

Finance
Decompose return risk into "risk given the macro regime" plus "regime risk". Fund-of-funds, credit models, and mixture models (e.g. returns with random volatility) all run on this identity — it is also how the variance of a random sum $S=X_1+\dots+X_N$ (N random, e.g. number of claims) is computed: $\operatorname{Var}(S)=E[N]\sigma^2+\operatorname{Var}(N)\mu^2$.
DEF Covariance & correlation — do they move together?— ⊡ ✕
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]$$

Positive: above-average $X$ tends to come with above-average $Y$. Negative: they move oppositely. The computational form $E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y$ is the one to use.

Rules (bilinearity — covariance behaves like a product)

Self$\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)=\operatorname{Var}(X)$
Symmetry$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X)$
Scaling$\operatorname{Cov}(aX+b,\,cY+d)=ac\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$ — constants b, d vanish
Additivity$\operatorname{Cov}(X+Z,\,Y)=\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)$
Independence ⇒$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ (because $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$)

Correlation — covariance made unit-free

$$\rho_{XY}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y},\qquad -1\le\rho\le 1$$

$\rho=\pm1$ iff $Y$ is an exact linear function of $X$ (slope sign = sign of ρ). ρ measures linear association only.

Trap
Uncorrelated ≠ independent. Take $X\in\{-1,0,1\}$ equally likely, $Y=X^2$: totally dependent (Y is a function of X), yet $\operatorname{Cov}=E[X^3]-E[X]E[X^2]=0$. Correlation is blind to nonlinear dependence. Exception: for jointly normal (X, Y), uncorrelated ⇔ independent.
THM Variance of a sum — the portfolio formula— ⊡ ✕
$$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\sigma_X^2+b^2\sigma_Y^2+2ab\,\rho\,\sigma_X\sigma_Y$$

The cross term is the whole story: positive covariance amplifies risk, negative covariance cancels it. With weights $w$ and $1-w$ on two assets this is the two-asset portfolio variance — computed end-to-end in module 10.

General case, $n$ variables:

$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_i w_iX_i\Big)=\sum_i w_i^2\sigma_i^2+2\sum_{i\lt j}w_iw_j\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\,\mathbf w$$

$\Sigma$ is the covariance matrix — symmetric, positive semidefinite. $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ is exactly the quadratic form of Linear Algebra module 8; that is why it is never negative.

Also needed: expectation of products & sums

Always$E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]$
Independent only$E[XY]=E[X]\,E[Y]$
General$E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]+\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$

Bivariate normal — a preview of regression

If $(X,Y)$ is jointly normal with correlation ρ, the conditional distribution is again normal with a linear mean:

$$E[Y\mid X=x]=\mu_Y+\rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X)$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x)=\sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2)$$

The slope $\rho\sigma_Y/\sigma_X$ is the regression beta; $1-\rho^2$ is the unexplained share — the $R^2$ story of the statistics course, one year early.

EX Full joint-table workout (the exam classic)— ⊡ ✕

Using the stock/bond table above: compute Cov(X, Y), ρ, and the variance of the equally-weighted portfolio $P=\tfrac12X+\tfrac12Y$.

  1. Means: $E[X]=11$ (found earlier); $E[Y]=0.45(-5)+0.55(5)=0.5$.
  2. $E[XY]=\!\sum xy\,p(x,y)\!=(-10)(-5)(0.10)+(-10)(5)(0.20)+(20)(-5)(0.35)+(20)(5)(0.35)=5-10-35+35=-5$.
  3. $\operatorname{Cov}=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]=-5-11(0.5)=-10.5$ — negative: mild hedge.
  4. Variances: $E[X^2]=0.3(100)+0.7(400)=310\Rightarrow\sigma_X^2=310-121=189$. $E[Y^2]=25\Rightarrow\sigma_Y^2=25-0.25=24.75$.
  5. $\rho=\dfrac{-10.5}{\sqrt{189}\sqrt{24.75}}\approx\dfrac{-10.5}{68.4}\approx -0.15$.
  6. $\operatorname{Var}(P)=\tfrac14(189)+\tfrac14(24.75)+2\cdot\tfrac14(-10.5)=47.25+6.19-5.25=\mathbf{48.2}$ — less than half the stock's variance 189: diversification, with the negative covariance doing extra work.
Insight
The complete chain — joint table → marginals → E[XY] → covariance → correlation → portfolio variance — is probably the single most-examined multi-step computation in finance-flavoured probability. Practise it until it is mechanical.
