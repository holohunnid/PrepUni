W01●countingPIN codes and repeated digits
A 4-digit PIN is chosen at random (each digit 0–9). Find the probability that (a) all digits are different, (b) at least one digit repeats.
Solution
  1. All PINs: $10^4=10000$ (multiplication rule; digits can repeat, order matters).
  2. (a) All different: $10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7=5040$ (each digit must avoid the previous ones). $P=\dfrac{5040}{10000}=0.504$.
  3. (b) "At least one repeat" is the complement of "all different": $P=1-0.504=0.496$.
Insight
Two counting modes in one problem: free choice ($10^4$) vs no-repetition ($10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7$), and the complement disposing of "at least one" instantly. Almost half of random PINs contain a repeat — coincidences are common.
W02●●countingExactly one pair in a poker hand
Five cards are dealt from a shuffled 52-card deck. Find the probability of exactly one pair (two cards of one rank, the other three of three different other ranks).
Solution
  1. All hands: $\binom{52}{5}=2{,}598{,}960$ — unordered, no replacement.
  2. Build a pair hand by choices: rank of the pair ($13$), its two suits ($\binom42=6$), the three other ranks ($\binom{12}{3}=220$ — chosen together to avoid ordering them), one suit each ($4^3=64$).
  3. Favourable: $13\cdot6\cdot220\cdot64=1{,}098{,}240$.
  4. $P=\dfrac{1{,}098{,}240}{2{,}598{,}960}\approx \mathbf{0.4226}$.
Insight
Structure the count as a sequence of independent decisions and multiply. The classic error is $12\cdot11\cdot10$ instead of $\binom{12}{3}$ for the side ranks — that would count each hand $3!$ times. Decide once: is this stage ordered or not?
W03●●countingShared birth months
Five people are in a seminar. Assuming all 12 birth months are equally likely and independent, find the probability that at least two share a birth month.
Solution
  1. Complement: all five months different.
  2. $P(\text{all different})=\dfrac{12\cdot11\cdot10\cdot9\cdot8}{12^5}=\dfrac{95040}{248832}\approx 0.382$.
  3. $P(\text{at least one shared})\approx 1-0.382=\mathbf{0.618}$.
Insight
The birthday problem in miniature: $\binom52=10$ pairs each get a $1/12$ shot at colliding, so a collision is more likely than not. Whenever "at least two share…", go straight to the all-different complement.
W04●conditionalSecond card is an ace
Two cards are drawn without replacement. Show, using the law of total probability, that P(second card is an ace) = 4/52 — the same as for the first card.
Solution
  1. Partition on the first card: $A_1$ = "first is an ace", $P(A_1)=\frac{4}{52}$.
  2. $P(A_2)=P(A_2\mid A_1)P(A_1)+P(A_2\mid A_1^c)P(A_1^c)=\frac{3}{51}\cdot\frac{4}{52}+\frac{4}{51}\cdot\frac{48}{52}$.
  3. $=\frac{12+192}{51\cdot52}=\frac{204}{2652}=\frac{4}{52}=\mathbf{\tfrac1{13}}$.
Insight
By symmetry, every position in a shuffled deck is equally likely to hold an ace — conditioning is only needed if you learn what the first card was. Distinguish "unknown" (symmetry applies) from "known" (condition on it).
W05●●bayesWhich urn did the ball come from?
Urn I holds 3 red and 2 blue balls; urn II holds 1 red and 4 blue. A fair coin picks the urn, then one ball is drawn. It is red. Find P(urn I | red).
Solution
  1. Priors: $P(I)=P(II)=\tfrac12$. Likelihoods: $P(R\mid I)=\tfrac35$, $P(R\mid II)=\tfrac15$.
  2. Total probability: $P(R)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac35+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac15=\tfrac25$.
  3. Bayes: $P(I\mid R)=\dfrac{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac35}{\tfrac25}=\mathbf{\tfrac34}$.
Insight
The posterior odds are the ratio of the paths: $\tfrac{3}{10}$ vs $\tfrac{1}{10}$, i.e. 3:1 → probability $\tfrac34$. Comparing path weights is faster than evaluating the full formula and immune to denominator slips.
W06●●●bayesTwo positive tests in a row
A disease has prevalence 1%. A test has sensitivity 95% and false-positive rate 10%, and repeated tests are independent given the true status. A person tests positive twice. Find the probability they have the disease after the first positive, and after the second.
Solution
  1. First test. Paths to a positive: $0.01\times0.95=0.0095$ (sick) vs $0.99\times0.10=0.099$ (healthy). Posterior $=\frac{0.0095}{0.0095+0.099}\approx \mathbf{0.088}$ — still probably healthy!
  2. Second test: rerun Bayes with the posterior as the new prior, $p=0.088$.
  3. Paths: $0.088\times0.95=0.0836$ vs $0.912\times0.10=0.0912$. New posterior $=\frac{0.0836}{0.0836+0.0912}\approx \mathbf{0.478}$.
  4. Two positives move 1% → 8.8% → 47.8%: each test multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio $0.95/0.10=9.5$. Check via odds: $\frac{1}{99}\times9.5^2\approx0.912$ odds → $0.477$ ✓.
Insight
Bayesian updating chains: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior. In odds form each independent piece of evidence just multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio — the cleanest way to handle repeated evidence, and the reason a single noisy signal (one flag, one backtest) should move your beliefs only modestly.
W07●●independencePairwise but not mutually independent
Flip two fair coins. Let A = "first is heads", B = "second is heads", C = "exactly one head". Show that A, B, C are pairwise independent but not mutually independent.
Solution
  1. Sample space: HH, HT, TH, TT, each $\tfrac14$. $P(A)=P(B)=\tfrac12$; $C=\{HT,TH\}$ so $P(C)=\tfrac12$.
  2. Pairs: $P(A\cap B)=P(HH)=\tfrac14=P(A)P(B)$ ✓. $P(A\cap C)=P(HT)=\tfrac14=P(A)P(C)$ ✓. $P(B\cap C)=P(TH)=\tfrac14$ ✓. All pairs pass.
  3. Triple: $A\cap B\cap C$ = "both heads and exactly one head" $=\varnothing$, so $P=0\ne P(A)P(B)P(C)=\tfrac18$. Not mutually independent.
Insight
Knowing any one of A, B tells you nothing about C — but knowing both determines C completely. Pairwise checks miss joint information; that is exactly why the mutual-independence definition demands the triple product too.
W08●conditionalConditioning shrinks the dice table
Two fair dice are rolled and you are told the sum is 8. What is the probability at least one die shows a 6?
Solution
  1. New (conditional) sample space: outcomes with sum 8: $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)$ — five outcomes, still equally likely.
  2. Favourable among them: $(2,6),(6,2)$ — two.
  3. $P=\mathbf{\tfrac25}$.
Insight
For equally likely outcomes, conditioning = "cross out the excluded rows and recount". No formula needed — the definition $P(A\mid B)=\frac{\#(A\cap B)}{\#B}$ does everything. Note $(4,4)$ appears once, not twice: ordered pairs, always.
W09●rvBuild a discrete distribution from scratch
A random variable has $P(X=k)=ck$ for $k=1,2,3,4$. Find c, $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $P(X\ge 3)$.
Solution
  1. Normalise: $c(1+2+3+4)=10c=1\Rightarrow c=\tfrac1{10}$.
  2. $E[X]=\tfrac{1}{10}(1\cdot1+2\cdot2+3\cdot3+4\cdot4)=\tfrac{30}{10}=3$.
  3. $E[X^2]=\tfrac1{10}(1+8+27+64)=10$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X)=10-3^2=\mathbf{1}$.
  4. $P(X\ge3)=p(3)+p(4)=\tfrac{3}{10}+\tfrac4{10}=\mathbf{0.7}$.
Insight
The invariable four-step drill: normalise, mean, $E[X^2]$ then variance by the computational formula, tail by summing. Do it mechanically and bank the marks.
W10●●expectationThe hat-check problem — linearity beats dependence
n guests leave their hats; the hats are returned uniformly at random, one each. Find the expected number of guests who get their own hat back.
Solution
  1. Let $X_i=\mathbf 1\{\text{guest } i \text{ gets own hat}\}$; the count is $X=X_1+\dots+X_n$.
  2. Each hat is equally likely to land anywhere: $E[X_i]=P(\text{match})=\tfrac1n$.
  3. Linearity (dependence irrelevant): $E[X]=n\cdot\tfrac1n=\mathbf{1}$ — one match on average, whatever n is.
Insight
The matches are heavily dependent (if $n-1$ guests get their own hats, so does the last) — and linearity does not care. Decompose counts into indicators, sum the probabilities, done. Direct computation of this distribution is a famous hard problem; the mean is one line.
W11●continuousDensity workout with a symmetric pdf
$f(x)=c(1-x^2)$ on $[-1,1]$, zero elsewhere. Find c, $E[X]$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $P(X\gt\tfrac12)$.
Solution
  1. $\int_{-1}^{1}c(1-x^2)dx=c\big(2-\tfrac23\big)=\tfrac{4c}{3}=1\Rightarrow c=\tfrac34$.
  2. $E[X]=0$ — the density is symmetric about 0; no integration needed.
  3. $\operatorname{Var}=E[X^2]=\tfrac34\int_{-1}^1 x^2(1-x^2)dx=\tfrac34\big(\tfrac23-\tfrac25\big)=\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac{4}{15}=\mathbf{\tfrac15}$.
  4. $P(X\gt\tfrac12)=\tfrac34\int_{1/2}^{1}(1-x^2)dx=\tfrac34\Big[x-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\Big]_{1/2}^{1}=\tfrac34\big(\tfrac23-\tfrac{11}{24}\big)=\mathbf{\tfrac{5}{32}}\approx0.156$.
Insight
Exploit symmetry before integrating: symmetric density ⇒ mean 0 and $\operatorname{Var}=E[X^2]$. Half the arithmetic disappears.
W12●●continuousMemoryless servers and the first failure
A server's lifetime is exponential with mean 5 years. (a) It has already run 3 years; find P(it survives 5 more). (b) Two such servers run independently; find the distribution of the time to the first failure and P(no failure in 5 years).
Solution
  1. Mean 5 ⇒ rate $\lambda=0.2$. (a) Memoryless: the 3 years already served are irrelevant: $P(T\gt3+5\mid T\gt3)=P(T\gt5)=e^{-0.2\cdot5}=e^{-1}\approx\mathbf{0.368}$.
  2. (b) $\min(T_1,T_2)\gt t$ iff both survive: $P=(e^{-0.2t})^2=e^{-0.4t}$ — so the first failure is $\text{Exp}(0.4)$, mean $2.5$ years.
  3. $P(\text{no failure in 5y})=e^{-0.4\cdot5}=e^{-2}\approx\mathbf{0.135}$.
Insight
Two exponential signatures: conditioning resets the clock (memoryless), and minima of independent exponentials are exponential with summed rates. "Time to first default/failure/arrival among n" questions are all this pattern.
W13●●mgfIdentify the distribution from its MGF
A random variable has $M_X(t)=\big(0.3+0.7e^{t}\big)^{10}$. Identify its distribution and state $E[X]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)$. Then verify the mean via $M'(0)$.
Solution
  1. Recall Bernoulli(p): $M(t)=1-p+pe^t$. A product of 10 identical such factors is the MGF of a sum of 10 independent Bernoulli(0.7).
  2. By uniqueness of MGFs: $X\sim\text{Binomial}(10,\,0.7)$. Hence $E[X]=7$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=10(0.7)(0.3)=\mathbf{2.1}$.
  3. Check: $M'(t)=10(0.3+0.7e^t)^9\cdot0.7e^t$, so $M'(0)=10\cdot1\cdot0.7=7$ ✓.
Insight
MGF questions are recognition questions: factor the expression into known MGFs. A power of an MGF ⇔ a sum of iid copies — that single observation identifies the distribution with no computation.
W14●discreteGuessing on a multiple-choice test
A test has 8 questions with 4 options each; a student guesses everything. Find the probability of (a) exactly 2 correct, (b) at least 2 correct.
Solution
  1. Story: 8 independent trials, success $p=\tfrac14$ → $X\sim\text{Bin}(8, 0.25)$.
  2. (a) $P(X=2)=\binom82(0.25)^2(0.75)^6=28\cdot0.0625\cdot0.1780\approx\mathbf{0.311}$.
  3. (b) Complement of {0, 1}: $P(X=0)=(0.75)^8\approx0.1001$; $P(X=1)=8(0.25)(0.75)^7\approx0.2670$.
  4. $P(X\ge2)=1-0.1001-0.2670\approx\mathbf{0.633}$.
Insight
"At least k" for small k: complement over the few small cases. Never sum the long side. And always name the distribution and its parameters before computing — it earns method marks even if arithmetic slips.
W15●●discretePoisson approximation for rare defaults
A portfolio holds 500 independent loans, each defaulting within the year with probability 0.004. Approximate the probability of (a) no defaults, (b) at least 3 defaults.
Solution
  1. Bin(500, 0.004) with n large, p small → Poisson with $\lambda=np=2$.
  2. (a) $P(X=0)=e^{-2}\approx\mathbf{0.135}$.
  3. (b) $P(X\ge3)=1-e^{-2}\big(1+2+\tfrac{2^2}{2}\big)=1-5e^{-2}\approx\mathbf{0.323}$.
  4. (Exact binomial: 0.1341 and 0.3229 — the approximation is excellent.)
Insight
Rare events in a big book: only $\lambda=np$ matters, not n and p separately. A 1000-loan book at 0.2% behaves identically. This collapse of two parameters into one is why Poisson is the universal model for counts of rare events.
W16●discreteWaiting for the first success
Each cold call converts with probability 0.2, independently. Find (a) P(first sale on the 4th call), (b) the expected number of calls until the first sale, (c) P(more than 6 calls needed).
Solution
  1. Geometric(0.2), counting trials. (a) Three failures then a success: $(0.8)^3(0.2)=\mathbf{0.1024}$.
  2. (b) $E[X]=1/p=\mathbf{5}$ calls.
  3. (c) "More than 6" = first six all fail: $(0.8)^6\approx\mathbf{0.262}$.
Insight
The tail shortcut $P(X\gt k)=(1-p)^k$ needs no summation — "more than k" just means k straight failures. Also note the convention: counting trials (mean $1/p$), not failures.
W17●●discreteQuality control without replacement
A lot of 20 items contains 4 defective. An inspector samples 5 items without replacement. Find (a) P(exactly 1 defective in the sample), (b) the expected number of defectives, (c) why Binomial(5, 0.2) is the wrong model here.
Solution
  1. Hypergeometric: N = 20, K = 4, n = 5.
  2. (a) $P(X=1)=\dfrac{\binom41\binom{16}{4}}{\binom{20}{5}}=\dfrac{4\cdot1820}{15504}\approx\mathbf{0.469}$.
  3. (b) $E[X]=n\frac KN=5\cdot\frac{4}{20}=\mathbf{1}$ — same as the binomial mean.
  4. (c) Draws are dependent: each defective removed lowers the defect rate for later draws. The binomial assumes a constant p; here the variance carries the finite-population correction $\frac{N-n}{N-1}=\frac{15}{19}$, so the true spread is smaller. (Binomial(5,0.2) gives $P(X=1)\approx0.410$ — noticeably off since n/N = 25%.)
Insight
The replacement question decides the model. Sampling a small fraction of a huge population → binomial is fine; sampling 25% of a lot of 20 → it is not. The mean never notices; the variance and the probabilities do.
W18●normalForward and backward through the z-table
Scores are $N(100, 15^2)$. Find (a) P(X > 130), (b) P(85 < X < 115), (c) the score that only 10% of the population exceeds.
Solution
  1. (a) $z=\frac{130-100}{15}=2$: $P=1-\Phi(2)=1-0.9772=\mathbf{0.0228}$.
  2. (b) The interval is $\mu\pm1\sigma$: $P=2\Phi(1)-1=\mathbf{0.6826}$ — quoted from the 68/95/99.7 anchors, no table needed.
  3. (c) Backward: $\Phi(z)=0.90\Rightarrow z=1.282$. $x=\mu+z\sigma=100+1.282\cdot15\approx\mathbf{119.2}$.
Insight
Sketch the curve and shade what you want before touching the table — it prevents every "wrong tail" error. Forward: standardise. Backward: find z, then un-standardise with $x=\mu+z\sigma$.
W19●●●transformDeriving χ²₁ from a standard normal
Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $Y=Z^2$. Derive the density of Y.
Solution
  1. $g(z)=z^2$ is not monotone → CDF method, collecting both roots. For $y\gt0$: $$F_Y(y)=P(Z^2\le y)=P(-\sqrt y\le Z\le\sqrt y)=\Phi(\sqrt y)-\Phi(-\sqrt y)=2\Phi(\sqrt y)-1.$$
  2. Differentiate (chain rule): $f_Y(y)=2\varphi(\sqrt y)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}=\frac{\varphi(\sqrt y)}{\sqrt y}$.
  3. Substitute $\varphi(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u^2/2}$: $$f_Y(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}\,e^{-y/2},\qquad y\gt0.$$
  4. This is Gamma$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ — the $\chi^2_1$ density. (Sanity: support positive ✓, integrates to 1 ✓, $E[Y]=E[Z^2]=1$ ✓.)
Insight
Non-monotone transformations force the CDF route, and the two roots produce the factor 2 that beginners lose. The check $E[Y]=\operatorname{Var}(Z)=1$ costs five seconds and catches most slips.
W20●●financeLognormal stock price
A stock trades at $S_0=50$; its one-year log return is $N(0.06,\ 0.2^2)$. Find (a) P(S₁ > 60), (b) the median and mean of S₁, and explain why they differ.
Solution
  1. (a) $P(S_1\gt60)=P\big(\ln(S_1/S_0)\gt\ln1.2\big)=P\Big(Z\gt\frac{0.1823-0.06}{0.2}\Big)=1-\Phi(0.61)\approx\mathbf{0.271}$.
  2. (b) Median: $50e^{0.06}\approx\mathbf{53.09}$ (the middle log return maps to the middle price — monotone transformations preserve medians).
  3. Mean: $E[S_1]=50e^{0.06+0.2^2/2}=50e^{0.08}\approx\mathbf{54.16}$.
  4. Mean > median because the lognormal is right-skewed: rare huge gains pull the average up while the typical outcome stays lower. The gap is exactly Jensen's $e^{\sigma^2/2}$ factor.
Insight
Monotone transforms preserve quantiles but not means. "Expected price" and "typical price" are different claims about a skewed asset — precision about which one you mean is exactly what the σ²/2 term encodes.
W21●continuousUniform waiting for a bus
A bus arrives at a uniform random time in the next 30 minutes. Find (a) P(you wait more than 20 min), (b) P(you wait more than 20 min given you have already waited 10), (c) expected wait.
Solution
  1. $W\sim U(0,30)$: probabilities are length fractions. (a) $P(W\gt20)=\frac{10}{30}=\mathbf{\tfrac13}$.
  2. (b) $P(W\gt20\mid W\gt10)=\frac{P(W\gt20)}{P(W\gt10)}=\frac{10/30}{20/30}=\mathbf{\tfrac12}$ — bigger than ⅓: unlike the exponential, the uniform remembers; time already waited squeezes the remaining window.
  3. (c) $E[W]=\frac{0+30}{2}=\mathbf{15}$ min.
Insight
Put this next to W12: uniform waits age (the deadline approaches), exponential waits don't (memoryless). Conditioning is just renormalising the remaining interval — no integrals required for uniforms.
W22●●jointJoint table: the full chain
$X\in\{0,1\}$, $Y\in\{0,1,2\}$ with joint probabilities: $p(0,0)=0.10$, $p(0,1)=0.20$, $p(0,2)=0.10$, $p(1,0)=0.15$, $p(1,1)=0.25$, $p(1,2)=0.20$. Find the marginals, show X and Y are dependent, and compute Cov(X, Y) and ρ.
Solution
  1. Marginals: $p_X(0)=0.40$, $p_X(1)=0.60$; $p_Y=(0.25,\ 0.45,\ 0.30)$.
  2. Dependence: test a cell. $p(0,1)=0.20$ but $p_X(0)p_Y(1)=0.40\times0.45=0.18\ne0.20$ — dependent. (Beware: cell (0,0) happens to satisfy $0.40\times0.25=0.10$ — one matching cell proves nothing; one failing cell settles it.)
  3. Means: $E[X]=0.6$; $E[Y]=0.45+2(0.30)=1.05$.
  4. $E[XY]$: only cells with $x=1,y\ge1$ contribute: $1\cdot1\cdot0.25+1\cdot2\cdot0.20=0.65$. $\operatorname{Cov}=0.65-0.6\times1.05=\mathbf{0.02}$.
  5. $\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.6-0.36=0.24$; $E[Y^2]=0.45+4(0.30)=1.65$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=1.65-1.1025=0.5475$. $\rho=\frac{0.02}{\sqrt{0.24\times0.5475}}\approx\mathbf{0.055}$.
Insight
Weak correlation, clear dependence — ρ near 0 does not mean "unrelated", it means "not much linear relation". And in independence checks, a single counterexample cell is a complete proof.
W23●●jointSum and difference of two dice — uncorrelated yet dependent
Roll a die twice; let $S=X+Y$ and $D=X-Y$. Show Cov(S, D) = 0, and show S and D are nevertheless not independent.
Solution
  1. Bilinearity: $\operatorname{Cov}(X+Y,\,X-Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)-\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)+\operatorname{Cov}(Y,X)-\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)-\operatorname{Var}(Y)$.
  2. The two rolls are identically distributed, so $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(S,D)=\mathbf{0}$: uncorrelated.
  3. Independent? $P(S=12)=\tfrac1{36}$ and $P(D=0)=\tfrac{6}{36}$, but $P(S=12,\ D=0)=P(\text{double six})=\tfrac1{36}\ne\tfrac1{36}\cdot\tfrac16$. Dependent — knowing $S=12$ forces $D=0$.
Insight
A structural zero: sum and difference of any two iid variables are uncorrelated, by pure algebra. Yet they share information. Zero covariance kills the linear link only — the counterexample to "uncorrelated ⇒ independent" you should carry into any exam.
W24●●●jointA random number of claims — tower rule at work
An insurer receives $N\sim\text{Poisson}(20)$ claims per year; claim sizes are iid with mean 500 and SD 300, independent of N. Find the mean and SD of the total annual payout $T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_i$.
Solution
  1. Condition on N. Given $N=n$: $E[T\mid N=n]=500n$, $\operatorname{Var}(T\mid N=n)=300^2 n$.
  2. Tower rule: $E[T]=E\big[E[T\mid N]\big]=E[500N]=500\times20=\mathbf{10{,}000}$.
  3. Law of total variance: $\operatorname{Var}(T)=E[\operatorname{Var}(T\mid N)]+\operatorname{Var}(E[T\mid N])=E[300^2N]+\operatorname{Var}(500N)$.
  4. $=90{,}000\times20+250{,}000\times20=1.8\text{M}+5.0\text{M}=6.8\text{M}$, so $\sigma_T=\sqrt{6.8\times10^6}\approx\mathbf{2{,}608}$.
Insight
Random sums decompose cleanly: mean = (mean count)(mean size); variance = within-scenario part + between-scenario part. Note the second term: even if every claim were exactly 500 (σ = 0), the payout would still be risky because N is. Uncertainty about "how many" is risk too.
W25●●portfolioMinimum-variance portfolio, end to end
Asset A: σ = 20%; asset B: σ = 30%; correlation ρ = 0.2. Find the weight on A that minimises portfolio variance, and the resulting portfolio volatility. Why is it below 20%?
Solution
  1. $\sigma_P^2(w)=w^2(0.04)+(1-w)^2(0.09)+2w(1-w)(0.2)(0.2)(0.3)$, cross term coefficient $\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B=0.012$.
  2. Minimise (set derivative to 0, or quote the formula): $w^*=\dfrac{\sigma_B^2-\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2-2\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B}=\dfrac{0.09-0.012}{0.04+0.09-0.024}=\dfrac{0.078}{0.106}\approx\mathbf{0.736}$.
  3. Plug back: $\sigma_P^2=0.736^2(0.04)+0.264^2(0.09)+2(0.736)(0.264)(0.012)\approx0.0217+0.0063+0.0047=0.0326$.
  4. $\sigma_P\approx\mathbf{18.1\%}$ — below even the safer asset's 20%.
Insight
Adding a riskier asset lowered total risk: with ρ well below 1, B's fluctuations partially offset A's. Diversification is a property of covariances, not of individual volatilities — the deepest one-liner in portfolio theory.
W26●cltCLT for a sum of uniforms
$X_1,\dots,X_{100}$ iid $U(0,1)$. Approximate $P\big(\sum X_i\gt55\big)$.
Solution
  1. Per draw: $\mu=\tfrac12$, $\sigma^2=\tfrac1{12}$.
  2. Sum: $S\approx N\big(100\times\tfrac12,\ 100\times\tfrac1{12}\big)=N(50,\ 8.33)$, $\sigma_S\approx2.887$.
  3. $P(S\gt55)=1-\Phi\big(\tfrac{55-50}{2.887}\big)=1-\Phi(1.73)\approx\mathbf{0.042}$.
Insight
The flat uniform is nothing like a bell curve — after 100 additions that is irrelevant. Only μ and σ² enter. Recognising "sum of many iid → normal, parameters $(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$" is the entire skill.
W27●●cltChebyshev vs CLT — guarantee vs estimate
Daily demand is iid with mean 50 and SD 10; consider the average over 100 days. Bound $P(|\bar X-50|\ge2)$ with Chebyshev, then approximate it with the CLT. Compare.
Solution
  1. $\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\tfrac{10^2}{100}=1$, so $\sigma_{\bar X}=1$.
  2. Chebyshev with $k=2$: $P(|\bar X-50|\ge2)\le\tfrac{1}{4}=\mathbf{0.25}$ — valid for any distribution.
  3. CLT: $\bar X\approx N(50,1)$, so $P=2\big(1-\Phi(2)\big)=2(0.0228)=\mathbf{0.046}$.
  4. Chebyshev's 25% vs CLT's 4.6%: the bound is loose but assumption-free; the CLT figure is sharp but needs n large enough for normality of the average.
Insight
Know which tool a question wants: "bound / at most, no distribution given" → Chebyshev; "approximate, n large" → CLT. They answer different questions — worst case vs typical case.
W28●●financeVaR and a one-step risk-neutral price
(a) A €5m portfolio has daily returns $N(0,\ 0.015^2)$. Compute the 99% one-day VaR and scale it to 10 days. (b) A stock at 100 moves to 110 or 95 in one period; the risk-free rate is 1%. Price a call with strike 100.
Solution
  1. (a) $\text{VaR}_{99\%}=5\text{m}\times2.326\times0.015\approx\mathbf{€174{,}500}$ (μ = 0 so no drift adjustment).
  2. 10-day horizon (iid): σ scales with √10: $\text{VaR}_{10d}\approx174{,}500\times3.162\approx\mathbf{€551{,}800}$.
  3. (b) $u=1.10$, $d=0.95$: $q=\dfrac{1.01-0.95}{1.10-0.95}=\dfrac{0.06}{0.15}=0.4$.
  4. Payoffs: 10 (up), 0 (down). Price $=\dfrac{0.4\times10}{1.01}\approx\mathbf{3.96}$.
Insight
Both halves are pure probability wearing finance clothes: VaR is a normal quantile times a √T scaling (modules 6 & 10); the option price is an expectation under an adjusted probability measure (modules 4 & 10). The mathematics never got harder — only the vocabulary changed.
