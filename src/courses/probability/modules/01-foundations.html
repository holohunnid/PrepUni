Foundations & counting

the language: outcomes, events, and how to count them
DEF Experiment, sample space, event — the three words everything is built on— ⊡ ✕

A random experiment is any procedure whose outcome you cannot predict in advance: flipping a coin, rolling a die, observing tomorrow's stock return. Probability theory does not predict the outcome — it assigns a number between 0 and 1 to each possible outcome, measuring how likely it is.

Sample space $\Omega$the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment
Outcome $\omega$one single element of $\Omega$ — exactly one occurs when you run the experiment
Event $A$any subset of $\Omega$ — a collection of outcomes. "$A$ occurs" means the realised outcome is in $A$
Probability $P(A)$a number in $[0,1]$ attached to each event; $P(A)=0.3$ means: repeat the experiment very many times and $A$ happens about 30% of the time
ExperimentSample space $\Omega$Example of an event
one coin flip$\{H, T\}$$A=\{H\}$ "heads"
one die roll$\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$$A=\{2,4,6\}$ "even number"
two dice36 ordered pairs $(i,j)$$A=\{(1,6),(2,5),\dots\}$ "sum is 7"
tomorrow's return$\mathbb{R}$ (a continuum)$A=\{r : r \lt -0.02\}$ "loss worse than 2%"

The first three sample spaces are finite (you can list the outcomes); the last is continuous. Modules 1–2 work with finite spaces; module 3 introduces the machinery for continuous ones.

DEF Combining events — the set-operation dictionary— ⊡ ✕

Events are sets, so they combine with set operations. Read each symbol as the English word next to it — every probability statement you will ever meet is built from these three.

Union $A\cup B$"$A$ or $B$ (or both)" — at least one of the two occurs
Intersection $A\cap B$"$A$ and $B$" — both occur. Also written $AB$
Complement $A^{c}$"not $A$" — $A$ does not occur. Also written $\bar A$
Difference $A\setminus B$"$A$ but not $B$" $= A\cap B^{c}$
Disjoint (mutually exclusive)$A\cap B=\varnothing$ — they cannot both happen (e.g. a die shows 1 and 6)
De Morgan's laws$(A\cup B)^{c}=A^{c}\cap B^{c}$, $\qquad (A\cap B)^{c}=A^{c}\cup B^{c}$
Tip
De Morgan in words: "not (at least one)" = "none", and "not (both)" = "at least one fails". This is the engine behind the "at least one" trick below — the complement of "at least one 6 in four rolls" is "no 6 in any of the four rolls".

Thinking of probability as area makes the addition rule below obvious: adding the area of $A$ and the area of $B$ counts the overlap $A\cap B$ twice, so you subtract it once.

Try "exactly one": that region is $(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)$ — it excludes the overlap. Exams like asking for it because it forces you to distinguish "or" (which includes both) from "exactly one".

DEF The three axioms — all of probability from three lines— ⊡ ✕

Every probability rule is a consequence of these three axioms (Kolmogorov, 1933):

1 · Non-negativity$P(A)\ge 0$ for every event $A$
2 · Normalisation$P(\Omega)=1$ — something must happen
3 · Additivityif $A_1, A_2, \dots$ are pairwise disjoint, $P\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big)=\sum_i P(A_i)$

Axiom 3 in words: if events cannot overlap, probabilities simply add. That is the only "hard" axiom, and it is exactly how areas behave.

Consequences you use constantly

Complement rule$P(A^{c})=1-P(A)$
Empty set$P(\varnothing)=0$
Monotonicity$A\subseteq B \Rightarrow P(A)\le P(B)$
Addition rule$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$
Three events$P(A\cup B\cup C)=\sum P(\text{singles})-\sum P(\text{pairs})+P(A\cap B\cap C)$
Union bound$P(A\cup B)\le P(A)+P(B)$ (equality iff disjoint)
DEF Classical probability — when all outcomes are equally likely— ⊡ ✕

If $\Omega$ has $N$ outcomes and symmetry makes them all equally likely (fair coin, fair die, well-shuffled deck), then probability is just counting:

$$P(A)=\frac{\#A}{\#\Omega}=\frac{\text{favourable outcomes}}{\text{possible outcomes}}$$

This is why the counting toolkit below matters: most exam questions on finite spaces reduce to "count the favourable cases, count all cases, divide".

Trap
The formula requires equally likely outcomes. "The market goes up or down, so P(up) = 1/2" is wrong — those two outcomes are not symmetric. Always ask: is there a physical symmetry (fair die, shuffled deck) that justifies equal likelihood?
Trap
Count outcomes consistently: with two dice, use all 36 ordered pairs. The sums 2–12 are NOT equally likely (sum 7 has 6 ways, sum 2 has 1 way). Working with unordered or aggregated outcomes and treating them as equally likely is the classic error.
EX Warm-up: two dice, three questions— ⊡ ✕

Roll two fair dice. Find (a) P(sum = 7), (b) P(at least one 6), (c) P(sum = 7 or at least one 6).

  1. Sample space: 36 ordered pairs $(i,j)$, all equally likely, each with probability $1/36$.
  2. (a) Sum 7: $(1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1)$ — 6 outcomes. $P=6/36=1/6$.
  3. (b) "At least one 6" is painful to list directly, so use the complement: "no 6 at all" means both dice show 1–5, which is $5\times 5=25$ outcomes. $P(\text{at least one }6)=1-\tfrac{25}{36}=\tfrac{11}{36}$.
  4. (c) Addition rule: $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$. The overlap "sum 7 and at least one 6" is $\{(1,6),(6,1)\}$: $2/36$. So $P=\tfrac{6}{36}+\tfrac{11}{36}-\tfrac{2}{36}=\tfrac{15}{36}=\tfrac{5}{12}$.
Insight
Two reflexes on display: (1) "at least one" → complement, always; (2) unions need the overlap subtracted. Between them these two moves solve half of all elementary probability questions.
DEF Counting toolkit — multiplication rule, permutations, combinations— ⊡ ✕

Multiplication rule

If a procedure has stage 1 with $n_1$ options, stage 2 with $n_2$ options, …, the total number of results is $n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$. (4-digit PIN: $10^4$ possibilities.)

Permutations — order matters

Number of ways to arrange $k$ objects chosen from $n$ distinct objects:

$$P(n,k)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}$$

All $n$ objects in a row: $n!$ ways. Convention: $0! = 1$.

Combinations — order does not matter

Number of ways to choose a group of $k$ from $n$, ignoring order:

$$\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}=\frac{P(n,k)}{k!}$$

Divide by $k!$ because each group of $k$ was counted once per internal ordering.

Useful identities

Symmetry$\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}$ — choosing who is in = choosing who is out
Edge values$\binom{n}{0}=\binom{n}{n}=1$, $\binom{n}{1}=n$
Pascal$\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}$
Binomial theorem$(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^k b^{n-k}$
Total subsets$\sum_k \binom{n}{k}=2^n$

The sampling 2×2 — which formula when

Drawing $k$ from $n$ objects:

order mattersorder doesn't
with replacement$n^k$$\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ (rare in exams)
without replacement$\dfrac{n!}{(n-k)!}$$\dbinom{n}{k}$

First ask: can the same object appear twice? Then: do two draws in different order count as different results?

Tip
Passwords/PINs/sequences → order matters. Committees/hands of cards/portfolios of stocks → order doesn't. If the question says "arrange", "sequence", "ranking" think permutations; "choose", "select", "committee", "hand" think combinations.
EX Committee counting— ⊡ ✕

From 6 women and 4 men, a committee of 3 is drawn at random. Find P(exactly 2 women).

  1. All committees: $\binom{10}{3}=120$, all equally likely (random draw).
  2. Favourable: choose 2 of the 6 women and 1 of the 4 men — multiplication rule across the two independent choices: $\binom{6}{2}\binom{4}{1}=15\cdot4=60$.
  3. $P=\dfrac{60}{120}=\dfrac12$.
Insight
"Exactly $k$ of one type" always factors as $\binom{\text{type 1}}{k}\binom{\text{type 2}}{\text{rest}}$ over $\binom{\text{total}}{\text{committee size}}$. This exact pattern returns as the hypergeometric distribution in module 5.
EX The birthday problem — why intuition fails— ⊡ ✕

23 people in a room. What is the probability at least two share a birthday (365 days, all equally likely)?

  1. "At least one shared birthday" → complement: compute P(all 23 birthdays different).
  2. All assignments: $365^{23}$. All-different assignments: $365\cdot364\cdots343$ (each new person must avoid the previous ones).
  3. $P(\text{all different})=\dfrac{365\cdot364\cdots343}{365^{23}}\approx 0.493$.
  4. $P(\text{shared})\approx 1-0.493=\mathbf{0.507}$ — better than a coin flip with only 23 people.
Insight
Intuition compares 23 to 365; the maths compares $\binom{23}{2}=253$ pairs to 365. Coincidences among many pairs are cheap. In finance the same logic warns you: among many strategies, some will look great purely by chance.
