Discrete distributions

the zoo, part I — recognise the story, not the formula
TIP How to use the zoo— ⊡ ✕

Every named distribution is just a PMF that shows up so often it earned a name. Each one is generated by a story. In an exam, your job is to match the question's story to the right distribution, then read the formulas off the table. The stories:

Key discriminator questions: fixed number of trials (binomial) or trials until something happens (geometric/neg-bin)? With replacement / independent (binomial) or without (hypergeometric)? Counting in a time window with no natural n (Poisson)?

DEF Bernoulli & Binomial — the workhorses— ⊡ ✕

Bernoulli(p) — one trial

$X=1$ ("success") with probability $p$, else $X=0$.

$$E[X]=p,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p)$$

Variance is largest at $p=\tfrac12$ (maximum uncertainty) and zero at $p=0,1$.

Binomial(n, p) — count successes in n independent trials

$$P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{\,n-k},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n$$ $$E[X]=np,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p)$$

Where the formula comes from: one specific sequence with $k$ successes has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ (independence multiplies); there are $\binom{n}{k}$ such sequences (choose which trials succeed); add them up. The mean is instant by linearity: $X$ is a sum of $n$ Bernoullis, so $E[X]=np$.

Finance
50 loans, each defaulting independently with $p=0.02$: number of defaults ~ Binomial(50, 0.02). $E=1$, $\sigma=\sqrt{50(0.02)(0.98)}\approx 0.99$. The binomial stock-price tree of module 10 is the same object: number of "up moves" in $n$ periods.
Trap
Binomial needs all three: fixed n, same p, independent trials. Sampling without replacement breaks independence → hypergeometric. "At least one success" is, as always, $1-P(X=0)=1-(1-p)^n$.
Tip
$P(X=k)$ maximised near $(n+1)p$ (the mode). Successive ratios $\frac{P(k+1)}{P(k)}=\frac{n-k}{k+1}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p}$ let you compute the whole PMF fast from $P(0)=(1-p)^n$.
INTERACTIVE Binomial PMF — and the normal shape hiding inside it— ⊡ ✕

Three things to notice while playing:

  • $p=0.5$ → symmetric; $p$ near 0 or 1 → skewed (long tail away from the boundary).
  • The distribution concentrates: the bulk sits within $\pm 2\sigma=\pm2\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ of $np$.
  • Small $p$, large $n$: bars match a Poisson with $\lambda=np$ — the "law of rare events" below.
DEF Geometric & negative binomial — waiting for success— ⊡ ✕

Geometric(p) — trials until the first success

$$P(X=k)=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,3,\dots$$ $$E[X]=\frac{1}{p},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2},\qquad P(X\gt k)=(1-p)^k$$

Story reading of the mean: success has chance 1-in-$1/p$, so you wait on average $1/p$ trials. The tail formula is just "the first $k$ trials all fail".

Trap — two conventions
Some books count trials ($k=1,2,\dots$, mean $1/p$ — used here), others count failures before the first success ($k=0,1,\dots$, mean $(1-p)/p$). Check which one the question uses before quoting formulas.

Memoryless: $P(X\gt m+k\mid X\gt m)=P(X\gt k)$. Ten failures in a row do not make success "due" — the gambler's fallacy is precisely the refusal to believe this.

Negative binomial(r, p) — trials until the r-th success

$$P(X=k)=\binom{k-1}{r-1}p^r(1-p)^{k-r},\qquad k=r,r+1,\dots$$ $$E[X]=\frac{r}{p},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}$$

Sum of $r$ independent geometrics — hence mean $r/p$. The binomial coefficient places the first $r-1$ successes among the first $k-1$ trials (the $k$-th trial must be the $r$-th success).

DEF Poisson — counting rare events— ⊡ ✕

$X\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)$ counts events in a fixed window when events occur independently at average rate $\lambda$ per window:

$$P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots$$ $$E[X]=\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda$$

Mean = variance = λ — the Poisson signature (and a quick model check on data).

Law of rare events (Poisson approximation)

Binomial(n, p) with large n, small p is ≈ Poisson($\lambda=np$). Rule of thumb: $n\ge 50$, $p\le 0.05$. This is why Poisson models "number of": operational-risk losses per year, market crashes per decade, trades per second, typos per page.

Example. 1000 policyholders, each claims with $p=0.002$: claims ≈ Poisson(2). $P(\text{no claims})=e^{-2}\approx 13.5\%$; $P(\ge 3)=1-e^{-2}(1+2+2)=1-5e^{-2}\approx 32.3\%$.

Scaling: events at rate λ per unit time → count in a window of length $t$ is Poisson($\lambda t$). Sums: independent Poissons add, $X+Y\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)$ (module 8).

DEF Hypergeometric & discrete uniform— ⊡ ✕

Hypergeometric — sampling without replacement

Population of $N$ items, $K$ of them "successes". Draw $n$ without replacement; $X$ = successes drawn:

$$P(X=k)=\frac{\dbinom{K}{k}\dbinom{N-K}{n-k}}{\dbinom{N}{n}},\qquad E[X]=n\frac{K}{N}$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=n\,\frac{K}{N}\Big(1-\frac{K}{N}\Big)\frac{N-n}{N-1}$$

Same mean as a Binomial with $p=K/N$, but variance shrunk by the finite-population correction $\frac{N-n}{N-1}$: each draw depletes the pool, so results are less variable. When $N\gg n$ the correction ≈ 1 and binomial is a fine approximation.

This is module 1's committee counting, upgraded to a distribution.

Discrete uniform on {1,…,N}

$$P(X=k)=\frac1N,\qquad E[X]=\frac{N+1}{2},\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{N^2-1}{12}$$

The die is $N=6$: mean 3.5, variance 35/12 — matching module 4's computation.

EX Choosing the right distribution — one scenario, three questions— ⊡ ✕

A trader's strategy wins on any given day with probability 0.55, days independent. (a) P(exactly 3 wins in 5 days)? (b) Expected number of days until the first losing day? (c) Over 250 trading days, roughly how many "crash alarms" if alarms are rare events at rate 2 per 250 days — P(no alarm all year)?

  1. (a) Fixed n = 5, same p, independent → Binomial(5, 0.55): $P=\binom{5}{3}(0.55)^3(0.45)^2\approx 10(0.166)(0.2025)\approx 0.337$.
  2. (b) "Until the first…" → Geometric with success = losing day, $p=0.45$: $E=1/0.45\approx 2.22$ days.
  3. (c) Rare events in a window → Poisson(2): $P(X=0)=e^{-2}\approx 0.135$.
Insight
Nothing was computed until the story was matched to a distribution. That matching step — n fixed? waiting? rare counts? replacement? — is the actual skill being examined.
