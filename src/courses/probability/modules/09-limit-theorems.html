09

Limit theorems

why averages stabilise and why the bell curve is everywhere
DEF The sample mean and its two vital statistics— ⊡ ✕

Take $X_1,\dots,X_n$ iid (independent, identically distributed) with mean μ and variance σ². Their average is the sample mean:

$$\bar X_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$$

Its mean and variance follow from linearity and independence — memorise both:

$$E[\bar X_n]=\mu\qquad\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

The average is unbiased (centred on the truth) and its spread shrinks like $\sigma/\sqrt n$ — the standard error. Everything in this module unpacks these two facts.

The √n rule
To halve the uncertainty of an average you need 4× the observations; to get one extra decimal digit of accuracy, 100×. Precision is bought at a quadratic price — this governs simulation budgets, polling sample sizes, and why diversification's benefits flatten out.
Finance
Equal-weight portfolio of n uncorrelated assets (each variance σ²): portfolio variance is σ²/n → 0. With correlated assets it converges to the average covariance instead — the undiversifiable "market risk" floor. Diversification kills idiosyncratic risk, never systematic risk.
THM Law of Large Numbers — averages converge— ⊡ ✕

Weak LLN. For iid $X_i$ with finite mean μ: for every $\varepsilon\gt0$,

$$P\big(|\bar X_n-\mu|\gt\varepsilon\big)\ \longrightarrow\ 0\qquad(n\to\infty)$$

The sample mean ends up as close to μ as you like, with probability as high as you like. Proof in one line (with finite σ², via Chebyshev):

$$P\big(|\bar X_n-\mu|\gt\varepsilon\big)\le\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\varepsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\to 0$$

This is why probability's definition as "long-run frequency" is self-consistent: relative frequencies are sample means of indicators, so they converge to the probability.

Trap — what the LLN does NOT say
It does not say deviations get "corrected". After 10 heads in a row, tails is not "due" — the coin has no memory. The average settles because early fluctuations get diluted by dividing by an ever-larger n, not because later flips compensate. Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy.
THM Central Limit Theorem — the shape of the fluctuations— ⊡ ✕

The LLN says $\bar X_n$ → μ; the CLT describes the shape of the remaining wobble. For iid $X_i$ with mean μ, variance σ² (any distribution!):

$$\frac{\bar X_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}=\frac{S_n-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\ \xrightarrow{\ d\ }\ N(0,1)$$

Practical reading: for large n (rule of thumb n ≳ 30),

$$\bar X_n\ \approx\ N\Big(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big)\qquad S_n=\textstyle\sum X_i\ \approx\ N\big(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2\big)$$

The remarkable part: the source distribution can be skewed, discrete, bimodal — its shape is forgotten. Only μ and σ² survive into the limit. That universality is why the normal rules statistics and why "many small independent shocks" so often ends in a bell curve.

Normal approximation to the binomial

$X\sim$ Bin(n, p) is a sum of Bernoullis, so for large n (rule: $np(1-p)\gtrsim 10$):

$$X\approx N\big(np,\ np(1-p)\big)$$

With the continuity correction (a discrete k occupies $[k-\tfrac12,k+\tfrac12]$):

$$P(X\le k)\approx\Phi\Big(\frac{k+\tfrac12-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\Big)$$
DEF Convergence concepts — exam-level version— ⊡ ✕
In probability
$X_n\xrightarrow{p}c$		$P(|X_n-c|\gt\varepsilon)\to0$: the RV itself pins down to a value. LLN is convergence in probability of $\bar X_n$ to μ
In distribution
$X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$		the CDFs converge: $F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)$ (at continuity points). Only the shape stabilises. CLT is convergence in distribution of the standardised mean to N(0,1)

Hierarchy: convergence in probability ⇒ in distribution (converse only when the limit is a constant). The two theorems don't clash: $\bar X_n$ collapses to the constant μ, while the rescaled deviation $\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)$ keeps a stable normal shape. The $\sqrt n$ magnifier is what separates them.

Finance — Monte Carlo
To price a payoff, simulate many scenarios and average: the LLN guarantees the average converges to the expectation, and the CLT gives the error bar: estimate ± $1.96\,s/\sqrt n$. Doubling accuracy costs 4× the simulations — the √n rule again.
EX CLT in action, both flavours— ⊡ ✕

(a) Daily P&L of a desk is iid with mean 0.1 and SD 2 (in k€). Approximate P(yearly P&L over 250 days exceeds 50). (b) A fair coin is flipped 100 times; approximate P(at most 45 heads) with continuity correction.

  1. (a) Sum: $S\approx N(250\times0.1,\ 250\times4)=N(25,1000)$, $\sigma_S=\sqrt{1000}\approx31.6$.
  2. $P(S\gt50)=1-\Phi\big(\frac{50-25}{31.6}\big)=1-\Phi(0.79)\approx 1-0.785=\mathbf{0.215}$.
  3. (b) $X\sim$ Bin(100, ½): μ = 50, σ = 5. Continuity correction: $P(X\le45)\approx\Phi\big(\frac{45.5-50}{5}\big)=\Phi(-0.9)$.
  4. $\Phi(-0.9)=1-\Phi(0.9)=1-0.8159=\mathbf{0.184}$ (exact value 0.184 — the correction earns its keep; without it you would get 0.159).
Insight
Sums scale as $(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2)$, averages as $(\mu,\ \sigma^2/n)$ — decide which one the question asks about before touching Φ. For discrete counts, add the ±½ unless n is huge.
