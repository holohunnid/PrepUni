04

Expectation, variance & moments

the centre, the spread, and the shape of a distribution
DEF Expected value — the long-run average— ⊡ ✕

The expected value (mean) of $X$ is the probability-weighted average of its values:

$$E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\ \ \text{(discrete)}\qquad E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x f(x)\,dx\ \ \text{(continuous)}$$

Two ways to read it: (1) long-run average — repeat the experiment thousands of times and the running average of outcomes settles at $E[X]$ (that is the Law of Large Numbers, module 9); (2) centre of mass — put the PMF/PDF on a see-saw; $E[X]$ is where it balances.

Example. Die: $E[X]=\frac{1+2+\dots+6}{6}=3.5$. Note $X$ can never equal 3.5 — the mean is a balance point, not a typical value.

Example. A lottery ticket costs 2 and pays 100 with probability 1%. Net payoff $E = 0.01(98)+0.99(-2)=-1$: on average you lose 1 per ticket.

LOTUS — expectation of a function

For $g(X)$ you do not need the distribution of $g(X)$; weight $g$'s values by the original probabilities ("Law Of The Unconscious Statistician"):

$$E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)\,p(x)\qquad\text{or}\qquad \int g(x)f(x)\,dx$$

Linearity — the most-used theorem in the course

$$E[aX+b]=a\,E[X]+b\qquad E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]\ \ \textbf{always}$$

Linearity holds for any random variables — dependent, correlated, whatever. No independence needed. Expectations of sums are painless; that is why so many tricks reduce problems to sums.

Tip — indicator trick
To count "how many of $n$ events happen", write $X=\mathbf 1_{A_1}+\dots+\mathbf 1_{A_n}$ where the indicator $\mathbf 1_A$ is 1 if $A$ occurs, else 0. Since $E[\mathbf 1_A]=P(A)$: $E[X]=\sum_i P(A_i)$ — even when the events are dependent. This one-liner cracks otherwise nasty problems (see W10).
DEF Variance and standard deviation — how spread out— ⊡ ✕

Variance is the expected squared distance from the mean; the standard deviation brings it back to the original units:

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E\big[(X-\mu)^2\big]=E[X^2]-\big(E[X]\big)^2,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$

The right-hand form ("mean of the square minus square of the mean") is the one you compute with: get $E[X]$, get $E[X^2]$ via LOTUS, subtract.

Example. Die: $E[X^2]=\frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6}=\frac{91}{6}$, so $\operatorname{Var}=\frac{91}{6}-3.5^2=\frac{35}{12}\approx 2.92$, $\sigma\approx 1.71$.

Finance
For a return $R$, $E[R]$ is the expected return and $\sigma$ is the volatility — the standard unit of risk. Mean–variance portfolio theory is literally these two numbers per asset plus the covariances of module 7.

Rules

Shift & scale$\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ — shifting doesn't change spread; the sign of $a$ disappears
Always ≥ 0$\operatorname{Var}(X)=0$ iff $X$ is a constant
Sum, independent$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ if independent (uncorrelated is enough)
Sum, general$\operatorname{Var}(X+Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$ — module 7
Standardising$Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}$ has mean 0, variance 1 — used constantly with the normal
Trap
Standard deviations do not add: $\sigma_{X+Y}\ne\sigma_X+\sigma_Y$. Variances add (under independence), then take the square root. Also $\operatorname{Var}(2X) = 4\operatorname{Var}(X) \ne \operatorname{Var}(X_1+X_2)=2\operatorname{Var}(X)$: doubling one bet is riskier than two independent bets — this is diversification, in one line.
DEF Moments, skewness, kurtosis — the shape numbers— ⊡ ✕

The $k$-th moment is $E[X^k]$; the $k$-th central moment is $E[(X-\mu)^k]$. The first two give mean and variance; the next two, standardised, describe shape:

Skewness$\gamma_1=E\Big[\big(\tfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\big)^3\Big]$ — asymmetry. $\gt 0$: long right tail; $\lt 0$: long left tail; symmetric ⇒ 0
Kurtosis$\gamma_2=E\Big[\big(\tfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\big)^4\Big]$ — tail weight. Normal has kurtosis 3; "excess kurtosis" = $\gamma_2-3$. Above 3 ⇒ fat tails: extreme outcomes more likely than the normal predicts
Finance
Daily equity returns show negative skew (crashes are bigger than rallies) and excess kurtosis (extreme days far more common than a normal model predicts). Risk models that assume normality understate tail risk — module 10 returns to this.
DEF Moment generating function— ⊡ ✕

The MGF packs every moment into one function:

$$M_X(t)=E\big[e^{tX}\big]$$

Two superpowers:

Moments by differentiation$E[X^k]=M_X^{(k)}(0)$ — differentiate $k$ times, set $t=0$
Sums of independent RVs$M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)\,M_Y(t)$ — products, not convolutions
Uniquenessif two RVs have the same MGF (near 0), they have the same distribution — used to identify the distribution of a sum in module 8
Linear transform$M_{aX+b}(t)=e^{bt}M_X(at)$

Example. $X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)$: $M(t)=E[e^{tX}]=(1-p)e^{0}+pe^{t}=1-p+pe^t$. Then $M'(0)=p=E[X]$ ✓ and $M''(0)=p=E[X^2]$, so $\operatorname{Var}=p-p^2=p(1-p)$ ✓.

Exam use: mostly (1) compute an MGF from a PMF/PDF, (2) recognise a known MGF to identify a distribution, (3) extract moments. The zoo tables in modules 5–6 list every MGF.

THM The three inequalities — bounds when you know almost nothing— ⊡ ✕

Markov (needs only the mean, X ≥ 0)

$$P(X\ge a)\le \frac{E[X]}{a}\qquad (a\gt 0)$$

Crude but universal: if average income is 50k, at most half the population can earn ≥ 100k.

Chebyshev (needs mean and variance)

$$P\big(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma\big)\le \frac{1}{k^2}$$

At most $1/k^2$ of the probability lies more than $k$ standard deviations from the mean — for any distribution. $k=2$: ≤ 25% (the normal's actual figure is 4.6% — Chebyshev is a worst-case guarantee, not an estimate). Chebyshev powers the proof of the Law of Large Numbers in module 9.

Jensen (needs convexity)

If $g$ is convex (curves upward, like $x^2$, $e^x$, $1/x$ on $x\gt0$):

$$E[g(X)]\ \ge\ g\big(E[X]\big)$$

Flipped for concave $g$ (like $\log x$, $\sqrt x$): $E[g(X)]\le g(E[X])$.

Finance
Utility functions are concave (diminishing marginal utility), so $E[u(W)]\le u(E[W])$: a risky wealth is worth less than its expected value for sure — that gap is why risk premia exist. Also: $E[e^X]\ge e^{E[X]}$ explains why lognormal mean exceeds $e^{\mu}$ (module 6).
EX Insurance contract — mean, variance, and the E[1/X] trap— ⊡ ✕

An insurer sells a policy for a premium of 12. With probability 0.1 the client claims 100, otherwise nothing. Let $\Pi$ be the insurer's profit. (a) Find $E[\Pi]$ and $\sigma_\Pi$. (b) Show that $E[1/X]\neq 1/E[X]$ for $X$ = claim size + 100.

  1. Profit: $\Pi=12-100=-88$ with prob. 0.1; $\Pi=12$ with prob. 0.9. $E[\Pi]=0.1(-88)+0.9(12)=2$: profitable on average.
  2. $E[\Pi^2]=0.1(7744)+0.9(144)=904$, so $\operatorname{Var}(\Pi)=904-4=900$, $\sigma_\Pi=30$. A mean of 2 with a SD of 30 — one policy is a gamble; only a large, diversified book (module 9) makes insurance a business.
  3. (b) $X=200$ w.p. 0.1, $X=100$ w.p. 0.9: $E[X]=110$, so $1/E[X]\approx 0.00909$. But $E[1/X]=0.1/200+0.9/100=0.0095$. Different — and $E[1/X]\gt 1/E[X]$, exactly as Jensen predicts for the convex function $1/x$.
Insight
Never push expectations through nonlinear functions: $E[g(X)]\ne g(E[X])$ unless $g$ is linear. LOTUS computes the left side; Jensen tells you which way the inequality goes.
