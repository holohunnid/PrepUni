Conditional probability & independence

updating probabilities when you learn something
DEF Conditional probability — probability inside a shrunken world— ⊡ ✕

$P(A\mid B)$ reads "the probability of $A$ given that $B$ happened". The idea: once you know $B$ occurred, the sample space shrinks from $\Omega$ down to $B$ — outcomes outside $B$ are no longer possible. Inside that smaller world, the share belonging to $A$ is the overlap:

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\qquad (P(B)\gt 0)$$

Picture the Venn diagram of module 1: conditioning on $B$ means "zoom into circle $B$ and rescale its area to 1". $P(A\mid B)$ is the fraction of $B$'s area covered by $A$.

Example. Roll a die. $A=\{6\}$, $B=\{\text{even}\}$. Knowing the roll is even leaves three equally likely outcomes $\{2,4,6\}$, so $P(A\mid B)=\tfrac{1}{3}$ — up from $\tfrac16$. Information changed the probability.

Multiplication rule (chain rule)

Rearranging the definition gives the tool for sequential experiments:

$$P(A\cap B)=P(B)\,P(A\mid B)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)$$ $$P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)\,P(C\mid A\cap B)$$

In words: multiply along the path of the story, each factor conditioned on everything before it.

Example. Two cards from a deck, no replacement. $P(\text{both aces})=\frac{4}{52}\cdot\frac{3}{51}=\frac{1}{221}$. The second factor is conditional: after one ace leaves, 3 aces remain among 51 cards.

Trap
$P(A\mid B)\ne P(B\mid A)$ in general. "Probability of a positive test given the disease" and "probability of the disease given a positive test" are wildly different numbers — confusing them is the base-rate fallacy, dissected below.
THM Law of total probability — average over the scenarios— ⊡ ✕

A partition $B_1,\dots,B_n$ splits $\Omega$ into non-overlapping scenarios that cover everything (exactly one of them happens). Then any event $A$ can be computed scenario by scenario:

$$P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$$

In words: the overall probability of $A$ is the weighted average of its probability in each scenario, weighted by how likely each scenario is. The simplest partition is $\{B, B^c\}$:

$$P(A)=P(A\mid B)P(B)+P(A\mid B^{c})P(B^{c})$$
Finance
A bond defaults with probability 3% in a recession and 0.5% in an expansion. If P(recession) = 0.2: $P(\text{default}) = 0.03(0.2)+0.005(0.8)=1\%$. Scenario-weighted averaging is the law of total probability.
THM Bayes' theorem — reversing the conditioning— ⊡ ✕

You know how likely the evidence is under each hypothesis; you want how likely the hypothesis is given the evidence. Bayes flips the arrow:

$$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A)}=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A\mid B)P(B)+P(A\mid B^{c})P(B^{c})}$$

Anatomy: $P(B)$ is the prior (belief before evidence), $P(A\mid B)$ the likelihood, $P(B\mid A)$ the posterior (belief after). The denominator is just the law of total probability — it makes the posteriors over all hypotheses sum to 1.

Tip
In an exam, never memorise the big fraction. Draw the tree (below), multiply along branches to get every path probability, then: posterior = (path you want) / (sum of all paths producing the observed evidence).
INTERACTIVE Bayes tree — drag the sliders, watch the posterior— ⊡ ✕

Setting: a fraction $p$ of firms will default this year (prior). A rating model flags a firm as "risky" with probability $s$ if it will truly default (sensitivity), and falsely flags a healthy firm with probability $f$ (false-positive rate). A firm just got flagged — what is the probability it actually defaults?

With the default settings the posterior is only ≈ 32% — even though the model catches 90% of defaulters. The reason: healthy firms are so numerous (95%) that even a 10% false-flag rate produces more false alarms than true hits.

Trap — base-rate fallacy
Ignoring the prior. A "90% accurate" test on a rare condition still yields mostly false positives. The prior $P(D)$ drags the posterior heavily — try sliding it and watch.
DEF Independence — when information changes nothing— ⊡ ✕

$A$ and $B$ are independent when learning that one happened does not change the probability of the other:

$$P(A\mid B)=P(A)\iff P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)$$

The product form $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$ is the official definition (it also works when $P(B)=0$) and the version you use to check independence. If $A,B$ are independent, so are $A, B^c$ and $A^c, B^c$.

Physically distinct mechanisms (two different dice, two different coin flips) are modelled as independent. But independence can also hold "by accident" numerically, and fail where you expect it — always verify with the product rule when asked.

Pairwise vs mutual

Three events are mutually independent only if the product rule holds for every pair and for the triple: $P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A)P(B)P(C)$. Pairwise independence alone does not imply the triple condition — a classic counterexample appears in module 12 (W07).

Trap — independent ≠ disjoint
These are near-opposites. Disjoint events cannot co-occur: knowing $A$ happened tells you $B$ certainly did not — maximal information, so disjoint events with positive probability are never independent. Independence means the overlap is exactly $P(A)P(B)$ — they do co-occur, at just the "neutral" rate.

Independence of many events — the workhorse

For independent events, "none of them happens" multiplies:

$$P(\text{none of } A_1,\dots,A_n)=\prod_{i=1}^n\big(1-P(A_i)\big)$$ $$P(\text{at least one})=1-\prod_{i=1}^n\big(1-P(A_i)\big)$$

Example. A system fails if any of 3 independent components fails; each fails with prob. 0.05. $P(\text{system fails})=1-0.95^3\approx 14.3\%$ — much bigger than any single 5%.

Finance
10 loans, each defaulting independently with prob. 2%: P(at least one default) $=1-0.98^{10}\approx 18.3\%$. Portfolio risk accumulates fast — and real defaults are positively correlated, which makes joint bad outcomes even likelier than the independent model says. Module 7 quantifies this.
EX Full Bayes computation, exam layout— ⊡ ✕

Factory machines A, B, C produce 50%, 30%, 20% of output with defect rates 1%, 2%, 4%. A random item is defective. Which machine most likely produced it?

  1. Partition by machine. Priors: $P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.3$, $P(C)=0.2$. Likelihoods: $P(D\mid A)=0.01$, $P(D\mid B)=0.02$, $P(D\mid C)=0.04$.
  2. Path probabilities (prior × likelihood): $0.005$, $0.006$, $0.008$. Total probability of a defect: $P(D)=0.019$.
  3. Posteriors: $P(A\mid D)=\tfrac{5}{19}\approx0.263$, $P(B\mid D)=\tfrac{6}{19}\approx0.316$, $P(C\mid D)=\tfrac{8}{19}\approx0.421$.
  4. Machine C — despite producing only 20% of items, its high defect rate dominates. Check: posteriors sum to 1 ✓.
Insight
The posterior ranks hypotheses by prior × likelihood — the denominator is the same for all of them, so you can compare paths before dividing. Sanity checks: every posterior in $[0,1]$, and they must sum to 1.
