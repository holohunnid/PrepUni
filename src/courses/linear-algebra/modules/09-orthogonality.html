Orthogonality

perpendicular bases, Gram–Schmidt, least squares
READ FIRST Why perpendicular is powerful— ⊡ ✕

Module 1 defined orthogonal ($\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}=0$, perpendicular). This module explains why you should want orthogonality everywhere. The standard axes of ℝ² are pleasant to work with precisely because they are perpendicular unit vectors: to find a point's coordinates you just read off components — no equations to solve. A general basis (module 6) makes you solve a linear system for every coordinate; an orthonormal basis gives coordinates by dot products alone.

The module builds up in three steps:

  • Orthonormal sets and orthogonal matrices — the vocabulary, and why these matrices represent rotations/reflections.
  • Gram–Schmidt — an algorithm that upgrades any basis to an orthonormal one, by repeatedly subtracting shadows (projections, module 1).
  • Least squares — the big application. When $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has no solution (typical with real data: more equations than unknowns), the best compromise is found by projecting $\mathbf{b}$ onto what $A$ can reach. This is exactly linear regression: OLS is this module's mathematics wearing a statistics hat.

Why orthonormal bases are nice

With a general basis, finding the coordinates of $\mathbf{x}$ means solving a system. With an orthonormal basis $\{\mathbf{q}_1,\dots,\mathbf{q}_n\}$, each coordinate is a single dot product — project onto each axis and you're done:

$$\mathbf{x}=\sum_i(\mathbf{q}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{q}_i,\qquad\|\mathbf{x}\|^2=\sum_i(\mathbf{q}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x})^2\;(\text{Parseval})$$

Why it works: dot the expansion $\mathbf{x}=c_1\mathbf{q}_1+\dots+c_n\mathbf{q}_n$ with $\mathbf{q}_j$; every term dies except $c_j$, because $\mathbf{q}_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{q}_j=0$ for $i\neq j$ and $1$ for $i=j$.

An orthogonal matrix $Q$ moves vectors rigidly — lengths and angles survive: $\|Q\mathbf{x}\|=\|\mathbf{x}\|$ and $(Q\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf T}(Q\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}$ (proof of the second: $\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Q\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}I\mathbf{y}$). So orthogonal matrices are precisely rotations and reflections; $\det Q=\pm1$ ($+1$ rotation, $-1$ reflection). Examples: rotation $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$, permutation matrices.

Projection onto a subspace

Module 1 projected onto a line. To project onto a whole subspace $W=C(A)$ (with $A$'s columns independent), the same "shadow" idea gives a projection matrix:

$$P=A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T},\qquad P^2=P\ (\text{projecting twice changes nothing}),\;P^{\mathsf T}=P$$

With orthonormal columns $Q$ the ugly middle factor collapses: $P=QQ^{\mathsf T}$. Onto a single vector $\mathbf{a}$: $P=\dfrac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}}{\mathbf{a}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a}}$ — module 1's formula in matrix clothing.

Check yourself: verify the columns of $Q=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ are orthonormal. (Each has length 1; their dot product is $\frac12(−1+1)=0$. This $Q$ is rotation by 45°.)

IDEA 2 Gram–Schmidt: manufacturing orthonormal bases— ⊡ ✕

Given any independent set $\{\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_k\}$, Gram–Schmidt produces an orthogonal set $\{\mathbf{u}_i\}$ with the same span. The idea is just module 1's projection used repeatedly: keep each new vector's perpendicular part only — subtract from $\mathbf{a}_i$ its shadows on all directions already built, so what remains is perpendicular to all of them:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_1&=\mathbf{a}_1 &&\text{(keep the first as is)}\\ \mathbf{u}_2&=\mathbf{a}_2-\frac{\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a}_2}{\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{u}_1 &&\text{(remove the part along }\mathbf{u}_1)\\ \mathbf{u}_3&=\mathbf{a}_3-\frac{\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a}_3}{\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_1}\mathbf{u}_1-\frac{\mathbf{u}_2^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a}_3}{\mathbf{u}_2^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_2}\mathbf{u}_2 &&\text{(remove both shadows)}\\ \mathbf{q}_i&=\mathbf{u}_i/\|\mathbf{u}_i\| &&\text{(normalise at the very end)} \end{aligned}$$

The span never changes at any step (you only subtract multiples of earlier vectors), so $\{\mathbf{q}_i\}$ is an orthonormal basis of the original span. Bookkeeping the subtracted coefficients gives the QR factorisation $A=QR$ with $R$ upper triangular — the numerically stable route to least squares.

Trap
Project onto the already-orthogonalised $\mathbf{u}_j$, not onto the original $\mathbf{a}_j$ — that's the whole point of the method. Keep fractions exact throughout, and normalise only at the end (normalising early buries you in square roots).
IDEA 3 Least squares: the best wrong answer— ⊡ ✕

Fit a line through 3 data points and you get 3 equations in 2 unknowns — usually no exact solution: $\mathbf{b}$ lies outside the column space $C(A)$ (module 6), the set of right-hand sides $A$ can actually produce. Rather than give up, minimise the total squared error:

$$\min_{\mathbf{x}}\|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2$$

The geometric solution. As $\mathbf{x}$ ranges over everything, $A\mathbf{x}$ sweeps out the subspace $C(A)$. We want the point of that subspace closest to $\mathbf{b}$ — and the closest point is always the perpendicular foot: the projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $C(A)$. So the optimal $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is characterised by the error $\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}-A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ being orthogonal to every column of $A$:

$$A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{0}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^{\mathsf T}A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}=A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}$$

The middle equation is the famous normal equations ("normal" = perpendicular). $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is invertible ⟺ columns of $A$ independent. Fitted values: $\hat{\mathbf{b}}=A\hat{\mathbf{x}}=P\mathbf{b}$ with the projection matrix $P=A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}$ from Idea 1 — the circle closes.

Finance link
Rename the symbols and this is OLS regression: $A\to X$ (data matrix), $\mathbf{b}\to\mathbf{y}$, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\to\hat\beta=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{y}$. "Residuals orthogonal to regressors" is not a statistical assumption — it is the geometry of projection. Estimating a stock's beta is projecting its returns onto the market's.
EX Worked examples: Gram–Schmidt and a least-squares line— ⊡ ✕

Gram–Schmidt on $\mathbf{a}_1=(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, $\mathbf{a}_2=(1,0,1)^{\mathsf T}$

  1. Keep the first: $\mathbf{u}_1=(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}$
  2. Shadow coefficient of $\mathbf{a}_2$ on $\mathbf{u}_1$: $\dfrac{\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{a}_2}{\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_1}=\dfrac{1}{2}$, so $\mathbf{u}_2=(1,0,1)^{\mathsf T}-\tfrac12(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1)^{\mathsf T}$
  3. Check orthogonality (always): $\mathbf{u}_1^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{u}_2=\tfrac12-\tfrac12+0=0$ ✓
  4. Normalise last: $\mathbf{q}_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, $\;\mathbf{q}_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt{6}}(1,-1,2)^{\mathsf T}$ (scaled $\mathbf{u}_2$ by 2 first — allowed, direction is all that matters)

Fit $y=\beta_0+\beta_1t$ through $(0,1),(1,3),(2,4)$

Three points, two unknowns — overdetermined, so least squares. Each row of $A$ is one point: a 1 (for the intercept) and its $t$-value:

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\;\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{pmatrix},\;A^{\mathsf T}A=\begin{pmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix},\;A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}8\\11\end{pmatrix}$$

Solve the normal equations with the 2×2 inverse formula ($\det=15-9=6$):

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}8\\11\end{pmatrix}=\frac16\begin{pmatrix}7\\9\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}7/6\\3/2\end{pmatrix}$$

Best-fit line: $\hat y=\tfrac76+\tfrac32t$. Residuals $\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{b}-A\hat\beta=(-\tfrac16,\tfrac13,-\tfrac16)^{\mathsf T}$ — no point is hit exactly, but check the defining property $A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{e}=\mathbf{0}$: column 1 gives $-\tfrac16+\tfrac13-\tfrac16=0$ ✓. The residuals sum to zero precisely because the model has an intercept column of ones.
