Quadratic forms & definiteness

x′Ax, the shape of variance
READ FIRST From ax² to x′Ax— ⊡ ✕

In one variable, the simplest curved function is the parabola $q(x)=ax^2$. Its whole personality is the sign of $a$: positive → a valley with a minimum at 0; negative → a hill with a maximum. A quadratic form is the many-variable version: a function $Q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf{x}$ built from squares ($x_1^2$, $x_2^2$, …) and cross products ($x_1x_2$, …), packaged with a symmetric matrix $A$.

The question "is $a$ positive or negative?" becomes: is $Q(\mathbf{x})$ positive for every direction $\mathbf{x}$, negative for every direction, or does it depend on the direction? That property of $A$ is called its definiteness, and in several variables there's a genuinely new possibility: a surface shaped like a horse saddle — a valley along one axis, a hill along another.

Why a finance student must care: portfolio variance is a quadratic form, $\sigma_p^2=\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf{w}$, and variance can never be negative — which is precisely the statement that a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is positive semidefinite. Definiteness is also the second-order condition of optimisation: it tells you whether a critical point is a min, a max, or a saddle.

IDEA 1 The definition & the classification— ⊡ ✕

For symmetric $A$ ($n\times n$), the quadratic form is the scalar function

$$Q(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}a_{ij}x_ix_j$$

Multiply it out in 2D to see the anatomy — diagonal entries multiply squares, off-diagonal entries feed the cross term:

$$\begin{pmatrix}x_1&x_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{pmatrix}=a x_1^2+2b\,x_1x_2+c\,x_2^2$$

Note the 2: the cross term $x_1x_2$ collects contributions from both off-diagonal entries, so the matrix entry is half the cross-term coefficient. Going from a formula to the matrix: squares on the diagonal, half of each cross coefficient off it. Any quadratic form can be written with a symmetric matrix this way.

Why do eigenvalues decide it? By the spectral theorem (module 7), in eigen-coordinates the cross terms vanish: $Q=\lambda_1y_1^2+\dots+\lambda_ny_n^2$. A sum of $\lambda_i\times(\text{square})$ is positive for every input exactly when every $\lambda_i>0$; one negative $\lambda$ gives a downhill direction.

The minor test ($D_k$ = determinant of the top-left $k\times k$ block; $D_1=a_{11}$, $D_2=a_{11}a_{22}-a_{12}^2$, …, $D_n=\det A$) is usually the fastest hand computation — no eigenvalues needed.

Exam shortcut
2×2 symmetric: PD ⟺ $a>0$ and $\det>0$. ND ⟺ $a<0$ and $\det>0$. $\det<0$ ⟹ indefinite (always). $\det=0$ ⟹ semidefinite; the sign of the trace tells you which kind.

Check yourself: classify $Q=x_1^2+4x_1x_2+x_2^2$. (Matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, $D_2=1-4=-3<0$ ⟹ indefinite: try $(1,1)$ → $Q=6$ but $(1,-1)$ → $Q=-2$.)

What the level sets look like

The set $\{\mathbf{x}: \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf{x}=1\}$ ("contour line at height 1") is an ellipse when $A$ is PD, a hyperbola when indefinite, parallel lines when PSD singular. The ellipse's axes point along the eigenvectors of $A$, with semi-axis lengths $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ — big eigenvalue = steep direction = short axis. (These are exactly the iso-variance ellipses of portfolio theory.)

Useful bounds (Rayleigh quotient): the form is always squeezed between its extreme eigenvalues,

$$\lambda_{\min}\|\mathbf{x}\|^2\le\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf{x}\le\lambda_{\max}\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$$
INTERACTIVE Shape of x′Ax for a symmetric 2×2— ⊡ ✕

$A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}$. Read the classification three ways at once — sign of $Q$ on the plot, eigenvalues, leading minors — and confirm all three always agree:

  • PD: all green — every direction goes uphill from the origin (a bowl seen from above).
  • indefinite: green and red quadrants split by the white eigen-axes — the saddle.
  • PSD: green everywhere except a line where $Q=0$ — the flat valley floor; note $\lambda_2=0$.
  • Increase $b$ slowly from a PD start and watch $D_2=ac-b^2$ hit zero: the moment cross-coupling outweighs the squares, the bowl cracks into a saddle.
EX Worked example: classify a 3×3— ⊡ ✕

Step 1 is always the same: read the matrix off the formula — squares on the diagonal, half of each cross coefficient off it:

$$Q(\mathbf{x})=2x_1^2+2x_2^2+3x_3^2+2x_1x_2-2x_2x_3\quad\Rightarrow\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&-1\\0&-1&3\end{pmatrix}$$

$x_1x_2$ coefficient 2 ⟹ $a_{12}=a_{21}=1$; $x_2x_3$ coefficient −2 ⟹ $a_{23}=a_{32}=-1$; no $x_1x_3$ term ⟹ $a_{13}=0$.

Leading principal minors

$$D_1=2>0,\qquad D_2=\begin{vmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{vmatrix}=4-1=3>0$$ $$D_3=\det A=2(6-1)-1(3-0)+0=10-3=7>0$$

All three positive ⟹ positive definite. Done — no eigenvalues needed.

Cross-check with trace and det

$\text{tr}=7=\sum\lambda_i>0$ and $\det=7=\prod\lambda_i>0$ are consistent with all $\lambda_i>0$ (necessary but not sufficient on their own — the minors test is the actual proof).

Consequence: $Q$ is a bowl with a unique global minimum at $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$; if $A$ were a covariance matrix, every non-zero portfolio would carry strictly positive variance — no riskless combination exists.

Trap
For semidefiniteness the leading minors are not enough — you must check all principal minors (every square submatrix symmetric about the diagonal). Counterexample: $\text{diag}(0,-1)$ has $D_1=0,D_2=0$, passing "all $D_k\ge0$", yet it is clearly not PSD (direction $(0,1)$ gives $Q=-1$).
