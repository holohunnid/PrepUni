04

Determinants

one number that says whether a square matrix is invertible
READ FIRST What the determinant actually measures— ⊡ ✕

The determinant assigns one number, $\det A$, to every square matrix. Before learning to compute it, know what it means — think of the matrix as the machine from module 2, transforming space:

  • $|\det A|$ is the scaling factor for area (2D) or volume (3D): feed the machine a shape of area 1, and out comes a shape of area $|\det A|$.
  • The sign records orientation: negative means the machine flips space over (like a reflection in a mirror).
  • $\det A=0$ is the disaster case: the machine squashes space flat — 2D collapses onto a line, 3D onto a plane. Squashing destroys information, so a $\det=0$ matrix cannot be undone: no inverse exists. This single test, $\det A\neq0$ ⟺ invertible, is the main practical use of determinants.

$A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$, columns $\mathbf{a}_1=\binom{a}{c}$ and $\mathbf{a}_2=\binom{b}{d}$. Experiments to run:

  • Make the columns parallel (try the singular preset): the parallelogram collapses to a line, $\det=0$, $A$ is not invertible — this is what "squashing space" looks like.
  • Swap the columns (swap preset): area identical, sign flips — orientation reversed.
  • rotate 90°: $\det=1$, area preserved, orientation preserved — rotations don't distort.
  • Double one column: det doubles (rule 2 below). Double both: det ×4 (rule 4 with $n=2$).
IDEA 1 Computing det: 2×2, 3×3, then any size— ⊡ ✕

2×2 — memorise this

Product of the main diagonal minus product of the anti-diagonal:

$$\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{vmatrix}=ad-bc$$

E.g. $\begin{vmatrix}3&1\\4&2\end{vmatrix}=6-4=2$. The vertical-bar notation $|\cdot|$ means "determinant of".

3×3 — Sarrus rule

Copy the first two columns to the right, then add the three ↘ diagonal products and subtract the three ↙ ones:

$$\begin{vmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&i\end{vmatrix}=aei+bfg+cdh-ceg-bdi-afh$$

n×n: cofactor (Laplace) expansion

The general method reduces an $n\times n$ determinant to $n$ smaller ones. Pick any row $i$ (or column): walk along it, and for each entry $a_{ij}$ multiply it by the determinant of what remains when you delete row $i$ and column $j$ (that smaller determinant is the minor $M_{ij}$), with an alternating sign:

$$\det A=\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}\,C_{ij},\qquad C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$$

The signs $(-1)^{i+j}$ follow a checkerboard — top-left is always $+$:

$$\begin{pmatrix}+&-&+\\-&+&-\\+&-&+\end{pmatrix}$$

Each 3×3 expands into three 2×2s; a 4×4 into four 3×3s; and so on recursively.

Exam shortcut
You may expand along any row or column — the answer is the same — so pick the one with the most zeros (each zero kills a whole term). Better still: use row operations to create zeros first (rule 3 below leaves det unchanged), then expand. And if the matrix is triangular, skip everything: det = product of the diagonal.

Check yourself: $\begin{vmatrix}2&0\\5&3\end{vmatrix}=?$ (Answer: $6-0=6$ — triangular, so just $2\cdot3$.)

IDEA 2 Properties: compute less, know more— ⊡ ✕

These rules follow from the area interpretation (e.g. doubling one column doubles the area; a flip changes the sign) and turn many exam questions into one-liners:

#Operation / factEffect on det
1Swap two rows$\det\to-\det$ (orientation flips)
2Multiply one row by $c$$\det\to c\det$
3Add multiple of a row to anotherunchanged — the elimination move is free!

7$\det(A^{-1})$$=1/\det A$ (undoing a ×3 scaling is a ×⅓ scaling)

11A zero row or column$0$

14Orthogonal $Q$$\pm1$ (rotations/reflections preserve volume)

Cramer's rule (square, $\det A\neq0$)

A determinant-only formula for solving $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$: to get unknown $x_i$, replace column $i$ of $A$ with $\mathbf{b}$ and take the ratio of determinants:

$$x_i=\frac{\det A_i}{\det A},\qquad A_i=A\text{ with column }i\text{ replaced by }\mathbf{b}$$

Fine for 2×2 and 3×3, or when you need just one unknown; far too slow beyond that — use elimination instead.

EX Worked example: 3×3 determinant two ways + Cramer— ⊡ ✕

Same matrix as the module-3 walkthrough — we now confirm, without solving, that its system had to have a unique solution:

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&-1\\-3&-1&2\\-2&1&2\end{pmatrix}$$

Method 1: Sarrus

$$\begin{aligned}\det A&=2(-1)(2)+1(2)(-2)+(-1)(-3)(1)\\&\quad-(-1)(-1)(-2)-1(-3)(2)-2(2)(1)\\&=-4-4+3+2+6-4=\color{#3ea76a}{-1}\end{aligned}$$

Method 2: cofactor along row 1

Walk along the top row; signs from the checkerboard are $+,-,+$:

$$\det A=2\begin{vmatrix}-1&2\\1&2\end{vmatrix}-1\begin{vmatrix}-3&2\\-2&2\end{vmatrix}+(-1)\begin{vmatrix}-3&-1\\-2&1\end{vmatrix}$$ $$=2(-2-2)-1(-6+4)-1(-3-2)=-8+2+5=\color{#3ea76a}{-1}\;\checkmark$$

Two independent methods agreeing is your arithmetic insurance. Since $\det A=-1\neq0$: invertible, unique solution for any $\mathbf{b}$.

Cramer for $x$ with $\mathbf{b}=(8,-11,-3)^{\mathsf T}$

Replace column 1 with $\mathbf{b}$ and expand:

$$A_1=\begin{pmatrix}\color{#4c8dff}{8}&1&-1\\\color{#4c8dff}{-11}&-1&2\\\color{#4c8dff}{-3}&1&2\end{pmatrix},\quad\det A_1=-2$$ $$x=\frac{\det A_1}{\det A}=\frac{-2}{-1}=2\;\checkmark\text{ (matches module 3)}$$
Trap
Signs in cofactor expansion follow the checkerboard, not the sign of the entry — expanding along row 2 starts with a minus. Second most common error: forgetting that $\det(2A)=2^3\det A=8\det A$ for a 3×3 (rule 4).
