07

Eigenvalues & eigenvectors

the directions a matrix only stretches
READ FIRST The idea in one picture— ⊡ ✕

A matrix $A$ moves vectors around: most vectors get rotated and stretched, landing somewhere off their original line. But almost every matrix has a few special directions where the action is dead simple — vectors on those lines stay on their line and are merely stretched (or shrunk, or flipped) by some factor. Those directions are the eigenvectors ("eigen" = German for "own/characteristic"); the stretch factors are the eigenvalues $\lambda$.

Why hunt for them? Because along an eigenvector the complicated matrix behaves like simple multiplication by a number. If you can find $n$ independent eigen-directions, you can describe the whole matrix in those terms — that's diagonalisation, and it turns hard problems into arithmetic:

  • $A^{100}$ (e.g. a Markov chain run 100 steps) without 100 multiplications;
  • long-run behaviour of dynamic systems ($\lambda$'s bigger than 1 grow, smaller than 1 die out);
  • PCA in statistics: eigen-directions of a covariance matrix are the axes of maximal variance.

Plan: definition and the recipe for finding eigenpairs, an interactive to build the geometric picture, then diagonalisation and its uses.

IDEA 1 Definition & the recipe— ⊡ ✕

For square $A$: a nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if

$$A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$$

Left side: what the machine does to $\mathbf{v}$. Right side: plain scaling by the number $\lambda$. The equation says they are the same — $A$ acts on $\mathbf{v}$ with no rotation at all. ($\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ is excluded: it satisfies the equation for every $\lambda$, telling us nothing. $\lambda=0$ is allowed — it means $A\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$, i.e. $\mathbf{v}$ is squashed to the origin.)

How to find them — and why the recipe works

The trick: move everything to one side. $A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}=\lambda I\mathbf{v}$ becomes

$$(A-\lambda I)\,\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$$

This is a homogeneous system (module 3), and we need a nontrivial solution $\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}$ — which exists only when the matrix $A-\lambda I$ is singular, i.e. has determinant zero. That gives a condition on $\lambda$ alone:

  1. Solve the characteristic equation $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. It is a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$; its roots are the eigenvalues (up to $n$ of them).
  2. For each root $\lambda_i$, solve $(A-\lambda_iI)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ by row reduction. The solutions form the eigenspace $E_{\lambda_i}=N(A-\lambda_iI)$ — any nonzero vector in it is an eigenvector (they come in whole lines: if $\mathbf{v}$ works, so does $2\mathbf{v}$).
  3. Verify: $A\mathbf{v}$ should equal $\lambda\mathbf{v}$. Ten seconds, catches everything.

2×2 shortcut

For a 2×2, the characteristic polynomial always takes this form — no need to expand the determinant by hand:

$$\lambda^2-\text{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det A=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=\frac{\text{tr}A\pm\sqrt{(\text{tr}A)^2-4\det A}}{2}$$

Multiplicities (how many times, in two senses)

  • Algebraic multiplicity (AM): how many times $\lambda$ appears as a root of the polynomial.
  • Geometric multiplicity (GM): how many independent eigenvectors it actually gets, $\dim E_\lambda=n-\text{rank}(A-\lambda I)$.
  • Always $1\le$ GM $\le$ AM. When GM < AM for some $\lambda$ ("missing" eigenvectors), diagonalisation fails — see the shear preset below.

Check yourself: what are the eigenvalues of $\text{diag}(3,-1)$? (Answer: 3 and −1 — for triangular or diagonal matrices the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries, with eigenvectors along the axes.)

IDEA 2 Properties worth knowing cold— ⊡ ✕

The first two rows are your permanent error-check: eigenvalues must add up to the trace and multiply to the determinant.

Trace$\text{tr}(A)=\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_n$
Determinant$\det A=\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$

Exam shortcut
Always check trace and det before hunting eigenvectors. For a 2×2 with integer entries: if $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\text{tr}$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det$, you are right. For a 3×3, find one root by trial (try $0,\pm1,\pm2$), then divide the cubic by $(\lambda-\lambda_1)$ to get a quadratic.

  • singular: one eigenvalue is 0 — watch its eigen-direction get squashed onto the origin.
IDEA 3 Diagonalisation: change to the eigen-coordinates— ⊡ ✕

Suppose $A$ ($n\times n$) has $n$ independent eigenvectors. Put them as columns of $P$, and the eigenvalues (same order!) on the diagonal of $D$:

$$P=[\mathbf{v}_1\,\cdots\,\mathbf{v}_n],\qquad D=\text{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A=PDP^{-1}$$

How to read $A=PDP^{-1}$: to apply $A$ to a vector, (1) $P^{-1}$: rewrite the vector in eigen-coordinates, (2) $D$: in those coordinates the map is just "stretch each axis by its $\lambda$" — the easy part, (3) $P$: translate back. $A$ is a simple stretch, seen in the right coordinate system.

When is it possible? Exactly when $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors ⟺ GM = AM for every eigenvalue. A convenient sufficient condition: $n$ distinct eigenvalues (each contributes at least one direction, and they're automatically independent).

The payoff: powers become trivial

Squaring telescopes: $A^2=PDP^{-1}PDP^{-1}=PD^2P^{-1}$ (the middle $P^{-1}P$ cancels), and so on:

$$A^k=PD^kP^{-1}=P\,\text{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\dots,\lambda_n^k)\,P^{-1}$$

Also $e^{A}=Pe^{D}P^{-1}$ and $A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}$: any function of $A$ reduces to that function of the $\lambda$'s.

Spectral theorem (symmetric matrices)

If $A=A^{\mathsf T}$, everything is as nice as possible: $A$ is always diagonalisable, the eigenvalues are real, and the eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, so $P$ becomes an orthogonal matrix $Q$ with $Q^{-1}=Q^{\mathsf T}$ — no inverse to compute:

$$A=Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^{\mathsf T}$$

The sum form says: a symmetric matrix is a weighted stack of rank-one projections onto perpendicular axes. This is the mathematical engine of PCA.

Finance link
Credit-rating transition matrix $T$: the $k$-year matrix is $T^k=PD^kP^{-1}$, and the long-run distribution is the eigenvector with $\lambda=1$. PCA: eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ are the principal components (uncorrelated risk factors); each eigenvalue is the variance carried by its component.
EX Worked example: eigen-decomposition & $A^{10}$— ⊡ ✕
$$A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$$
  1. Eigenvalues. $\text{tr}=7$, $\det=12-2=10$, so $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0\Rightarrow(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0$: $\lambda_1=5,\lambda_2=2$. Check: $5+2=7=$ tr ✓, $5\cdot2=10=$ det ✓.
  2. Eigenvector for $\lambda=5$. $A-5I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}$. Row 2 is −2× row 1 — as it must be: $A-\lambda I$ has to be singular (if it isn't, your $\lambda$ is wrong). One equation survives: $-v_1+v_2=0$, so $\mathbf{v}_1=\binom11$.
  3. Eigenvector for $\lambda=2$. $A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ ⟹ $2v_1+v_2=0$ ⟹ $\mathbf{v}_2=\binom{1}{-2}$.
  4. Verify both. $A\binom11=\binom55=5\binom11$ ✓; $A\binom1{-2}=\binom2{-4}=2\binom1{-2}$ ✓.
  5. Assemble. $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, and by the 2×2 inverse formula $P^{-1}=\frac{1}{-3}\begin{pmatrix}-2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
  6. Use it. $A^{10}=P\begin{pmatrix}5^{10}&0\\0&2^{10}\end{pmatrix}P^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot5^{10}+2^{10}&5^{10}-2^{10}\\2\cdot5^{10}-2\cdot2^{10}&5^{10}+2\cdot2^{10}\end{pmatrix}$ — ten matrix multiplications reduced to two scalar powers.

$A$ is not symmetric, so the eigenvectors need not be orthogonal — indeed $(1,1)\cdot(1,-2)=-1\neq0$. That's fine: $P^{-1}$ exists because the $\lambda$'s are distinct.

Trap
The eigenvector comes from the null space of $A-\lambda I$, which must be singular — a pivot in every column means your $\lambda$ is wrong; recompute it, don't force a vector. And keep the column order of $P$ matched to the order of the $\lambda$'s in $D$, or $PDP^{-1}$ reassembles the wrong matrix.
