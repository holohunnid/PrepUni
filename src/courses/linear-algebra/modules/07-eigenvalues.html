Eigenvalues & eigenvectors

the directions a matrix only stretches
DEF Definition & how to find them— ⊡ ✕

For square $A$, a nonzero $\mathbf{v}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if

$$A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$$

$A$ acts on $\mathbf{v}$ as pure scaling by $\lambda$: no rotation. $\lambda=0$ means $\mathbf{v}\in N(A)$.

Recipe

  1. Rewrite: $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$. Nontrivial $\mathbf{v}$ requires $A-\lambda I$ singular.
  2. Characteristic equation: $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. This is a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$; its roots are the eigenvalues.
  3. For each $\lambda_i$, solve $(A-\lambda_iI)\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}$ by row reduction. The solution space is the eigenspace $E_{\lambda_i}=N(A-\lambda_iI)$.
  4. Check: $A\mathbf{v}$ should equal $\lambda\mathbf{v}$.

2×2 shortcut

$$\lambda^2-\text{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det A=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda=\frac{\text{tr}A\pm\sqrt{(\text{tr}A)^2-4\det A}}{2}$$

Multiplicities

  • Algebraic (AM): how many times $\lambda$ is a root of the char. polynomial.
  • Geometric (GM): $\dim E_\lambda=n-\text{rank}(A-\lambda I)$.
  • Always $1\le$ GM $\le$ AM.

Exam shortcut
Use trace and det to check your eigenvalues before finding eigenvectors. For a 2×2 with integer entries: if $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\text{tr}$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det$, you are right. For a 3×3, after finding one root by trial (try 0, ±1, ±2), divide the cubic by $(\lambda-\lambda_1)$.
INTERACTIVE Eigenvectors of a 2×2 — watch which directions stay on their own line— ⊡ ✕

Things to notice:

  • symmetric: the two eigen-directions are perpendicular. Always true for symmetric matrices.
  • diagonal: eigenvectors are the axes, eigenvalues are the diagonal.
  • shear: only one eigen-direction; AM = 2, GM = 1, not diagonalisable.
  • rotation: no real eigenvectors; $\lambda=\pm i$.
  • singular: one eigenvalue is 0; its eigenvector is sent to the origin.
DEF Diagonalisation— ⊡ ✕

$A$ is diagonalisable if $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal. Then

$$P=[\mathbf{v}_1\,\cdots\,\mathbf{v}_n]\ (\text{eigenvectors as columns}),\qquad D=\text{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$$

Condition: $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors ⟺ GM = AM for every eigenvalue. Sufficient: $n$ distinct eigenvalues.

Why you care: powers

$$A^k=PD^kP^{-1}=P\,\text{diag}(\lambda_1^k,\dots,\lambda_n^k)\,P^{-1}$$

Also $e^{A}=Pe^{D}P^{-1}$, $A^{-1}=PD^{-1}P^{-1}$.

Spectral theorem (symmetric matrices)

If $A=A^{\mathsf T}$, then $A$ is always diagonalisable, with an orthonormal eigenvector matrix $Q$ ($Q^{\mathsf T}Q=I$):

$$A=Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^{\mathsf T}$$

No inverse to compute. This is the basis of PCA on a covariance matrix.

Finance link
Markov transition matrix $T$ for credit ratings: the $k$-year matrix is $T^k=PD^kP^{-1}$. The long-run distribution is the eigenvector with $\lambda=1$. PCA: eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ are principal components; eigenvalues are the variance explained.
EX Worked example: eigen-decomposition & $A^{10}$— ⊡ ✕
$$A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$$
  1. $\text{tr}=7$, $\det=10$: $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0\Rightarrow(\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0$, so $\lambda_1=5,\lambda_2=2$. Check $5+2=7$ ✓, $5\cdot2=10$ ✓.
  2. $\lambda=5$: $A-5I=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix}$, row 2 = −2·row 1 ⟹ $-v_1+v_2=0$ ⟹ $\mathbf{v}_1=\binom11$.
  3. $\lambda=2$: $A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ ⟹ $2v_1+v_2=0$ ⟹ $\mathbf{v}_2=\binom{1}{-2}$.
  4. Check: $A\binom11=\binom55=5\binom11$ ✓; $A\binom1{-2}=\binom2{-4}=2\binom1{-2}$ ✓.
  5. $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\begin{pmatrix}5&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, $P^{-1}=\frac{1}{-3}\begin{pmatrix}-2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$
  6. $A^{10}=P\begin{pmatrix}5^{10}&0\\0&2^{10}\end{pmatrix}P^{-1}=\frac13\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot5^{10}+2^{10}&5^{10}-2^{10}\\2\cdot5^{10}-2\cdot2^{10}&5^{10}+2\cdot2^{10}\end{pmatrix}$

Not symmetric, so eigenvectors are not orthogonal: $(1,1)\cdot(1,-2)=-1\neq0$. Fine, $P^{-1}$ still exists since $\lambda$'s are distinct.

Trap
The eigenvector for $\lambda$ comes from the null space of $A-\lambda I$, which must be singular. If your row reduction of $A-\lambda I$ gives a pivot in every column, your $\lambda$ is wrong. Also: keep the order of columns in $P$ matched with the order of $\lambda$'s in $D$.
