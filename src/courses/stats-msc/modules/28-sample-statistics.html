Sample statistics

lecture 6, notes p.39 · Point Estimation deck §3 · exercise sets 1–2
READ FIRST Summarising a dataset with a few numbers— ⊡ ✕

A dataset is not easy to communicate in its raw form: nobody reads 10,000 returns. We summarise it with a handful of sample statistics, such as the average, the spread and the middle value.

The key definition: a sample statistic is a function of the random sample, $Y=t(X_1,\dots,X_n)$, that uses the data and nothing else (no unknown parameter). Because $\underline X$ is random, every statistic is a random variable with its own distribution. That is the subject of modules 29–30.

This module lists the statistics the course uses: sample mean, sample variance, sample standard deviation, sample moments (raw and central), order statistics, sample median. It also gives the shortcut formula for $S^2$ that the exercise sets rely on.

What you need for the exam
Compute all of them by hand for a small sample. Remember that $S^2$ divides by $n-1$, not $n$. Recognise them in R code: a third of the exam is R multiple choice, and "which formula does this function compute?" is a standard question.
LECTURE 6 The most common statistics (notes p.39)— ⊡ ✕
Sample mean$\displaystyle\bar X=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i$
Sample variance$\displaystyle S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2$
Sample st. deviation$S=\sqrt{S^2}$
(Non-central) sample moment of order $r$$\displaystyle M_r=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^r$ ($M_1=\bar X$)
(Central) sample moment of order $r$$\displaystyle \bar M_r=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^r$
Order statistics$Y_1\le Y_2\le\dots\le Y_n$: the data sorted. $Y_1=\min\{X_i\}$, $Y_n=\max\{X_i\}$
Sample median$M_e=\begin{cases}Y_{\frac{n+1}2}&n\text{ odd}\\[4pt]\dfrac{Y_{\frac n2}+Y_{\frac n2+1}}2&n\text{ even}\end{cases}$

The notes draw an arrow from $M_r$ at $r=1$ back to $\bar X$: the sample mean is the first raw sample moment. Module 32 (method of moments) is built on these $M_r$.

TRAP · two "variances" with two denominators
$S^2$ divides by $n-1$. The second central sample moment $\bar M_2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ divides by $n$. Module 31 calls $\bar M_2$ the analogy estimator $\hat\sigma^2$, and shows it is biased while $S^2$ is not. R's var() and sd() use $n-1$.
FORMULA The shortcut for $S^2$ (exercise set 1, Q4)— ⊡ ✕

Expanding the square gives a formula that needs only $\sum x_i$ and $\sum x_i^2$:

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar x)^2&=\sum x_i^2+n\bar x^2-2\bar x\sum x_i\\&=\sum x_i^2+n\bar x^2-2n\bar x^2=\sum x_i^2-n\bar x^2\end{aligned}$$ $$\boxed{\;s^2=\frac1{n-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2-n\bar x^2\Big)\;}$$

The same identity divided by $n$ is $\bar M_2=M_2-\bar X^2$: the sample version of $\mathbb V(X)=\mathbb E(X^2)-\mathbb E(X)^2$. Module 32 uses it.

EXERCISE SET 1 · Q4 · employees of Swiss and British companies

Swiss sample: $n_X=5$, $\sum x_i=15$, $\sum x_i^2=73$. British sample: $n_Y=10$, $\sum y_i=40$, $\sum y_i^2=232$. Find the means and the sample variances, then the pooled variance $s_c^2=\frac{(n_X-1)s_X^2+(n_Y-1)s_Y^2}{n_X+n_Y-2}$.

  1. $\bar x=15/5=3$, and $s_X^2=\frac1{4}(73-5\cdot3^2)=\frac{28}4=\mathbf 7$.
  2. $\bar y=40/10=4$, and $s_Y^2=\frac19(232-10\cdot4^2)=\frac{72}9=\mathbf 8$.
  3. $s_c^2=\frac{4\cdot7+9\cdot8}{13}=\frac{100}{13}\approx\mathbf{7.69}$. A weighted average of the two variances, with weights $n-1$. It comes back in module 38 for the CI of a difference of means.

EXERCISE SET 2 · Q2 · salaries of 5 men and 5 women (in 1000 EUR)

Men: $25,19,16,17,23$. Women: $15,18,21,20,24$. (a) Sample median of the men. (b) $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$ for the women.

  1. (a) Order statistics $16,17,19,23,25$. $n=5$ is odd, so $M_e=Y_{3}=\mathbf{19}$.
  2. (b) $\bar y=98/5=19.6$. Deviations: $-4.6,-1.6,1.4,0.4,4.4$; squares $21.16+2.56+1.96+0.16+19.36=45.2$. So $\hat\sigma^2=45.2/5=\mathbf{9.04}$, while $s^2=45.2/4=11.3$.

The exercise sheet continues with $\mathbb E(\hat\sigma^2)$ and the bias of $\hat\sigma^2$. That is module 31.

EXERCISE SET 1 · Q3(a) · a bad week for a stock

Daily returns of one week: $-0.06,-0.10,0.01,-0.02,-0.04$. Find $\bar x$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2$.

  1. $\bar x=\frac{-0.06-0.10+0.01-0.02-0.04}5=\frac{-0.21}5=\mathbf{-0.042}$.
  2. Deviations $-0.018,-0.058,0.052,0.022,0.002$. Squares sum to $0.00688$, so $\hat\sigma^2=0.00688/5=\mathbf{0.001376}$.

Answer B. Option A ($0.0408$) is a typical slip: forgetting to square or dividing in the wrong place. Parts (b) and (c) of this exercise use the sampling distributions of module 30.

R Questions 2 showed three user-written functions and asked which formula each one computes:

  • xbar<-mean(x); y<-mean(abs(x-xbar)) is $\frac1n\sum|x_i-\bar x|$, the average absolute deviation. Careful: the option on the sheet writes $\frac1{n-1}$, which does not match mean().
  • mean((x-xbar)^3)/mean((x-xbar)^2)^(3/2) is the skewness $\bar M_3/\bar M_2^{3/2}$.
  • n/(n-1)^2*sum((x-xbar)^4)/var(x)^2: since var(x)$=\frac{n}{n-1}\bar M_2$, this simplifies to $\frac{\frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^4}{\bar M_2^2}$, the kurtosis $\bar M_4/\bar M_2^2$.
Kurtosis versus excess kurtosis
Kurtosis $\mu_4/\sigma^4$ equals 3 for every normal. Excess kurtosis $\beta_2=\mu_4/\sigma^4-3$ is 0 for the normal. Exercise set 1, Q2: for $X\sim N(1,4)$ the true statement is "the excess kurtosis is 0" (B), not "the kurtosis is 0". It matters again in module 29 for $\mathbb V(S^2)$.

?check yourselfIs it a statistic?
Which of these are sample statistics? (a) $\frac1n\sum(X_i-\mu)^2$ with $\mu$ unknown; (b) $\max\{X_i\}-\min\{X_i\}$; (c) $\bar X/S$; (d) $\bar X-\mu$.
Answer
(b) and (c) only. (a) and (d) contain the unknown $\mu$, so we cannot compute their values even after observing the data. The notes make exactly this point about (a) at the start of module 29.
