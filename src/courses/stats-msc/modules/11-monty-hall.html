Monty Hall

lecture 2, notes p.5–6 · slides: Conditional 22–24
READ FIRST The most famous argument about probability— ⊡ ✕

A TV show (Let's Make a Deal, host Monty Hall). Behind three doors: one car, two goats. You pick a door. The presenter — who knows where the car is — opens one of the two remaining doors and shows you a goat. Then he asks: do you want to switch to the other closed door?

Almost everyone's intuition says "two doors left, so it's 50/50, switching doesn't matter". That is wrong: switching wins with probability 2/3. The lecture proves it with Bayes' theorem (module 10); this module adds the intuition, a tree, and a game you can play until you believe it.

EX LECTURE 2 · notes p.5–6 · slides Conditional 23–24 — the Bayes solution— ⊡ ✕
  1. Events. Say we choose door 1 and the presenter opens door 2 (any other labelling is the same by symmetry). $$A=\{\text{car behind door 1}\},\ B=\{\ldots\text{door 2}\},\ C=\{\ldots\text{door 3}\}$$ Before anything is opened, $P(A)=P(B)=P(C)=\tfrac13$. $\{A,B,C\}$ is a partition.
  2. Question. Compare $P(A\mid\text{opens door 2})$ — staying wins — with $P(C\mid\text{opens door 2})$ — switching wins.
  3. Likelihoods — how would the presenter behave in each scenario?
    • If $A$ (car behind your door): doors 2 and 3 both hide goats; he picks one at random: $P(\text{opens }2\mid A)=\tfrac12$.
    • If $B$: he never reveals the car: $P(\text{opens }2\mid B)=0$.
    • If $C$: he cannot open your door or the car's door — door 2 is forced: $P(\text{opens }2\mid C)=1$.
  4. Denominator (total probability, module 08): $$P(\text{opens }2)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13+0\cdot\tfrac13+1\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac12$$
  5. Bayes: $$P(A\mid\text{opens }2)=\frac{P(\text{opens }2\mid A)P(A)}{P(\text{opens }2)}=\frac{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13}{\tfrac12}=\frac13$$ $$P(C\mid\text{opens }2)=\frac{P(\text{opens }2\mid C)P(C)}{P(\text{opens }2)}=\frac{1\cdot\tfrac13}{\tfrac12}=\frac23$$
  6. Conclusion. It is more convenient to switch: it doubles the chance of winning the car.

TRAP "Two doors left, so 50/50"— ⊡ ✕

Equal probabilities need a reason — symmetry. Here there is none: the host's choice was not random. He was forced away from the car, and his action carries information (likelihood 1 for door 3 vs $\tfrac12$ for door 1). Bayes weighs exactly that asymmetry.

The answer depends on the host's rules. The standard assumptions, all used above: he knows where the car is, always opens a goat door you did not pick, chooses at random when he has a choice, and always offers the switch.

?check yourselfAn ignorant host
Suppose the host does not know where the car is: he opens door 2 or 3 at random, and it happens to show a goat. Should you switch now?
Answer
Now the evidence is "opens 2 and it's a goat". Likelihoods: under $A$: $\tfrac12$; under $B$: 0 (it would have shown the car); under $C$: $\tfrac12$ (he picks door 2 half the time, and it's a goat). Equal likelihoods for $A$ and $C$ ⇒ posteriors $\tfrac12,\tfrac12$. Switching no longer helps — the host's knowledge was the whole point.
