$\bar X$ and $S^2$ for Bernoulli & normal data

lecture 6, notes pp.42–44 · Point Estimation deck §4 · exercise set 1
READ FIRST When we know the whole sampling distribution, not just its moments— ⊡ ✕

Module 29 gave the mean and variance of $\bar X$ for any population. For two populations we get the entire distribution, and with it any probability we like:

Bernoulli$(p)$ population$n\bar X=\sum X_i\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)$, so $\bar X$ is a rescaled binomial on $\{0,\tfrac1n,\dots,1\}$
Normal $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ population$\bar X\sim N(\mu,\tfrac{\sigma^2}n)$ exactly, $\ \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$, $\ \frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}$
Any population, $n$ large$\bar X\approx N(\mu,\tfrac{\sigma^2}n)$, by the Central Limit Theorem

This is where the χ² and Student-t of module 26 pay off. They are exactly the distributions of the sample variance and of the standardised mean with $\sigma$ estimated. Confidence intervals (modules 37–38) are built directly on these lines.

What you need for the exam
Compute $P(\bar X=\bar x)$ with dbinom for a Bernoulli sample. Compute $P(\bar X<c)$ for normal data. Compute $P(S^2>c)$ with the χ² result, and the reverse: a cut-off for $S^2$ with qchisq. Know the degrees of freedom: $n-1$, not $n$.
LECTURE 6 Bernoulli population (notes p.42 · deck §4.1)— ⊡ ✕

$X\sim\text{Bern}(p)$ $\Rightarrow$ the number of successes $\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)$ (module 19). We want $P(\bar X=\bar x)$. Since $\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i$, the event $\{\bar X=\bar x\}$ is the event $\{n\bar X=n\bar x\}=\{\sum X_i=n\bar x\}$:

$$P\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i=t\Big)=\binom nt p^t(1-p)^{n-t},\quad t=0,1,\dots,n$$ $$\boxed{\;P(\bar X=\bar x)=P\Big(\sum X_i=n\bar x\Big)=\binom{n}{n\bar x}p^{n\bar x}(1-p)^{n-n\bar x}\;}$$

Support. $\text{Supp}(\sum X_i)=\{0,1,\dots,n\}$, so

$$\text{Supp}(\bar X)=\Big\{\frac0n,\frac1n,\dots,\frac nn\Big\}=\Big\{0,\frac1n,\frac2n,\dots,1\Big\}.$$

LECTURE 6 Normal population: $\bar X$ is exactly normal (notes p.43 · deck §4.2)— ⊡ ✕

The result used: if $X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^2)$ are independent and $a_i$ are known constants, then

$$\boxed{\;\sum_{i=1}^na_iX_i\ \sim\ N\Big(\sum_{i=1}^na_i\mu_i,\ \sum_{i=1}^na_i^2\sigma_i^2\Big)\;}$$

A linear combination of independent normals is normal. Mean and variance follow the usual rules; the new fact is that the shape stays normal.

Consider $\bar X$ as a linear combination with $\mu_i=\mu$, $\sigma_i^2=\sigma^2$, $a_i=\frac1n$:

$$\sum\tfrac1nX_i\sim N\Big(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum\frac1n\mu}_{\mu},\ \underbrace{\textstyle\sum\frac1{n^2}\sigma^2}_{\frac1{n^2}n\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}n}\Big)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\boxed{\bar X\sim N\Big(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}n\Big)}$$

Note (notes p.43): when the distribution of $X$ is unknown but $n$ is "large", the result is still valid approximately, thanks to the Central Limit Theorem:

$$\bar X\ \approx\ N\Big(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}n\Big)\qquad\text{when $n$ is large,}$$

for any population with finite mean and variance. Rules of thumb from the CI deck: $n\ge30$ if $\sigma$ is known, $n\ge50$ if it is estimated.

Consequence: the standardised sample mean is standard normal (exactly for normal data, approximately otherwise):

$$Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2/n}}=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1).$$

THEOREM Normal population: $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ (notes p.43 · deck p.17)— ⊡ ✕

When $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ we can also recover the distribution of a function of $S^2$:

$$\boxed{\;(n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}\;}$$

Why a χ². $(n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{n-1}{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\frac{X_i-\bar X}\sigma\Big)^2$: a sum of squared normals, which is how module 26 defined the χ². It has $n-1$ degrees of freedom, not $n$, because the $n$ deviations satisfy one constraint, $\sum(X_i-\bar X)=0$.

Why it is useful. It turns any question about $S^2$ into a χ² question:

$$P(S^2>t)=P\Big((n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2}>(n-1)\frac t{\sigma^2}\Big)=1-\texttt{pchisq}\Big((n-1)\frac t{\sigma^2},\,n-1\Big).$$

Deck, also: for normal data $\bar X$ and $S^2$ are independent. This is a special property of the normal, and it is what makes the Student-t result below work.

LECTURE 6 · notes pp.43–44 · deck pp.20–21 · X ~ N(10, 20), n = 25
  1. The mean. $\bar X\sim N\big(10,\frac{20}{25}\big)=N(10,0.8)$.
  2. The variance. $(n-1)\frac{S^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{24}{20}S^2=1.2\,S^2\sim\chi^2_{24}$.
  3. $P(S^2>10)$. Multiply both sides by 1.2: $P(1.2S^2>12)=1-$pchisq(12, 24)$\approx\mathbf{0.98}$. With $\sigma^2=20$, a sample variance above 10 is almost certain.
  4. A cut-off. Find $x$ with $P(1.2S^2>x)=0.05$: $x=$qchisq(0.95, 24)$=\mathbf{36.42}$. Corresponding cut-off for $S^2$: $36.42/1.2=\mathbf{30.35}$. Only 5% of samples of 25 give $S^2$ above 30.35.
THEOREM Normal population, $\sigma$ unknown: $\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}$ (deck §4.3)— ⊡ ✕

Usually $\sigma^2$ is unknown, so $Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$ cannot be computed. Replace $\sigma$ by $S$:

$$\boxed{\;T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}\;}$$

Why a Student-t. Module 26: $t_\nu=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}$ with $Z\sim N(0,1)$ independent of $V\sim\chi^2_\nu$. Here $Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$, $V=\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ (independent of $\bar X$), and $\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-1)}}=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\cdot\frac{\sigma}{S}=T$. The unknown $\sigma$ cancels.

Estimating $\sigma$ adds uncertainty, so the t has heavier tails than $N(0,1)$ and larger cut-offs. As $n\to\infty$ it tends to $N(0,1)$.

DECK pp.24–25 · cut-offs for X ~ N(10, 20)
  1. $n=25$: $T=\frac{\bar X-10}{\sqrt{S^2/25}}\sim t_{24}$. The 5% cut-off $P(T>t)=0.05$: qt(1-0.05, df=24) $=\mathbf{1.711}$.
  2. Normal for comparison: qnorm(1-0.05) $=$ qnorm(0.05, lower.tail=F) $=\mathbf{1.645}$.
  3. $n=101$: $t_{100}$ cut-off qt(0.95, df=100) $=\mathbf{1.660}$, much closer to 1.645. For large samples the t cut-offs can be replaced by the normal ones.
FORMULA Two samples: the difference of two sample means (deck pp.26–28)— ⊡ ✕

Two independent samples $(X_1,\dots,X_{n_1})$ and $(Y_1,\dots,Y_{n_2})$, e.g. household incomes in two regions. In general, regardless of the distribution:

$$\mathbb E(\bar X-\bar Y)=\mu_X-\mu_Y,\qquad\mathbb V(\bar X-\bar Y)=\frac{\sigma_X^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{n_2}.$$

Variances add, even for a difference: $\mathbb V(-\bar Y)=(-1)^2\mathbb V(\bar Y)$.

  • Normal populations: $\bar X-\bar Y\sim N\big(\mu_X-\mu_Y,\ \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{n_2}\big)$.
  • Bernoulli populations, large $n_1,n_2$: $\bar X-\bar Y\approx N\big(p_1-p_2,\ \frac{p_1(1-p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{p_2(1-p_2)}{n_2}\big)$.
  • Normal, equal unknown variance: $\dfrac{(\bar X-\bar Y)-(\mu_1-\mu_2)}{\sqrt{S_c^2\big(\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}\big)}}\sim t_{n_1+n_2-2}$, with the pooled variance $S_c^2=\frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2+(n_2-1)S_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}$ (module 28).

Q4(d) Both normal: $\bar X+\bar Y\sim N\big(\mu_X+\mu_Y,\ \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n_X}+\frac{\sigma_Y^2}{n_Y}\big)$, answer C. Each mean has its own $n$, and the variances add for a sum too.

Q5 $X_i\sim N(\mu_X,\sigma^2)$, $Y_j\sim N(\mu_Y,\sigma^2)$, independent samples of sizes $n,m$.

  1. $\bar X-\bar Y\sim N\big(\mu_X-\mu_Y,\ \sigma^2(\frac1n+\frac1m)\big)$.
  2. $\frac{(n-1)S_X^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$ and $\frac{(m-1)S_Y^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{m-1}$ are independent, and independent χ²'s add their degrees of freedom (a sum of $n-1$ plus $m-1$ squared normals): $$\frac{(n-1)S_X^2+(m-1)S_Y^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n+m-2}.$$
EXERCISE SET 1 · Q3(b)–(c) · the bad week, continued

Daily returns i.i.d. $N(0.01,\,0.05)$; this week (n = 5) gave $\bar x=-0.042$ and $\hat\sigma^2=0.001376$ (module 28). (b) Probability that next week's average is even lower?

  1. $\bar X\sim N\big(0.01,\frac{0.05}5\big)=N(0.01,0.01)$, so $\sigma_{\bar X}=0.1$.
  2. $P(\bar X\le-0.042)=\Phi\Big(\frac{-0.042-0.01}{0.1}\Big)=\Phi(-0.52)=\mathbf{0.3015}$. Answer C.

(c) Probability that next week's variance is higher? Write $\hat\sigma^2=\frac{n-1}nS^2$, so $\frac{n\hat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}=\chi^2_4$:

$$P(\hat\sigma^2\ge0.001376)=P\Big(\frac{5\hat\sigma^2}{0.05}\ge\frac{5\cdot0.001376}{0.05}\Big)=1-F_{\chi^2_4}(0.1376)\approx0.998.$$
