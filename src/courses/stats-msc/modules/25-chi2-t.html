χ², Student-t & percentiles

slides: Special Continuous RVs 20–28, 31 · ahead of the lecture notes
READ FIRST Distributions built out of normals, and how to read them backwards— ⊡ ✕

Module 24 gave you the normal. This module builds two new distributions from normals, because they are exactly what you get when you compute statistics from normal data:

$\chi^2(k)$a sum of $k$ squared independent standard normalsdescribes sample variances
Student-t$(\nu)$a standard normal divided by $\sqrt{\chi^2(\nu)/\nu}$describes the standardised mean when $\sigma$ is unknown

Then the deck turns the question around: instead of "what is the probability below $x$?" it asks "which $x$ has probability $p$ below it?" That value is a percentile, and percentiles of $N(0,1)$, $t$ and $\chi^2$ are the "critical values" of every confidence interval and test in the inference part of the course.

Tools reused: the cdf method (module 14) for $W=Z^2$; $\Phi$ and its symmetry (module 24); $\mathbb{E}$, $\mathbb{V}$ and their rules (module 22); the exponential of module 23 reappears twice.

What you really need for the exam
Recognise the definitions ($\chi^2$ = sum of squared normals; $t=Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$), know the moments ($k$ and $2k$; $0$ and $\nu/(\nu-2)$), know the shapes (χ² skewed right; t symmetric with heavier tails than the normal, tending to it as $\nu\to\infty$), and compute percentiles — with $\Phi$ by hand, or with qnorm, qt, qchisq, qexp in R. Nobody computes the $\chi^2$ or $t$ cdf by hand.
EXAMPLE (slide 20) · the distribution of $W=Z^2$

Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$. Find the distribution function and the density of $W=Z^2$.

  1. Support. Obviously $W>0$ (a square is never negative, and $P(Z=0)=0$). So $F_W(x)=0$ for $x\le0$, and we work with $x>0$.
  2. The cdf trick (module 14): write the event about $W$ as an event about $Z$. For $x>0$, $z^2\le x$ exactly when $-\sqrt x\le z\le\sqrt x$: $$F_W(x)=P(W\le x)=P(Z^2\le x)=P(-\sqrt x<Z<\sqrt x)=\Phi(\sqrt x)-\Phi(-\sqrt x).$$ (By symmetry this is also $2\Phi(\sqrt x)-1$.)
  3. Differentiate, using the chain rule. $\Phi'=f_Z$, and the inner derivatives are $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt x=\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}(-\sqrt x)=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$: $$f_W(x)=f_Z(\sqrt x)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}-f_Z(-\sqrt x)\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}\right)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}f_Z(\sqrt x)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}f_Z(-\sqrt x).$$
  4. Use evenness, $f_Z(-\sqrt x)=f_Z(\sqrt x)$, so the two halves are equal: $$f_W(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt x}f_Z(\sqrt x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(\sqrt x)^2}{2}}=\boxed{\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}\,e^{-\frac x2}\qquad x>0\,}$$
  5. Name it (slide 20). The random variable $W=Z^2$ is called a $\chi^2$ variable with one degree of freedom.
Insight
The method never used a formula for $\Phi$ — only that $\Phi'=f_Z$. That is why the cdf trick is so powerful: probabilities can stay "unknown" as long as you only need their derivatives.
DEF $\chi^2$ with $k$ degrees of freedom (slide 21)— ⊡ ✕

Let $Z_1,\dots,Z_k$ be independent $N(0,1)$ random variables. Then

$$\boxed{\,W=\sum_{i=1}^{k}Z_i^2\ \sim\ \chi^2(k)\ \text{ or }\ \chi^2_k\,}$$

The number of squared normals, $k$, is the degrees of freedom. Slide 20 was the case $k=1$.

pdf and cdf of $\chi^2_k$

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2\right)}\,x^{\frac k2-1}\,e^{-\frac x2}\qquad0\le x<\infty$$ $$F(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac k2\right)}\,\gamma\!\left(\frac k2,\frac x2\right)\qquad0\le x<\infty$$

where $\Gamma$ is the Gamma function and $\gamma(a,b)$ the (lower) incomplete gamma function, both explained on the right. The density formula is given, not derived — the derivation needs joint distributions.

Two checks that tie it to what you know

  • $k=1$: with $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$, the constant is $\frac{1}{2^{1/2}\sqrt\pi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ and the power is $x^{-1/2}$, so $f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi x}}e^{-x/2}$ — exactly slide 20 ✓.
  • $k=2$: $\Gamma(1)=1$ and $x^0=1$, so $f(x)=\frac12e^{-x/2}$: the exponential with $\lambda=\tfrac12$ of module 23 (mean 2).
DEF The Gamma function (slide 21)— ⊡ ✕

For $z>0$,

$$\boxed{\,\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt\,}$$

(the slide writes $\Gamma(x)$ on the left and $t^{z-1}$ inside — it is one and the same variable). And the lower incomplete gamma function stops the integral at $b$:

$$\gamma(a,b)=\int_0^b t^{a-1}e^{-t}\,dt,\qquad\text{so }\gamma(a,b)\to\Gamma(a)\text{ as }b\to\infty.$$

That is why $F(x)=\gamma(\frac k2,\frac x2)/\Gamma(\frac k2)$ climbs from 0 to 1.

Why $\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$ (the slide's first line)

  1. $\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt=1$.
  2. Integration by parts ($u=t^z$, $dv=e^{-t}dt$) gives the recursion $\Gamma(z+1)=\big[-t^ze^{-t}\big]_0^\infty+z\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt=z\,\Gamma(z)$.
  3. So $\Gamma(2)=1$, $\Gamma(3)=2\cdot1$, $\Gamma(4)=3\cdot2\cdot1$, …, $\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$ for positive integers $n$. The Gamma function is the factorial extended to non-integers ("otherwise" on the slide).

$\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$

Substitute $t=u^2/2$ ($dt=u\,du$, $t^{-1/2}=\sqrt2/u$): $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\sqrt2}{u}e^{-u^2/2}u\,du=\sqrt2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2/2}du=\sqrt2\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{2}=\sqrt\pi$, using the Gaussian integral of module 24.

THEOREM $\mathbb{E}(W)=k$ and $\mathbb{V}(W)=2k$ (slide 24)— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{\,\mathbb{E}(W)=k\,}\qquad\boxed{\,\mathbb{V}(W)=2k\,}$$

The slide states the results; here is why they hold, one squared normal at a time.

  1. Mean of one square. $\mathbb{E}(Z^2)=\mathbb{V}(Z)+\big(\mathbb{E}(Z)\big)^2=1+0=1$ (module 24).
  2. Fourth moment. Parts with $u=x^3$, $dv=xf_Z(x)dx$, $v=-f_Z(x)$: $$\mathbb{E}(Z^4)=\big[-x^3f_Z(x)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+3\int x^2f_Z(x)\,dx=0+3\,\mathbb{E}(Z^2)=3.$$
  3. Variance of one square. $\mathbb{V}(Z^2)=\mathbb{E}(Z^4)-\big(\mathbb{E}(Z^2)\big)^2=3-1=2$.
  4. Add them up. Expectations always add: $\mathbb{E}(W)=\sum_{i=1}^k\mathbb{E}(Z_i^2)=k$. For independent variables variances add too (a fact you will meet with joint distributions): $\mathbb{V}(W)=\sum_{i=1}^k\mathbb{V}(Z_i^2)=2k$.

DEF Student-t with $\nu$ degrees of freedom (slide 25)— ⊡ ✕

Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$ and $V\sim\chi^2(\nu)$ be independent. Then

$$\boxed{\,T=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}=Z\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{V}}\,}$$

is a Student-t random variable with $\nu$ degrees of freedom.

Intuition. $V/\nu$ is an average of $\nu$ squared standard normals, so $\mathbb{E}(V/\nu)=1$ and $\mathbb{V}(V/\nu)=2\nu/\nu^2=2/\nu$. The denominator is therefore a noisy estimate of 1. Dividing $Z$ by something that is sometimes well below 1 occasionally blows it up, so $T$ has more extreme values than $Z$. As $\nu$ grows the noise $2/\nu$ vanishes and $T$ becomes $Z$.

Student-t plays an important role when estimating the mean of a normally distributed population when the sample size is small and the variance is unknown.

The sample-mean construction (slide 25)

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ be independent, with sample mean and sample variance

$$\overline{X}_n=\frac1n(X_1+\dots+X_n),\qquad S_n^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\overline{X}_n)^2$$

($S_n^2$ is an unbiased estimator of the variance — the inference part explains the $n-1$). Then:

  1. The variance part is $\chi^2$: $$V=(n-1)\frac{S_n^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\overline{X}_n)^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2(n-1).$$
  2. The mean part is standard normal: $\overline X_n$ has mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2/n$, so $$Z=(\overline X_n-\mu)\frac{\sqrt n}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1),$$ and it can be shown that $Z$ and $V$ are independent.
  3. Build $T$ with $\nu=n-1$. The denominator is $\sqrt{V/(n-1)}=\sqrt{S_n^2/\sigma^2}=S_n/\sigma$, so $$T\equiv\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/\nu}}=\frac{(\overline X_n-\mu)\sqrt n/\sigma}{S_n/\sigma}=\boxed{\,(\overline X_n-\mu)\frac{\sqrt n}{S_n}\,}\sim t(n-1).$$
Why $S_n$ replaces $\sigma$ — a preview of inference
In practice $\sigma$ is unknown, so $Z=(\overline X_n-\mu)\sqrt n/\sigma$ cannot be computed. Step 3 shows the fix: replace $\sigma$ by the sample sd $S_n$ and the unknown $\sigma$ cancels. The price is that the result is no longer $N(0,1)$ but $t(n-1)$, a little wider, to account for the extra uncertainty in $S_n$. This is the t-statistic, and it gives the 95% confidence interval $\overline x\pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,S_n/\sqrt n$. The student-t approaches the standard normal as the degrees of freedom go to infinity.
PICTURE The Student-t density (slide 26)— ⊡ ✕
$$f(x)=\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\;\Gamma\!\left(\frac\nu2\right)}\left(1+\frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}\qquad-\infty<x<\infty$$
  • Symmetric, with the y-axis as axis of symmetry (slide 26): $f$ depends on $x$ only through $x^2$.
  • Heavier tails than the normal: the tail decays like a power of $x$, not like $e^{-x^2/2}$.
  • Tends to $N(0,1)$ as $\nu\to\infty$: $\ln\!\big(1+\frac{x^2}{\nu}\big)^{-\frac{\nu+1}{2}}=-\frac{\nu+1}{2}\ln\!\big(1+\frac{x^2}{\nu}\big)\approx-\frac{\nu+1}{2}\cdot\frac{x^2}{\nu}\to-\frac{x^2}{2}$, and the constant tends to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$.

FORMULA The Student-t cdf (slide 27)— ⊡ ✕
$$F(x)=\frac12+x\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{{}_2F_1\!\left(\frac12,\frac{\nu+1}{2};\frac32;-\frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\;\Gamma\!\left(\frac\nu2\right)}$$

where ${}_2F_1$ is the hypergeometric function (the slide says "hyperbolic" — a slip; ${}_2F_1$ is Gauss's hypergeometric function, a power series you will never evaluate by hand).

How to read it. $F(0)=\frac12$ (symmetry), and the second term is an odd function of $x$ that pushes $F$ up for $x>0$ and down for $x<0$. The slide 27 plot shows the four cdfs ($\nu=1,2,5,\infty$) as S-curves all passing through $(0,\tfrac12)$: the $\nu=1$ curve is the flattest (still about 0.03 at $x=-10$), the normal the steepest.

One case by hand (not on the slides). For $\nu=1$, ${}_2F_1(\tfrac12,1;\tfrac32;-x^2)=\frac{\arctan x}{x}$, and with $\Gamma(1)=1$, $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$:

$$F(x)=\frac12+\frac{\arctan x}{\pi}\qquad(\text{Cauchy}),\qquad\text{e.g. }F(2)=\frac12+\frac{1.1071}{\pi}=0.8524.$$

For every other $\nu$ use software: pt(q, df) in R. pt(2, df = 1) → 0.8524164.

THEOREM Moments of the Student-t (slide 28)— ⊡ ✕
Expected value$\mathbb{E}(X)=0$ for $\nu>1$; otherwise undefined
Variance$\mathbb{V}(X)=\dfrac{\nu}{\nu-2}$ for $\nu>2$
$\mathbb{V}(X)=\infty$ for $1<\nu\le2$; otherwise undefined
  • Mean 0 by symmetry — but only when the integral $\int|x|f(x)dx$ is finite, which needs $\nu>1$.
  • $\nu=1$ is the Cauchy distribution, $f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}$ (plug $\nu=1$ into slide 26: $\Gamma(1)/(\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\frac12))=\frac1\pi$). It has no mean: $\int_0^\infty\frac{x}{\pi(1+x^2)}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\big[\ln(1+x^2)\big]_0^\infty=\infty$. The tails are so heavy that the positive and negative parts are each infinite, and "$\infty-\infty$" is undefined. Averages of Cauchy draws never settle down.
  • Variance above 1, shrinking to 1: $\nu=3$: $3$; $\nu=5$: $\frac53\approx1.667$; $\nu=30$: $\frac{30}{28}\approx1.071$; $\nu\to\infty$: 1, the $N(0,1)$ value.
TRAP · "symmetric, so the mean is 0"
True for $\nu>1$ only. For the Cauchy the centre of symmetry is 0 and the median is 0, but the mean does not exist. This is the case slide 29 excludes: "some random variables do not have finite mean or variance".

DEF Percentiles (slide 31)— ⊡ ✕

One can characterise a probability distribution by dividing points, called percentiles.

Definition. The $(100p)$th percentile, $0<p<1$, is a point $\pi_p$ such that

$$P(X\le\pi_p)=p\qquad\text{and}\qquad P(X>\pi_p)=1-p.$$

So $\pi_p$ is the solution of the equation

$$\boxed{\,F(\pi_p)=p\,}$$

For the continuous distributions of this course $F$ is continuous and strictly increasing on the support, so the solution is unique: $\pi_p=F^{-1}(p)$, the cdf read backwards — from the probability on the vertical axis to the value on the horizontal axis.

The most important percentiles:

  • the median $m=\pi_{1/2}$ — a point such that at most half the population lies below it and at most half above it;
  • the quartiles $q_1=\pi_{1/4}$ and $q_3=\pi_{3/4}$, the first and third quartiles. Together with the median they cut the distribution into four pieces of probability $\tfrac14$.

WORKED PERCENTILES (computed here) · uniform, exponential, normal
  1. Uniform $U(a,b)$. Solve $\frac{\pi_p-a}{b-a}=p$: $\pi_p=a+p(b-a)$. For the bus arrival $U(0,30)$: median 15 (7:15), quartiles 7.5 and 22.5 minutes past 7.
  2. Exponential$(\lambda)$: the median. Solve $F(m)=\frac12$: $$1-e^{-\lambda m}=\tfrac12\ \Longrightarrow\ e^{-\lambda m}=\tfrac12\ \Longrightarrow\ -\lambda m=\ln\tfrac12=-\ln2\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{\,m=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}\,}$$ In general $\pi_p=-\ln(1-p)/\lambda$. For Exponential(1): $q_1=\ln\frac43=0.288$, $m=\ln2=0.693$, $q_3=\ln4=1.386$ — the figure above. The median $0.693/\lambda$ is below the mean $1/\lambda$: right skew.
  3. $N(0,1)$: the quartiles. $q_3$ solves $\Phi(q_3)=0.75$; the table (or qnorm(0.75)) gives $q_3=0.674$. By symmetry $\Phi(-q_3)=1-0.75=0.25$, so $q_1=-0.674$, and the median is 0. In general $\pi_{1-p}=-\pi_p$ for any symmetric distribution.
  4. $N(0,1)$: $z_{0.975}=1.960$. $\Phi(1.96)=0.975$, so the 97.5th percentile is 1.960 and, by symmetry, the 2.5th is $-1.960$: the central 95% of $N(0,1)$ lies in $\pm1.96$ (the "±1.96" preset of module 23's figure).
  5. Any normal. $F(\pi_p)=\Phi\big((\pi_p-\mu)/\sigma\big)=p$ gives $\pi_p=\mu+\sigma z_p$. For the earnings $N(5,4)$ of module 24: $q_3=5+2(0.674)=6.349$ and $\pi_{0.975}=5+2(1.96)=8.92$.
Insight
Percentiles transform like the variable: if $Y=\mu+\sigma Z$ then $\pi_p(Y)=\mu+\sigma\,\pi_p(Z)$. That is why one table of $z_p$ serves every normal — standardisation once more.
WORKED PERCENTILES (computed here) · Student-t and χ²
  1. $t_{5,\,0.975}=2.571$. There is no closed form: qt(0.975, df = 5) → 2.570582. Check: pt(2.570582, 5) → 0.975. By symmetry $t_{5,\,0.025}=-2.571$. Compared with $z_{0.975}=1.960$, the heavier tails push the cut-off outwards: a 95% interval from $n=6$ observations ($\nu=5$) is $2.571/1.960\approx1.31$ times wider than it would be with $\sigma$ known.
  2. $\chi^2_{1,\,0.95}=3.841$, by hand. Since $W=Z^2$, for $c>0$: $P(W\le c)=P(|Z|\le\sqrt c)=2\Phi(\sqrt c)-1$. Setting this to 0.95 gives $\Phi(\sqrt c)=0.975$, so $\sqrt c=1.96$ and $$c=1.96^2=3.8416\quad(\texttt{qchisq(0.95, 1)}\to3.841459).$$ The 95th percentile of $\chi^2(1)$ is the square of the 97.5th percentile of $N(0,1)$.
  3. χ² is not symmetric. Its lower and upper percentiles are not mirror images: for $\chi^2(9)$ the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are 2.700 and 19.023, around a mean of 9.

TRAP · degrees of freedom are $n-1$
With $n$ observations the t-statistic has $\nu=n-1$. For $n=6$: qt(0.975, 5) $=2.571$, not qt(0.975, 6) $=2.447$. The same $n-1$ appears in $V=(n-1)S_n^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2(n-1)$.
TRAP · which tail?
The slides' $\pi_p$ has area $p$ to the left, so 1.96 is $\pi_{0.975}$. Many books write it $z_{0.025}$ or $z_{\alpha/2}$ with $\alpha=0.05$, naming the area to the right. R's q-functions always use the left area (unless lower.tail = FALSE). Draw the picture before you look anything up.
