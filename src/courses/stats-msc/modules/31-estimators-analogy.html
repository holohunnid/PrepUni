Estimators & the analogy method

lecture 6, notes pp.44–45 · Point Estimation deck §5 · exercise set 2
READ FIRST Point estimation: one number for the unknown $\theta$— ⊡ ✕

We have a model $\{f(x;\theta),\ \theta\in\Theta\}$ and data. Since the model is fixed, learning the best $f$ reduces to learning the best $\theta$. Point estimation assigns to $\theta$ a single "reasonable" value computed from the data.

The rest of the estimation block has two halves, exactly as the notes announce:

Methods to find estimatorsanalogy (this module) · method of moments (32) · maximum likelihood (33–34) · Bayesian methods (not covered)
Methods to evaluate and compare thembias, MSE, efficiency, UMVUE (35) · consistency, asymptotics (36)

The analogy method is the simplest idea of all: estimate a population quantity with the same quantity computed on the sample. It often works, and it shows the first example of a biased estimator.

What you need for the exam
The estimator / estimate distinction (capital / small letter). Analogy estimates of a mean, median, proportion and variance, computed by hand. The bias of $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$, which is $-\sigma^2/n$. Exercise set 2, Q2 is the model question.
LECTURE 6 Point estimation methods: the vocabulary (notes p.44)— ⊡ ✕

Basic assumption: we have a parametric statistical model $\{f(x;\theta);\ \theta\in\Theta\}$. $\theta$ is unknown, so, looking at the data, we assign to $\theta$ a "reasonable" value: the point estimate of $\theta$.

Estimator of $\theta$any statistic $T=t(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ we use to estimate $\theta$. A function of the random sample, so the estimator is random.
Estimate of $\theta$the realised value $t(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ of the estimator. A number, not random.

Notation used from here on: $\hat\theta$ or $\tilde\theta$ for an estimator. The course writes $\tilde\theta$ for method-of-moments and analogy estimators and $\hat\theta$ for maximum likelihood, though the exercise sheets are not always consistent.

METHOD Analogy estimation (notes p.45)— ⊡ ✕

Intuition: estimate a population quantity with the analogous quantity computed on the sample.

populationsample (analogy estimator)
mean $\mu=\mathbb E(X)$sample mean $\bar X$
median $m$sample median $M_e$
proportion $p$sample proportion $\bar X$ (of 0/1 data)
variance $\sigma^2=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]$$\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$
quantile $\pi_p$sample quantile

The variance needs care. The literal analogue of $\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]$ would be $\frac1n\sum(X_i-\mu)^2$, but that contains the unknown $\mu$. It is not a statistic (module 29), and therefore not an estimator. So $\mu$ is itself replaced by its analogue $\bar X$.

Is $\hat\sigma^2$ centred on $\sigma^2$? Multiply and divide by $n-1$ to make $S^2$ appear (notes p.45):

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb E(\hat\sigma^2)&=\mathbb E\Big[\frac{n-1}{n-1}\cdot\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\Big]=\mathbb E\Big[\frac{n-1}n\underbrace{\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2}_{S^2}\Big]\\&=\frac{n-1}n\,\mathbb E(S^2)=\boxed{\frac{n-1}n\,\sigma^2}\end{aligned}$$

On average $\hat\sigma^2$ falls short of $\sigma^2$ by $\frac{\sigma^2}n$: it underestimates the variance. Its bias (module 35) is $\mathbb E(\hat\sigma^2)-\sigma^2=-\frac{\sigma^2}n<0$. The bias vanishes as $n\to\infty$; module 36 calls that "asymptotically unbiased".

Limitations (notes p.45)
1. There are no guarantees that analogy estimators work well: $\hat\sigma^2$ is already biased.
2. Sometimes parameters have no clear interpretation. What is "the sample analogue" of the $\alpha$ in $f(x)=\alpha x^{\alpha-1}$, or of the shape of a Gamma? We need a general recipe. The method of moments (module 32) and maximum likelihood (module 33) are two such recipes.
EXERCISE SET 2 (homework 21/11) · Q2 · salaries of 5 men and 5 women

Men $X$: $25,19,16,17,23$. Women $Y$: $15,18,21,20,24$ (thousand EUR).

  1. (a) Analogy estimate of the median of male salary. The analogy estimator of the population median is the sample median: sorted $16,17,19,23,25$, $n=5$ odd, $M_e=Y_3=\mathbf{19}$ (answer B).
  2. (b) Analogy estimate of the variance of female salary. $\bar y=19.6$, $\hat\sigma^2=\frac15\sum(y_i-19.6)^2=\frac{45.2}5=\mathbf{9.04}$. (On the sheet the closest option is 9; the exact value is 9.04.)
  1. (c) Expected value of that estimator. $\mathbb E(\hat\sigma^2)=\frac{n-1}n\sigma^2$ (answer C).
  2. (d) Unbiased? $B_{\sigma^2}(\hat\sigma^2)=\frac{n-1}n\sigma^2-\sigma^2=-\frac1n\sigma^2<0$: biased, it underestimates the population variance.
  3. (e) Asymptotically unbiased? $\lim_{n\to\infty}\big(-\frac1n\sigma^2\big)=0$: yes, the bias vanishes as the sample grows.
R Analogy estimates in R— ⊡ ✕

y <- c(15, 18, 21, 20, 24)
mean(y) → 19.6
median(c(25,19,16,17,23)) → 19
mean((y - mean(y))^2) → 9.04 (analogy $\hat\sigma^2$)
var(y) → 11.3 (that is $S^2$: $9.04\cdot\frac54$)
var(y) * (length(y) - 1) / length(y) → 9.04

TRAP
There is no built-in R function for $\hat\sigma^2$. If an R question writes var(x), it means $S^2$.
TRAP "Biased" is not "bad", and "unbiased" is not "good"— ⊡ ✕

It is tempting to throw $\hat\sigma^2$ away now. Don't:

  • Module 32 shows that $\hat\sigma^2$ is also the method-of-moments estimator of $\sigma^2$ for normal data.
  • Module 34 shows it is the maximum likelihood estimator too.
  • Module 35 shows that for normal data it has a smaller mean squared error than the unbiased $S^2$. The lecturer's exam warning there is literally "choose an estimator because of lower bias? No."
