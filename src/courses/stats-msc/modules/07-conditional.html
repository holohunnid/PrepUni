07

Conditional probability

lecture 1, notes p.9–12 · slides: Conditional 2–9
READ FIRST New information changes probabilities— ⊡ ✕

Probabilities are not fixed facts about the world — they depend on what you know. If you learn that the book on the floor does not belong to an Italian student, the chance that it belongs to a Chinese student goes up. If you learn the stock went up yesterday, the chance of two up-moves in a row changes.

The lecture introduces the idea with two examples before giving any formula, and so does this module. In both, the same thing happens: the outcomes ruled out by the information are thrown away, and the probabilities of what is left are scaled up so that they sum to 1 again. The formula $P(A\mid B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$ is just that sentence written in symbols.

Read the bar as "given"
$P(A\mid B)$ is "the probability of $A$ given $B$": we take $B$ as known to have happened (whether or not it really has — we can condition on hypotheticals). $P(A)$ without a bar is the unconditional probability.
EX LECTURE 1 · notes p.9–10 · slides Conditional 2 — the book in the classroom— ⊡ ✕

A classroom has 8 Chinese, 7 Italian and 5 Korean students. A book is found. What is the probability that it belongs to a Chinese student?

  1. Sample space. $\Omega=\{C_1,\dots,C_8,\ I_1,\dots,I_7,\ K_1,\dots,K_5\}$, where $C_i$ = "the book belongs to the $i$-th Chinese student" (similarly $I_i$, $K_i$). $|\Omega|=20$.
  2. Event. $E=\{C_1,\dots,C_8\}$. Assuming equally likely owners (module 05): $$P(E)=\frac{|E|}{|\Omega|}=\frac{8}{20}=0.4\qquad\text{— the unconditional probability.}$$
  3. New information: the book does not belong to an Italian. Then $P(I_1)=\dots=P(I_7)=0$. If the remaining 13 outcomes stay equally likely, each now has probability $\frac1{13}$: $$P(C_1)=\dots=P(C_8)=P(K_1)=\dots=P(K_5)=\tfrac1{13}.$$
  4. Answer. $P(E\mid\text{book not of }I_1,\dots,I_7)=\dfrac{8}{13}\approx0.615$.

The slides add a footnote: writing plain $P$ in step 3 is "somehow ambiguous" — those are the probabilities after conditioning, a new probability measure. The $P(\cdot\mid B)$ notation below fixes that.

EX LECTURE 1 · notes p.10–11 · slides Conditional 3–5 — a stock price in two steps— ⊡ ✕

At $t=0$ a stock costs $P_0$. Each period it moves up by a factor $u>1$ with probability $p_u=\tfrac23$, or down by $d=\tfrac1u<1$ with probability $p_d=\tfrac13$. So $P_1\in\{P_0u,\ P_0d\}$ and $P_2\in\{P_1u,\ P_1d\}$. What is the probability that $P_2=P_0u^2$?

  1. Sample space — pairs (price at $t=1$, price at $t=2$): $$\Omega=\{(P_0u,P_0u^2),\ (P_0u,P_0),\ (P_0d,P_0),\ (P_0d,P_0d^2)\}$$ Note $P_0ud=P_0$ because $d=1/u$: up-then-down returns to the start (the tree "recombines").
  2. Probabilities multiply along a path: $$\begin{aligned}P((P_0u,P_0u^2))&=p_up_u=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac49\\P((P_0u,P_0))&=p_up_d=\tfrac29\\P((P_0d,P_0))&=p_dp_u=\tfrac29\\P((P_0d,P_0d^2))&=p_dp_d=\tfrac19\end{aligned}$$ (they sum to $\tfrac99=1$ ✓), so $P(P_2=P_0u^2)=\tfrac49$.
  3. Now suppose we know the first move was up. The two paths starting with $P_0d$ become impossible: $P((P_0d,P_0))=0=P((P_0d,P_0d^2))$. What remains is the second step alone: $$P((P_0u,P_0u^2))=p_u=\tfrac23,\qquad P((P_0u,P_0))=p_d=\tfrac13.$$
  4. "More precisely", as the notes say, that first number is $P\big((P_0u,P_0u^2)\,\big|\,P_1=P_0u\big)=\tfrac23$.
Finance
This is the binomial tree used to price options: $u$, $d$ and the step probabilities fix the distribution of the price at maturity. With the formula below you can check the conditional answer: $P(\text{two ups}\mid\text{first up})=\frac{P(\text{two ups})}{P(\text{first up})}=\frac{4/9}{2/3}=\frac23$ ✓.
DEF Conditional probability— ⊡ ✕

Let $A$ and $B$ be two events with $P(B)>0$. The conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}}$$
  • Numerator $P(A\cap B)$: of the outcomes still possible (those in $B$), keep the ones where $A$ also happens.
  • Denominator $P(B)$: rescale, so that the new world $B$ has total probability 1.

Check it on the classroom: $P(E\mid\text{not }I)=\dfrac{P(E\cap\text{not }I)}{P(\text{not }I)}=\dfrac{8/20}{13/20}=\dfrac{8}{13}$ ✓ — the same answer the "throw away and rescale" reasoning gave.

P(B) > 0 is required
You cannot condition on an impossible event — you would divide by 0.
A conditional probability is a probability
Fix $B$: then $A\mapsto P(A\mid B)$ satisfies all three axioms, with $B$ playing the role of $\Omega$. So every rule of module 05 has a conditional twin, e.g. $P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)$.
RULE Law of multiplication and the chain rule— ⊡ ✕

Multiply the definition through by $P(B)$ — and, swapping the roles of $A$ and $B$, by $P(A)$:

$$\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)=P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}$$

This law of multiplication runs the definition backwards: it builds a joint probability from a conditional one — which is usually what a problem hands you ("the second step, given the first"). It is exactly what we did along the branches of the stock tree.

For three events, apply it twice:

$$P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)\,P(C\mid A\cap B)$$

and for $m$ events — the chain rule:

$$P(A_1\cap\dots\cap A_m)=P(A_1)\,P(A_2\mid A_1)\,P(A_3\mid A_2\cap A_1)\cdots P(A_m\mid A_{m-1}\cap\dots\cap A_1)$$

Read it as a story told in order: the first thing happens, then the second given the first, then the third given the first two…

SLIDES · Conditional 8–9 — the party robbers

200 guests (you included) draw from a hat with 199 blank papers and one "you lose"; whoever draws it is robbed. The robbers let you choose your turn. When should you draw?

  1. First: $P(E_1)=\tfrac1{200}$.
  2. Second — the chain rule, since the second person can only lose if the first drew a blank: $$P(E_2)=\frac{199}{200}\cdot\frac1{199}=\frac1{200}.$$
  3. Third: $P(E_3)=\frac{199}{200}\cdot\frac{198}{199}\cdot\frac1{198}=\frac1{200}$. The fractions telescope — every numerator cancels the next denominator.
  4. Last: $P(E_{200})=\frac{199}{200}\cdot\frac{198}{199}\cdots\frac12\cdot\frac11=\frac1{200}$.
Insight
It does not matter when you draw: everyone has the same $\tfrac1{200}$. Drawing late means you need all the others to survive first, which exactly compensates the better odds at the end.
TRAP P(A | B) is not P(B | A)— ⊡ ✕

The bar is not symmetric. In the classroom, $P(\text{Chinese}\mid\text{not Italian})=\frac8{13}$, but $P(\text{not Italian}\mid\text{Chinese})=1$ — every Chinese student is not Italian. Mixing the two up is the "prosecutor's fallacy", and module 10 (Bayes) shows it can be off by a factor of 50: a test that is positive 99% of the time given a disease does not mean you have the disease with 99% probability given a positive test.
