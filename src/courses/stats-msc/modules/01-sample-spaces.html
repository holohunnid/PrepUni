01

Sample spaces & events

lecture 1, notes p.1–5 · slides: Axioms 2–3, 9–10
READ FIRST The question this module answers— ⊡ ✕

Before we can say "the probability of X is 0.3" we need to say what X is. This module builds the vocabulary that every later formula is written in. It has only three words, and the whole course rests on them:

  • random experiment — something whose outcome is uncertain, but whose possible outcomes we can list in advance (toss a die: we don't know the face, but we know it is one of six);
  • sample space $\Omega$ — the set of all possible outcomes;
  • event — a subset of $\Omega$, i.e. a collection of outcomes we care about ("the face is even").

The big move is the last one: events are sets. That sounds pedantic, but it buys us an entire algebra for free — "A or B" becomes a union, "A and B" an intersection, "not A" a complement (module 02) — and probability will be defined as a function that eats sets (module 04).

One sentence to remember
An event happens when the outcome $\omega$ that actually occurs lies inside it. Rolling a 4 makes "even" happen and "less than 3" not happen — same roll, different events.
DEF Sample space and outcomes— ⊡ ✕

The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. Its elements are the outcomes, written $\omega\in\Omega$.

  • Ex 1 — toss a die. $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, with outcomes $\omega_1=1,\dots,\omega_6=6$.
  • Ex 2 — flip a coin. $\Omega=\{H,T\}$, with $\omega_1=H$, $\omega_2=T$.
DEF Events are subsets of Ω— ⊡ ✕

Subsets of $\Omega$ are called events, written with capital letters ($E$, $A$, $B$, …). In Ex 1 (the die):

$$\begin{aligned} A&=\{\text{even number}\}=\{2,4,6\}\subset\Omega\\ B&=\{\text{number}<3\}=\{1,2\}\\ C&=\{\text{negative number}\}=\{\ \}=\emptyset\\ D&=\{\text{number}<10\}=\Omega \end{aligned}$$

Two events get names of their own: the empty set $\emptyset$ is the impossible event (it contains no outcome, so it can never happen) and $\Omega$ itself is the sure event (it contains every outcome, so it always happens).

IDEA How big can Ω be? Finite, countably infinite, uncountable— ⊡ ✕

The die has 6 outcomes and two dice have $6^2=36$ (ordered pairs $(x,y)$, one red die and one blue die). But $|\Omega|$ — the number of outcomes — does not have to be finite. The lecture gives two examples that go beyond, and the distinction between them comes back in module 15 (discrete vs continuous random variables).

The stubborn coin flipper — countably infinite

Toss a coin until the first head. You might stop at once, or after one tail, or after two… so

$$\Omega=\{H,\ TH,\ TTH,\ TTTH,\ \dots\}$$

Infinitely many outcomes, but you can count them: the 1st, the 2nd, the 3rd… Every outcome gets a natural number.

Lifetime of a light bulb — uncountable

Measure how many hours a bulb lasts. Any non-negative real number is possible:

$$\Omega=[0,+\infty)$$

Now you cannot list the outcomes one by one — between any two lifetimes there are infinitely many others. Events are typically intervals (module 02 works them out):

Why this distinction will matter
On a finite or countable $\Omega$ we can give every single outcome its own probability and add them up. On an uncountable $\Omega$ like $[0,\infty)$ we cannot: single points will turn out to have probability 0 (module 06), and probability has to live on intervals instead. That split — countable vs uncountable — is exactly the split between discrete and continuous random variables in module 15.
LECTURE 1 · notes p.2 · slides Axioms 3 — reading an event off a sentence

Most exam mistakes in this chapter are translation mistakes. The recipe the lecture uses is always the same: write $\Omega$ first, then collect the outcomes that make the sentence true.

  1. "Observing an even number." Go through $\Omega=\{1,\dots,6\}$ and keep those that are even: $\{2,4,6\}$.
  2. "Observing a number less than 3." Keep 1 and 2: $\{1,2\}$. (Strictly less — 3 itself is out.)
  3. "Observing a negative number." No face is negative, so nothing is kept: $\emptyset$. The sentence is legal, the event just can't happen.
  4. "Observing a number less than 10." Every face qualifies: the event is all of $\Omega$.
Insight
Events are about which outcomes, not about how likely they are. $\emptyset$ and $\Omega$ are perfectly good events; probability is attached later, in module 04.
?check yourselfTwo coins
You flip two coins. Write $\Omega$, and the event "at least one head".
Answer
$\Omega=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}$ — order matters, so $HT$ and $TH$ are different outcomes (first coin, second coin). "At least one head" $=\{HH,HT,TH\}$: everything except $TT$.
?check yourselfIs it an event?
For one die, is $\{7\}$ an event? Is $\{2,5\}$? Is $\Omega$?
Answer
$\{7\}$ is not — 7 is not an outcome, so $\{7\}\not\subset\Omega$ (the event "rolling a 7" is written $\emptyset$). $\{2,5\}$ is (any subset is). $\Omega$ is — the sure event.
