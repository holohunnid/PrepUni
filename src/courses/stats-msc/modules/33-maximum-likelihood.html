Maximum likelihood: idea & recipe

lecture 7, notes pp.48–50 · Point Estimation deck §5.2–5.3
READ FIRST Choose the $\theta$ under which your data were most probable— ⊡ ✕

Module 27 computed $f(\underline x;\theta)=\prod f(x_i;\theta)$, the probability of the observed sample under a given $\theta$. Now freeze the data and let $\theta$ move. Each candidate $\theta$ gets a score, "how probable would my data have been if this were the truth?" Pick the $\theta$ with the highest score. That is the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE).

The computation is an ordinary one-variable maximisation: take logs, differentiate, set to zero, check the second derivative, check the boundaries. The MLE is the most widely used estimator because of its properties (modules 34 and 36).

EXAM (lecturer, notes pp.50 and 52)
"Find the MLE" is the open question of the exam. Expect one parameter to be found. For multi-parameter models expect only theory questions, no exercises. Every step of the recipe below earns marks: the log-likelihood, the first-order condition, the second-order check, and the boundaries.
LECTURE 7 Intuition (notes p.48)— ⊡ ✕

Find the value of $\theta$ at which the probability of the observed data is maximised.

Example: we observe $\underline x=(0.001,\ 0.0013,\ -0.003)$. Two candidate models:

  • $N(\mu=0,\ \sigma^2=1)$: the data sit right at the centre of the bell, so this one is more coherent with $\underline x$;
  • $N(\mu=10,\ \sigma^2=1)$: the data would be 10 standard deviations below the mean, so it is less coherent with $\underline x$.

Formally $f(\underline x;\mu=0,\sigma^2=1)>f(\underline x;\mu=10,\sigma^2=1)$: the observed $\underline x$ is more likely to be observed under $N(0,1)$, so $\mu=0$ should be preferred over $\mu=10$.

DEF The likelihood function and the MLE (notes p.48 · deck p.33)— ⊡ ✕

We define the likelihood function as

$$\boxed{\;L(\theta;\underline x)=\prod_{i=1}^nf(x_i;\theta)\;}$$

It is the joint pmf/pdf of $\underline X$ evaluated at the data. The difference is that it is read as a function of $\theta$, not of $\underline x$. If $L(\theta_1;\underline x)>L(\theta_2;\underline x)$ then we prefer $\theta_1$ to $\theta_2$: there is more evidence in the data for $\theta_1$.

$$\boxed{\;\hat\theta=\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}L(\theta;\underline x)\;}$$

Search over all $\theta$ in the parameter space $\Theta$: $\hat\theta$ is the point where $L$ is maximum.

Max. likelihood estimator$\hat\theta(\underline X)$, with capital $\underline X$: random
Max. likelihood estimate$\hat\theta(\underline x)$, with small $\underline x$: a number
TRAP · the likelihood is not a density in $\theta$
$L(\theta)$ does not integrate to 1 over $\theta$, and $L(\hat\theta)$ is not "the probability that $\theta=\hat\theta$". It is a probability of the data (or a density), indexed by $\theta$. Only comparisons $L(\theta_1)$ vs $L(\theta_2)$ have a meaning.
RECIPE In practice: maximise the log-likelihood (notes p.49 · deck §5.3)— ⊡ ✕

It is simpler to maximise the log-likelihood:

$$\boxed{\;\ell(\theta;\underline x)=\ln L(\theta;\underline x)=\ln\prod_{i=1}^nf(x_i;\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\ln f(x_i;\theta)\;}$$
  • Analytically easier: the log turns the product into a sum, and sums are easy to differentiate.
  • Avoids computational problems: a product of 1000 numbers below 1 underflows to 0 on a computer; a sum of logs does not.

$L$ or $\ell$ will give the same $\hat\theta$, since $\ln$ is a monotone (increasing) transformation: whatever maximises $L$ maximises $\ln L$.

  1. FOC. Find $\hat\theta$ by setting $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ell(\theta;\underline x)=0$ and solving for $\theta$.
  2. SOC. Check $\dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\ell(\theta;\underline x)\Big|_{\theta=\hat\theta}<0$, so that it is a maximum.
  3. Boundaries. Finally check the values of $\ell(\theta;\underline x)$ at the boundaries of $\Theta$. A local maximum is only global if the function is lower at the edges. Typically $\ell\to-\infty$ there.
Two routes to $\ell$ (notes p.49)
Route 1: $f(x_i;\theta)\to L(\theta;\underline x)\to\ell(\theta;\underline x)$: build the product, then take the log.
Route 2 (faster if you are looking for $\ell$): $f(x_i;\theta)\to\ln f(x_i;\theta)\to\ell=\sum_i\ln f(x_i;\theta)$: take the log of one density, then sum. If the question asks for capital $L$, use route 1.
LECTURE 7 · notes pp.49–50 · deck pp.41–42 · MLE of p for a Bernoulli sample

$X\sim\text{Bern}(p)$, $p\in[0,1]$, $f(x;p)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x}$, $x\in\{0,1\}$. $\underline X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ i.i.d.

  1. Likelihood (route 1). $$L(p;\underline x)=\prod_{i=1}^np^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i}=p^{\sum x_i}(1-p)^{\sum(1-x_i)}=p^{\sum x_i}(1-p)^{n-\sum x_i}$$
  2. Log-likelihood. $$\ell(p;\underline x)=\ln p^{\sum x_i}+\ln(1-p)^{n-\sum x_i}=\sum x_i\ln p+\Big(n-\sum x_i\Big)\ln(1-p)$$ Route 2 gives the same: $\ln f(x;p)=x\ln p+(1-x)\ln(1-p)$, and $\sum_i$ of it is the line above.
  3. FOC. $$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial p}=\frac{\sum x_i}p+\frac{n-\sum x_i}{1-p}\cdot(-1)=\frac{\sum x_i}p-\frac{n-\sum x_i}{1-p}\overset{\text{fix}}=0$$ With $\sum x_i=n\bar x$ and dividing by $n$: $\frac{\bar x}{\hat p}-\frac{1-\bar x}{1-\hat p}=0$. Common denominator: $\frac{\bar x-\bar x\hat p-\hat p+\bar x\hat p}{\hat p(1-\hat p)}=0\Rightarrow\bar x-\hat p=0\Rightarrow\boxed{\hat p=\bar x}$
  1. SOC. $$\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial p^2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\Big(\frac{\sum x_i}p-\frac{n-\sum x_i}{1-p}\Big)=-\frac{\sum x_i}{p^2}-\frac{n-\sum x_i}{(1-p)^2}<0$$ Both terms are $\le0$ for every $p$, so $\bar x$ is a maximum point.
  2. Boundaries, $\Theta=[0,1]$ (when $0<\sum x_i<n$): $$\lim_{p\to0}\ell=\lim\Big[\sum x_i\underbrace{\ln p}_{\to-\infty}+\big(n-\textstyle\sum x_i\big)\underbrace{\ln(1-p)}_{\to0}\Big]=-\infty$$ and in the same way $\lim_{p\to1}\ell=-\infty$ (now $\ln(1-p)\to-\infty$). So the interior maximum is global.
  3. Conclusion. The MLE is the sample proportion, $\hat p=\bar X$. It is also the MME and the analogy estimator.
TRAP · all zeros or all ones (deck pp.34–36)
If $\underline x=(1,1,1)$ then $L(p)=p^3$, which is increasing on $[0,1]$, so $\hat p=1$, on the boundary. Setting $\frac{dL}{dp}=3p^2=0$ gives $p=0$, the wrong answer: there the derivative is zero but the likelihood is at its minimum. Likewise $(0,0,0)$ gives $L=(1-p)^3$, so $\hat p=0$. The formula $\hat p=\bar x$ still gives the right answers (1 and 0). The FOC route simply can't find them, which is why step 5 of the recipe exists.
INTERACTIVE Move $\theta$, watch $L$ and $\ell$: same maximiser, different shapes— ⊡ ✕

Try this. Bernoulli $(1,0,1)$: the peak is at $\hat p=2/3$, where $\ell'(p)=\frac2p-\frac1{1-p}=0$ (the deck's computation). Switch to $(1,1,1)$: the curve rises all the way to $p=1$ and the slope there is not zero. The normal preset reproduces the deck's plot, with its maximum at $\hat\mu=1.35$ and $L(1)>L(2)$. Poisson $(2,4,3,3)$: compare $L(3.5)=0.00163$ (module 27) with the larger $L(3)$. The score readout is $\ell'(\theta)$; it is zero only at the green line.

?check yourselfThe deck's (1, 0, 1)
Bernoulli sample $(1,0,1)$. Write $L(p)$, $\ell(p)$, and find $\hat p$ with the FOC.
Answer
$L=p\cdot(1-p)\cdot p=p^2(1-p)$; $\ell=2\ln p+\ln(1-p)$; $\ell'=\frac2p-\frac1{1-p}=0\Rightarrow2(1-p)=p\Rightarrow\hat p=\frac23$. The interior maximum, $=\bar x$.
?check yourselfWhy the log is allowed
Could we maximise $L^2$, or $\sqrt L$, or $-L$ instead of $L$?
Answer
$L^2$ and $\sqrt L$: yes. Since $L\ge0$, both are increasing transformations of $L$ and keep the same maximiser. $-L$: no, that is decreasing, so maximising $-L$ minimises $L$. The log is chosen because it turns products into sums.
