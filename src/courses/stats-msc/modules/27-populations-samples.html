READ FIRST The turn from probability to statistics— ⊡ ✕

Modules 01–26 all ran in one direction. The distribution was given (a fair die, a Poisson with $\lambda=3.5$, an $N(5,4)$) and we computed probabilities of data. From this module on the direction flips:

Probability (modules 01–26)model known → what data will we see?"$X\sim\text{Pois}(3.5)$. What is $P(X=2)$?"
Statistics (from here)data seen → which model produced it?"We counted $2,4,3,3$. What is $\lambda$?"

This module sets up the vocabulary the rest of the course uses: population, sample, statistical model $\{f(x;\theta),\ \theta\in\Theta\}$, parameter space, i.i.d. random sample, and the difference between the random sample $\underline X$ and the observed sample $\underline x$. The one formula to take away is the joint pmf/pdf of an i.i.d. sample, the product of the marginals. Module 33 renames that product the likelihood.

What you need for the exam
Write the model and its parameter space for a given story. Know that i.i.d. means "independent" and "identically distributed". Compute $f(\underline x;\theta)=\prod_i f(x_i;\theta)$ for a small observed sample. Most exam questions in the estimation block start with exactly this setup.
LECTURE 5 What the lecturer said (notes p.36)— ⊡ ✕

Statistical inference: the process of coming to some conclusion about a population, based on data collected from a sample.

  • Population: the set of all statistical units. It can be
    • finite, e.g. the population of human beings, or of the companies in a country;
    • infinite, e.g. the set of outcomes of a physical experiment (you can always repeat it once more).
    The course works with infinite populations, which are also a good approximation of large finite ones.
  • Sample: the subset of the population that we actually observe. In practice it is a dataset: one row per statistical unit ($1,\dots,n$, with $n$ the sample size; the population size $N$ is different, and for us $N=+\infty$), one column per feature.

Each column is treated as $n$ repetitions of a random variable, and is described by a pmf or pdf. But we don't know the specific form of that distribution. For example we want an exponential model, but we don't know $\lambda$. So we assume the distribution belongs to a family, a statistical model indexed by a parameter $\theta$:

$$\boxed{\;\{f(x;\theta),\ \theta\in\Theta\}\;}$$

$\theta$ is the parameter. $\Theta$ is the parameter space, the set of all possible parameter values. Making inference means learning the most reasonable value for $\theta$ from the sample.

LECTURE 5 · notes p.37 · deck p.3 · Ex 1: employees per company

$X$ = number of employees in a company (the company is the statistical unit). Assume $X\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda$ unknown.

  1. Model. $\{f(x;\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}}{x!},\ \lambda\in\Theta\}$.
  2. Parameter space. A Poisson mean must be positive: $\lambda>0\Rightarrow\Theta=(0,+\infty)$.
  3. Data. We collect $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, say $n=100$ companies, and learn $\lambda$. The deck already hints at the answer: estimate $\lambda$ by the average number of employees of the 100 companies (since $\mathbb E(X)=\lambda$, module 20). Modules 32 and 34 prove that this is what both estimation methods give.
LECTURE 5 · notes p.37 · deck p.4 · Ex 2: length of a metal bar

$X$ = length of a metal bar produced by an industrial process. $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with both parameters unknown.

  1. Parameter. A vector this time: $\theta=(\mu,\sigma^2)$.
  2. Parameter space. $\Theta=\{(\mu,\sigma^2):\ -\infty<\mu<+\infty,\ \sigma^2>0\}$, a half-plane.
  3. Data. Measure $n=50$ bars, learn $\theta$. Natural guesses: the mean and the variance of the 50 lengths.
  4. Using what we know. A length can't be negative: $P(X<0)=0$ for every unit. So we may restrict to $\Theta'=\{(\mu,\sigma^2):\ \mu>0\}$. (Strictly, a normal always puts some mass below 0. The restriction just says that "$\mu$ negative" is not a sensible model for lengths.)
Insight
The parameter space carries real information. Module 33 shows that the maximum of the likelihood must be searched inside $\Theta$, boundaries included, and exercise set 3 has a case ($\mu\ge0$) where that changes the answer.
DEF Random sample: extraction, i.i.d., and $\underline X$ versus $\underline x$ (notes p.37 · deck §2)— ⊡ ✕

We collect a random sample: a subset of the population whose statistical units are extracted at random,

  • with replacement: a unit extracted for the sample can be extracted again;
  • without replacement: once extracted, a unit is removed.

In big populations ($N$ large) the distinction is irrelevant, and for an infinite population (a repeatable experiment) it does not even arise. The deck adds: in a finite population, you need replacement for the units to be independent.

So a random sample of size $n$ is $\{X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n\}$ where the $X_i$ are assumed i.i.d.:

  • independent: $X_i$ does not affect $X_j$ for $j\neq i$ (module 09);
  • identically distributed: every $X_i$ has the same pmf/pdf, the population's $f(x;\theta)$.

We have to distinguish two objects with the same letters:

$\underline X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$random sample: $n$ random variables, before we look. Capital letters.
$\underline x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$observed sample: the realised values of the random sample, plain numbers. Small letters.

The sample space $\mathcal X$ is the set of all possible samples: $\text{Supp}(X)^n$.

TRAP · capital or small letter
The same distinction comes back in every later module: an estimator $T(\underline X)$ is random and has a distribution, a mean and a variance; an estimate $T(\underline x)$ is a number. Writing $\mathbb E(\bar x)$ for an observed $\bar x=3.2$ is meaningless, because the expectation of a number is the number itself. The lecturer is strict about the capital letters.
THEOREM The joint pmf/pdf of an i.i.d. sample is the product of the marginals (notes p.38)— ⊡ ✕

To recover the pmf/pdf of the whole sample $\underline X$, exploit the i.i.d. assumption. Independence turns the joint probability into a product (module 09). Identical distribution makes every factor the same function $f(\cdot;\theta)$:

$$\boxed{\;f(\underline x;\theta)=f(x_1;\theta)\,f(x_2;\theta)\cdots f(x_n;\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}f(x_i;\theta)\;}$$

Read it as: the probability of observing exactly this sample (for discrete $X$), or its density (for continuous $X$), under the parameter value $\theta$.

From the deck (not lectured): if $f$ is in the exponential family, $f(x;\theta)=h(x)c(\theta)\exp\{\sum_jw_j(\theta)t_j(x)\}$, then so is the joint distribution of the sample, with $t_j(\underline x)=\sum_it_j(x_i)$ and $d(\theta)=c(\theta)^n$. This is why sums such as $\sum x_i$ keep appearing in the estimators of modules 32–34.

LECTURE 5 · notes p.38 · deck pp.6–7 · a Poisson sample (2, 4, 3, 3)

$X\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda=3.5$, so $f(x;\lambda)=\frac{\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}}{x!}$, $x=0,1,\dots$ We observe $\underline x=(2,4,3,3)$. How probable was that?

  1. Spaces. $\text{Supp}(X)=\{0,1,2,\dots\}=\mathbb N_0$, infinite and countable, so $\mathcal X=\mathbb N_0^4$, all 4-tuples of non-negative integers.
  2. Product of the marginals. $$f(\underline x;\lambda)=\prod_{i=1}^4f(x_i;\lambda)=\frac{3.5^2e^{-3.5}}{2!}\cdot\frac{3.5^4e^{-3.5}}{4!}\cdot\frac{3.5^3e^{-3.5}}{3!}\cdot\frac{3.5^3e^{-3.5}}{3!}$$
  3. Numbers. $0.1850\cdot0.1888\cdot0.2158\cdot0.2158=\mathbf{0.00163}$.
  4. Compactly. $f(\underline x;\lambda)=\dfrac{\lambda^{\sum x_i}e^{-n\lambda}}{\prod x_i!}=\dfrac{3.5^{12}e^{-14}}{2!\,4!\,3!\,3!}$. The data enter only through $\sum x_i=12$ (and through the constant $\prod x_i!$).
R corner
prod(dpois(c(2,4,3,3), lambda = 3.5)) → 0.00162611. Change 3.5 to 3: 0.00189, larger. So $\lambda=3$ "explains" this sample better than $3.5$. Turning that comparison into a method is exactly maximum likelihood (module 33), and the winner will be $\bar x=12/4=3$.
TRAP Three slips with samples— ⊡ ✕
  • Multiplying is only allowed for independent units. $f(\underline x)=\prod f(x_i)$ needs independence. Daily returns of one stock over consecutive days, or draws without replacement from a small urn, may not satisfy it.
  • Order matters in $\mathcal X$. $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ are different samples, each with probability $0.03$. The event "one manager with 0 cars and one with 1" has probability $0.06$.
  • $n$ is not $N$. $n$ is how many units we observed; $N$ is the population size (for us infinite). Formulas such as $\mathbb V(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$ (module 29) use the sample size $n$.

For each story give the model and $\Theta$: (a) whether each of 200 loans defaults; (b) the waiting time between two trades, assumed memoryless; (c) daily returns assumed Gaussian with known variance 0.04.
Answer
(a) $\text{Bern}(p)$, $\Theta=[0,1]$. (b) $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$, $\Theta=(0,\infty)$ (module 24: memoryless). (c) $N(\mu,0.04)$, $\theta=\mu$, $\Theta=\mathbb R$. Only $\mu$ is unknown, so it is a one-parameter model.
?check yourselfProbability of a sample
$X\sim\text{Bern}(p)$ with $p=0.3$. Compute $f(\underline x;p)$ for $\underline x=(1,0,0,1,0)$. Then say for which $p$ the same formula is largest.
Answer
$f=p^2(1-p)^3=0.09\cdot0.343=0.03087$. As a function of $p$, $p^2(1-p)^3$ is maximised at $p=2/5$, the proportion of ones. Module 33 derives this in general: $\hat p=\bar x$.
