Mean & variance of $\bar X$ and $S^2$

lecture 6, notes pp.40–41 · Point Estimation deck §3.1 · exercise set 1
READ FIRST A statistic is a random variable, so it has a distribution— ⊡ ✕

Draw a sample, compute $\bar x$. Draw another sample from the same population, and you get a different $\bar x$. Repeat forever and the values of $\bar x$ form a distribution, the sampling distribution of $\bar X$. Every statistic has one.

Finding the full sampling distribution is often impossible. But its mean and variance are easy for the two statistics we care most about, and they hold whatever the population:

$$\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu,\qquad \mathbb V(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}n,\qquad \mathbb E(S^2)=\sigma^2.$$

These three lines drive the whole estimation block. $\bar X$ is centred on the right value; its spread shrinks like $1/n$, so more data means a less variable $\bar X$; and dividing by $n-1$ is exactly what makes $S^2$ centred on $\sigma^2$.

What you need for the exam
The three boxed results and the two-line proofs for $\bar X$ (linearity, then independence). Know that $\mathbb E(S)\le\sigma$ (Jensen) and the formula for $\mathbb V(S^2)$, which simplifies to $\frac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}$ for normal data. The last one is used for MSE in module 35.
LECTURE 6 What the lecturer said (notes p.40)— ⊡ ✕

Note 1: sample statistics are RVs.

Note 2: $\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)^2$ is not a statistic, since it depends on the unknown $\mu$: we don't know its realised value even after we observe $\underline x$.

The distribution of a sample statistic is called a sampling distribution:

$$Y=t(X_1,\dots,X_n)\ \Rightarrow\ F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)=P\big(t(X_1,\dots,X_n)\le y\big).$$

Often it is not possible to derive $F_Y$, but there are some exceptions (module 30). The moments, however, we can always get.

THEOREM $\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu$, regardless of the distribution of $X$— ⊡ ✕
$$\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mathbb E\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\Big)\overset{\text{linearity}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E(X_i)\overset{\text{i.d.}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mu=\frac1n\,n\mu=\boxed{\mu}$$

Two ingredients: linearity of $\mathbb E$ (module 16), and that every $X_i$ has the same mean $\mu$. Independence is not needed for this one.

Exercise set 1, Q1
$X_1,\dots,X_n$ have means $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n$. When is $\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu_1$? Answer B: when $\mu_i=\mu_1$ for all $i$. "It exists one $j$ with $\mu_j=\mu_1$" is not enough, and independence (D) is irrelevant for a mean.
THEOREM $\mathbb V(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$, regardless of the distribution of $X$— ⊡ ✕
$$\mathbb V(\bar X)=\mathbb V\Big(\frac1n\sum X_i\Big)=\frac1{n^2}\mathbb V\Big(\sum X_i\Big)\overset{\text{indep.}}=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb V(X_i)\overset{\text{i.d.}}=\frac1{n^2}\,n\sigma^2=\boxed{\frac{\sigma^2}n}$$

Here $\sigma^2$ is the population variance. The constant comes out squared (module 17). Independence is what lets the variance of the sum split into the sum of the variances.

More data ⇒ less variable $\bar X$: quadruple $n$ and the standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$ of $\bar X$ halves. This $\sigma/\sqrt n$ is called the standard error of the mean, and it reappears in every confidence interval (modules 37–38).

THEOREM $\mathbb E(S^2)=\sigma^2$, and what it does not say about $S$ (notes pp.40–41)— ⊡ ✕

The notes skip the steps; here they are. Start from the shortcut of module 28, $\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\sum X_i^2-n\bar X^2$, so

$$S^2=\frac1{n-1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2-n\bar X^2\Big).$$

Now use $\mathbb E(Y^2)=\mathbb V(Y)+\mathbb E(Y)^2$ twice: $\mathbb E(X_i^2)=\sigma^2+\mu^2$ and $\mathbb E(\bar X^2)=\frac{\sigma^2}n+\mu^2$. Then

$$\mathbb E(S^2)=\frac1{n-1}\Big[n(\sigma^2+\mu^2)-n\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}n+\mu^2\Big)\Big]=\frac{(n-1)\sigma^2}{n-1}=\boxed{\sigma^2}$$

Why $n-1$. The deviations are measured from $\bar X$, which is itself fitted to the data, so they come out slightly too small on average. Exactly one "$\sigma^2$" worth is lost, which leaves $n-1$.

But $\mathbb E(S)\le\sigma$. The square root of an unbiased estimator is not unbiased. This is a consequence of Jensen's inequality: for a convex function $\varphi$,

$$\varphi\big(\mathbb E(X)\big)\le\mathbb E\big(\varphi(X)\big).$$

Take $\varphi(s)=s^2$ (convex) and $X=S$:

$$\big(\mathbb E(S)\big)^2\le\mathbb E(S^2)=\sigma^2\ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt{\mathbb E(S)^2}\le\sqrt{\sigma^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\mathbb E(S)\le\sigma}$$

Jensen is the same fact as $\mathbb V(S)=\mathbb E(S^2)-\mathbb E(S)^2\ge0$. The inequality is strict unless $S$ is constant.

THEOREM The variance of $S^2$, and kurtosis (notes p.41 · deck p.9)— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{\;\mathbb V(S^2)=\frac{\sigma^4}n\Big(\beta_2+\frac{2n}{n-1}\Big)\;},\qquad\beta_2=\frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4}-3$$

with $\mu_4=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^4]$ the fourth central moment. $\frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4}$ is the kurtosis, and $\beta_2$ the excess kurtosis: the "intensity of the tails" compared with a normal. Heavy tails make $S^2$ jumpy, because one extreme observation moves it a lot.

Special case $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: the normal has $\beta_2=0$, so

$$\mathbb V(S^2)=\frac{\sigma^4}n\cdot\frac{2n}{n-1}=\boxed{\frac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}}$$

This is the number module 35 plugs into the MSE of $S^2$.

TRAP What the three results do and do not say— ⊡ ✕
  • $\mathbb E(\bar X)=\mu$ is about the random $\bar X$, averaged over all samples. Your one observed $\bar x$ is almost never exactly $\mu$.
  • $\mathbb V(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n$ uses the population $\sigma^2$, not $S^2$. In practice $\sigma^2$ is unknown and gets replaced by $S^2$. That substitution is what turns $N(0,1)$ into Student-t in modules 30 and 38.
  • Nothing here says $\bar X$ is normal. Mean and variance are distribution-free; the shape is not. Normality of $\bar X$ needs normal data, or a large $n$ via the CLT (module 30).
  • $S$ is biased even though $S^2$ is not. Unbiasedness does not survive non-linear functions. Invariance does survive them for MLEs, which is one reason module 34 likes MLEs.
?check yourselfPrecision on demand
Daily returns have $\sigma=2\%$. How many days are needed for the standard error of $\bar X$ to be $0.25\%$?
Answer
$\sigma/\sqrt n=0.25\Rightarrow\sqrt n=2/0.25=8\Rightarrow n=64$ days. Halving it to $0.125\%$ needs $n=256$: four times the data for twice the precision.
