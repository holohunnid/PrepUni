Bayes' theorem

lecture 2, notes p.2–4 · slides: Conditional 18–21
READ FIRST Turning P(evidence | cause) into P(cause | evidence)— ⊡ ✕

Problems usually hand you probabilities in the "forward" direction: how likely a test is to be positive if you are ill, how often a factory makes a defective chip. But the question you care about goes backwards: I tested positive — how likely am I ill? This chip is defective — which factory made it?

Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning. It is nothing new: it is the definition of conditional probability (module 07) with the numerator rewritten by the multiplication law and the denominator by the law of total probability (module 08). Three things you already know, glued together.

The punchline of the lecture's first example is worth knowing before you start: a test that is 99% accurate on the ill can leave a positive-testing person with only a 2% chance of being ill. Rare things stay unlikely even after good evidence.

THEOREM Bayes' theorem— ⊡ ✕

Let $B_1,\dots,B_m$ be a partition of $\Omega$ and $A$ an event with $P(A)>0$. Then for each $i$

$$\boxed{P(B_i\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}{P(A\mid B_1)P(B_1)+\dots+P(A\mid B_m)P(B_m)}}$$

Where each piece comes from (the notes' arrows)

  1. Definition (module 07): $P(B_i\mid A)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B_i)}{P(A)}$.
  2. Numerator — law of multiplication: $P(A\cap B_i)=P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)$.
  3. Denominator — law of total probability over the partition: $P(A)=\sum_jP(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)$.

So the compact form is

$$P(B_i\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i)}{P(A)}.$$

Vocabulary (not on the slides, but universal)

$P(B_i)$prior — what you believed before seeing $A$
$P(A\mid B_i)$likelihood — how well $B_i$ explains the evidence
$P(B_i\mid A)$posterior — updated belief after seeing $A$
SEE IT The three ingredients— ⊡ ✕
The shortcut the notes end with: ∝
The denominator $P(A)$ is the same for every $i$ — it only makes the posteriors add up to 1. So $$P(B_i\mid A)\ \propto\ P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$$ ("proportional to"). Compute likelihood × prior for each $B_i$, then divide each by their total. The figure at the bottom of this module is exactly that.
EX LECTURE 2 · notes p.3 · slides Conditional 18 — a rare disease and a good test— ⊡ ✕

A disease affects 1 person in 1000: $P(D)=0.001$, $P(D^c)=0.999$. A medical test has

$$\begin{aligned}P(\text{positive}\mid D)&=0.99&&P(\text{negative}\mid D)=0.01\ \text{"false negative"}\\P(\text{negative}\mid D^c)&=0.95&&P(\text{positive}\mid D^c)=0.05\ \text{"false positive"}\end{aligned}$$

Your test is positive (PT). What is $P(D\mid PT)$?

  1. Partition: $\{D,D^c\}$. Evidence: $A=PT$.
  2. Bayes: $$P(D\mid PT)=\frac{P(PT\mid D)P(D)}{P(PT\mid D)P(D)+P(PT\mid D^c)P(D^c)}$$
  3. Numbers: $$=\frac{0.99\cdot0.001}{0.99\cdot0.001+0.05\cdot0.999}=\frac{0.00099}{0.00099+0.04995}=\frac{0.00099}{0.05094}\approx0.0194\approx0.02$$
Why so small? Count people.
Screen 100,000 people. About 100 are ill, and 99 of them test positive. Of the 99,900 healthy, 5% — 4,995 — also test positive. So among the 5,094 positives only 99 are ill: $99/5094\approx2\%$. The false positives win because there are so many more healthy people to produce them. The figure shows exactly this tree.
Try this
Press test again a few times: a second positive test (independent, same accuracy) uses 0.0194 as the new prior and gives ≈ 0.28; a third gives ≈ 0.89. Bayes is a machine you can feed its own output — that is how evidence accumulates.
EX LECTURE 2 · notes p.4 · slides Conditional 19 — which factory made the defective chip?— ⊡ ✕

Three factories produce chips: $F_1$ has 50% of the market, $F_2$ 40%, $F_3$ 10%. Defect rates: $P(D\mid F_1)=0.01$, $P(D\mid F_2)=0.02$, $P(D\mid F_3)=0.03$. A chip is defective. Which factory most likely made it?

  1. Total probability of a defect (the denominator, module 08): $$P(D)=0.01\cdot0.5+0.02\cdot0.4+0.03\cdot0.1=0.005+0.008+0.003=0.016$$
  2. Bayes for each factory: $$P(F_1\mid D)=\frac{0.01\cdot0.5}{0.016}=\frac{0.005}{0.016}=\frac{5}{16}$$ $$P(F_2\mid D)=\frac{0.008}{0.016}=\frac8{16}\ \longleftarrow\text{most likely}\qquad P(F_3\mid D)=\frac{0.003}{0.016}=\frac3{16}$$
  3. Check: $\tfrac5{16}+\tfrac8{16}+\tfrac3{16}=1$ ✓.

The slides tell it as Betty's reasoning: first she blames $F_1$ (biggest producer), then $F_3$ (worst quality). Both are half-right: size and quality both count, multiplied — and $F_2$ wins, with $0.4\times0.02$.

The "∝" route (notes)
$P(F_i\mid D)\propto P(D\mid F_i)P(F_i)$: the products are $0.005 : 0.008 : 0.003$, i.e. $5:8:3$. Divide by the total 16. No need to compute $P(D)$ separately — it is the total.
$P(H_3)=1-P(H_1)-P(H_2)$
EX SLIDES · Conditional 20–21 — three more, same machine— ⊡ ✕

Smoker on the plane

3% of men and 1% of women smoke. 40 men, 60 women on board; someone smokes in the restroom. $P(\text{man}\mid S)$?

$$P(M\mid S)=\frac{0.4\cdot0.03}{0.4\cdot0.03+0.6\cdot0.01}=\frac{0.012}{0.018}=\frac23$$

Men are fewer, but three times as likely to smoke: 12 : 6.

Bobby has no brothers

Families have 1, 2 or 3 children (prob. $\tfrac13$ each), boys and girls equally likely. Bobby has no brothers — $A$ = "the family has exactly one boy". $P(A\mid B_1)=\tfrac12$, $P(A\mid B_2)=\tfrac12$ (GB, BG of 4), $P(A\mid B_3)=\tfrac38$ (3 of 8 orders).

$$P(B_1\mid A)=\frac{\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12}{\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12+\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac38}=\frac4{11}$$

Bobby has no sisters

Now $A$ = "the family has no girls": $P(A\mid B_1)=\tfrac12$, $P(A\mid B_2)=\tfrac14$, $P(A\mid B_3)=\tfrac18$.

$$P(B_1\mid A)=\frac{\tfrac16}{\tfrac16+\tfrac1{12}+\tfrac1{24}}=\frac{4/24}{7/24}=\frac47$$

Surprise: not 4/11. "All boys" gets rarer much faster with family size than "exactly one boy" does ($\tfrac18$ vs $\tfrac38$ for three kids), so big families are penalised more.

TRAP The base-rate fallacy— ⊡ ✕

"The test is 99% accurate, so a positive result means 99% ill" confuses $P(+\mid D)=0.99$ with $P(D\mid+)\approx0.02$. The missing ingredient is the prior (base rate) $P(D)=0.001$. In an exam, whenever you see a conditional probability given one way and asked the other way, it is a Bayes question: write the partition, the priors, the likelihoods, then the formula.

?check yourselfDefault risk
20% of a bank's borrowers are "risky". A risky borrower misses a payment with probability 0.3, a safe one with 0.05. A borrower misses a payment. Probability they are risky?
Answer
$\dfrac{0.3\cdot0.2}{0.3\cdot0.2+0.05\cdot0.8}=\dfrac{0.06}{0.10}=0.6$. One missed payment triples the risk estimate (0.2 → 0.6).
