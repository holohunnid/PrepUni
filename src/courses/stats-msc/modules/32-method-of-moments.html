Method of moments

lectures 6–7, notes pp.45–47 · Point Estimation deck §5.1 · exercise sets 2–3
READ FIRST Match the model's moments to the data's moments— ⊡ ✕

The model gives the population moments $\mathbb E(X),\mathbb E(X^2),\dots$ as formulas in $\theta$. The data give the sample moments $M_1=\bar X,\ M_2=\frac1n\sum X_i^2,\dots$ as numbers. With enough data the two should be close, so set them equal and solve for $\theta$. One equation per unknown parameter.

It is the oldest general estimation recipe (K. Pearson, late 1800s). It always produces an answer quickly, and sometimes the answer is absurd (a negative number of trials, below), which is a good reason to also learn maximum likelihood.

EXAM (lecturer, notes p.47)

LECTURE 6 The method (notes pp.45–46 · deck p.30)— ⊡ ✕

Intuition: sample moments should be close to population moments if we have enough data. So we equate

population moments$\mathbb E_\theta(X),\ \mathbb E_\theta(X^2),\ \mathbb E_\theta(X^3),\dots$
sample moments$\bar X=\frac1n\sum X_i,\ \ \bar X_2=\frac1n\sum X_i^2,\ \ \bar X_3=\frac1n\sum X_i^3,\dots$
$$\begin{cases}\mathbb E_\theta(X)=\bar X\\ \mathbb E_\theta(X^2)=\bar X_2\\ \quad\vdots\\ \mathbb E_\theta(X^p)=\bar X_p\end{cases}$$

How many equations? It depends on the dimension of $\theta$: as many equations as unknown parameters. Solve the system for $\theta$: the solution $\tilde\theta$ is the MME (method-of-moments estimator) of $\theta$.

  • $X\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)$: 1 equation to learn 1 parameter. $\mathbb E(X)=\bar X$, solve for $\lambda$.
  • $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: 2 equations for 2 parameters. $\mathbb E(X)=\bar X$ and $\mathbb E(X^2)=\bar X_2$, solve for $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$.
Note: it is a semi-parametric method (notes p.46)
We don't need to know the whole distribution of $X$, as long as we know enough moments. The MME of a mean is $\bar X$ whatever the family. Maximum likelihood, by contrast, uses the full pmf/pdf.
TRAP · the deck writes $m_r$ and $\mu_r$
The notes write $\bar X_r$ for the sample moment, the deck writes $m_r=\frac1n\sum X_i^r$ and $\mu_r=\mathbb E(X^r)$. Same objects. Careful: here $\mu_r$ is a raw moment, not the mean $\mu$.
LECTURE 6 · notes p.45 · X ~ Exp(λ): one equation
  1. Population moment. $\mathbb E_\lambda(X)=\frac1\lambda$ (module 24).
  2. Equate. $\frac1{\tilde\lambda}=\bar X$.
  3. Solve. $\boxed{\tilde\lambda=\frac1{\bar X}}$. Average waiting time 4 minutes ⇒ estimated rate $0.25$ per minute.

Same idea for $\text{Pois}(\lambda)$: $\mathbb E(X)=\lambda$, so $\tilde\lambda=\bar X$. For $\text{Bern}(p)$: $\tilde p=\bar X$. For $\text{Geo}(p)$ (version I): $\frac1p=\bar X$, so $\tilde p=1/\bar X$.

LECTURE 6 · notes p.46 · deck pp.31–32 · X ~ N(μ, σ²), both unknown
  1. Two parameters, two equations. $\theta=(\mu,\sigma^2)$. $\mathbb E(X)=\mu$; and from $\mathbb V(X)=\mathbb E(X^2)-\mathbb E(X)^2$, $\ \mathbb E(X^2)=\mathbb V(X)+\mathbb E(X)^2=\sigma^2+\mu^2$.
  2. Equate. $\begin{cases}\tilde\mu=\bar X\\ \tilde\sigma^2+\tilde\mu^2=\bar X_2\end{cases}$
  3. Solve. $\boxed{\tilde\mu=\bar X,\quad\tilde\sigma^2=\bar X_2-\bar X^2}$

So the MM estimator of $\mu$ is $\bar X$, and of $\sigma^2$ is $\bar X_2-\bar X^2$. Which familiar statistic is that? See the next panel.

DERIVATION $\bar X_2-\bar X^2$ is the analogy estimator $\hat\sigma^2$ (notes p.46)— ⊡ ✕

Add and subtract $\bar X$ inside the square:

$$\begin{aligned}\tilde\sigma^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nX_i^2-\bar X^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big((X_i-\bar X)+\bar X\big)^2-\bar X^2\\ &=\frac1n\sum\Big[(X_i-\bar X)^2+\bar X^2+2\bar X(X_i-\bar X)\Big]-\bar X^2\\ &=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2+\underbrace{\frac1n\sum\bar X^2}_{=\bar X^2}+2\bar X\underbrace{\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)}_{=0}-\bar X^2\\ &=\boxed{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)^2}\end{aligned}$$

Why the middle term vanishes: $\sum(X_i-\bar X)=\sum X_i-n\bar X=n\bar X-n\bar X=0$. Deviations from the mean always sum to zero.

So for the normal, the MME of $\sigma^2$ corresponds to the analogy estimator $\hat\sigma^2$ of module 31, with the same bias $-\sigma^2/n$. The notes add "(not always)": in general the two methods give different estimators. The deck writes the result as $\tilde\sigma^2=\frac{n-1}nS^2$.

LECTURE 7 When the method fails: Binomial with N and p both unknown (notes p.47 · deck pp.32–33)— ⊡ ✕

$X\sim\text{Bin}(N,p)$ with both $N$ and $p$ unknown; $(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ i.i.d. We need two equations.

  1. Moments. $\mathbb E(X)=Np$; $\ \mathbb E(X^2)=\mathbb V(X)+\mathbb E(X)^2=Np(1-p)+(Np)^2$.
  2. Equate. $\begin{cases}\tilde N\tilde p=\bar X\\ \tilde N\tilde p(1-\tilde p)+(\tilde N\tilde p)^2=\bar X_2\end{cases}$
  3. Solve. Substitute $\tilde N\tilde p=\bar X$ into the second: $\bar X(1-\tilde p)+\bar X^2=\bar X_2$, so $1-\tilde p=\frac{\bar X_2-\bar X^2}{\bar X}$. Then $$\boxed{\tilde p=\frac{\bar X}{\tilde N},\qquad\tilde N=\frac{\bar X^2}{\bar X-(\bar X_2-\bar X^2)}}$$
  4. Data $\underline x=(1,5,7,1)$, $n=4$: $\bar x=3.5$, $\bar x_2=\frac{1+25+49+1}4=19$, $\bar x_2-\bar x^2=6.75$. Then $\tilde N=\frac{12.25}{3.5-6.75}=\mathbf{-3.77}$ and $\tilde p=\frac{3.5}{-3.77}=\mathbf{-0.929}$.
Both estimates are impossible
$N$ must be a positive integer and $p\in[0,1]$. MM estimates need not respect the parameter space. Here the sample variance (6.75) exceeds the sample mean (3.5), but a binomial always has variance $Np(1-p)<Np$ = mean. The data look "over-dispersed" for a binomial, and the moment equations have no admissible solution.

EXERCISE SET 2 · Q3 · $f(x)=\frac12(1+\theta x)$ on $[-1,1]$
  1. (a) Mean. $\mathbb E(X)=\int_{-1}^1x\,\frac12(1+\theta x)dx=\Big[\frac{x^2}4+\frac{\theta x^3}6\Big]_{-1}^1=\frac\theta6+\frac\theta6=\frac\theta3$.
  2. (b) Variance. $\mathbb E(X^2)=\Big[\frac{x^3}6+\frac{\theta x^4}8\Big]_{-1}^1=\frac13$, so $\mathbb V(X)=\frac13-\frac{\theta^2}9=\frac{3-\theta^2}9$.
  3. (c) MME. $\frac{\tilde\theta}3=\bar X\Rightarrow\boxed{\tilde\theta=3\bar X}$ (answer D).
  4. (d) Unbiased? $\mathbb E(3\bar X)=3\mathbb E(\bar X)=3\cdot\frac\theta3=\theta$. Bias 0: unbiased.
EXERCISE SET 2 · Q4 · Laplace(b): when the first moment is useless

$f(x;b)=\frac1{2b}e^{-|x|/b}$, $b>0$. Symmetric around 0, so $\mathbb E(X)=0$ and $\mathbb V(X)=\mathbb E(X^2)=2b^2$ (answer B).

  1. First moment fails. $\mathbb E(X)=0=\bar X$ contains no $b$: no equation for $b$.
  2. Use the second. $2\tilde b^2=\frac1n\sum X_i^2\Rightarrow\boxed{\tilde b=\sqrt{\tfrac1{2n}\textstyle\sum X_i^2}}$ (answer D).
  3. Alternative moment. $\mathbb E(|X|)=\int|x|f(x)dx=\int_0^\infty\frac xbe^{-x/b}dx=b$. Equating to $\frac1n\sum|X_i|$ gives $\hat b=\frac1n\sum|X_i|$ (answer C).

Lesson: "moment" can be any expectation that involves the parameter. Pick the one that makes the equation solvable.

?check yourselfExercise set 3, Q2(c): Beta(θ, 1)
$f(x;\theta)=\theta x^{\theta-1}$ on $[0,1]$, $\theta>0$. Find the MME of $\theta$.
Answer
$\mathbb E(X)=\int_0^1\theta x^\theta dx=\frac\theta{\theta+1}$. Set $\frac{\tilde\theta}{\tilde\theta+1}=\bar X$: $\tilde\theta=\bar X\tilde\theta+\bar X$, so $\tilde\theta=\frac{\bar X}{1-\bar X}$. Module 34 finds the MLE $\hat\theta=-\frac n{\sum\ln X_i}$: a different estimator.
?check yourselfUniform(0, θ)
$X\sim U(0,\theta)$. Find the MME of $\theta$. Is it always admissible for the observed sample $(1,2,9)$?
Answer
$\mathbb E(X)=\theta/2\Rightarrow\tilde\theta=2\bar X$. For $(1,2,9)$: $\tilde\theta=2\cdot4=8$. But we observed a 9, so $\theta\ge9$ for sure: the estimate contradicts the data. The MLE (module 34) is $\max X_i=9$ and never has this problem.
