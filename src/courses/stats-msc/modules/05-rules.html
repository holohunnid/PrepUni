05

Rules from the axioms

lecture 1, notes p.7–8 · slides: Axioms 15–19
READ FIRST The toolbox you will use in every exercise— ⊡ ✕

Module 04 gave three axioms. This module derives the working rules from them — the ones you reach for without thinking: equally likely outcomes, the complement rule, "probabilities are between 0 and 1", "a bigger event is more likely", the addition rule for overlapping events, and splitting an event in two. Each proof is two or three lines, and each is built the same way: cut the event into disjoint pieces, then use additivity. Learn that one move and you can re-derive the whole list in an exam.

THEOREM Countable Ω: outcome probabilities sum to 1— ⊡ ✕

If $\Omega=\{\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\dots\}$ is countable and $p_i:=P(\{\omega_i\})$, then

$$p_1+p_2+p_3+\dots=1.$$

Why: $\Omega$ is the union of the disjoint singletons $\{\omega_1\},\{\omega_2\},\dots$, so axiom 3 gives $P(\Omega)=\sum_ip_i$, and axiom 2 says this is 1.

This is how you specify a probability on a countable $\Omega$: give each outcome a non-negative weight, make the weights sum to 1, and every event's probability is the sum of its weights. (It is the seed of the pmf in module 15.)

EXAMPLE · the stubborn coin flipper, fair coin
$P(H)=\tfrac12$, $P(TH)=\tfrac14$, $P(TTH)=\tfrac18$, … so $p_k=(\tfrac12)^k$ and $\sum_k p_k=\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\dots=1$ ✓. Then, for example, $P(\text{done within 3 tosses})=\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18=\tfrac78$.
THEOREM Equally likely outcomes— ⊡ ✕

When each $\omega\in\Omega$ is equally likely and $\Omega$ has $N$ outcomes,

$$\boxed{P(E)=\frac{|E|}{|\Omega|}=\frac{\text{number of outcomes in }E}{\text{number of outcomes}}}$$

Why: $N$ equal numbers summing to 1 (previous theorem) must each equal $1/N$. An event with $k$ outcomes is the disjoint union of $k$ singletons, so its probability is $k\cdot\frac1N$.

Only when "equally likely" is true
The sum of two dice takes 11 values, but $P(\text{sum}=7)\neq\frac1{11}$: the sums are not equally likely, the pairs are. Always count in a sample space whose outcomes really are equally likely.
THEOREM Complement rule and bounds— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{P(E^c)=1-P(E)}\qquad\boxed{0\le P(E)\le1}$$

Complement — proof. $E$ and $E^c$ are disjoint ($E\cap E^c=\emptyset$) and $E\cup E^c=\Omega$. Finite additivity and axiom 2:

$$P(E)+P(E^c)=P(E\cup E^c)=P(\Omega)=1\ \Longrightarrow\ P(E^c)=1-P(E).$$

Bounds — proof. $P(E)\ge0$ is axiom 1. And $P(E)=1-P(E^c)\le1$ because $P(E^c)\ge0$ (axiom 1 again).

Exam reflex: "at least one" ⇒ complement
"At least one six in two rolls" has many cases; its complement "no six at all" has one: $P=1-\tfrac{25}{36}=\tfrac{11}{36}$. Whenever an event is described with "at least", compute its complement.
THEOREM Monotonicity: a bigger event is at least as likely— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{A\subseteq B\ \Longrightarrow\ P(A)\le P(B)}$$

Proof (notes p.8). Split $B$ into $A$ and the ring around it:

$$B=A\cup(B-A),$$

two pieces with no intersection. So

$$P(B)=P(A)+\underbrace{P(B-A)}_{\ge0}\ \ge\ P(A).\ \square$$

A useful by-product of the proof: $P(B-A)=P(B)-P(A)$ when $A\subseteq B$.

THEOREM Inclusion–exclusion for probabilities (the addition rule)— ⊡ ✕

The counting rule of module 03, with $P$ in place of $|\cdot|$. For any events $A,B$ (not necessarily disjoint):

$$\boxed{P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)}$$

and for three sets

$$\begin{aligned}P(A\cup B\cup C)=\;&P(A)+P(B)+P(C)\\&-P(A\cap B)-P(B\cap C)-P(A\cap C)\\&+P(A\cap B\cap C).\end{aligned}$$

Proof for two sets (the slides omit it)

  1. $A\cup B=A\cup(B-A)$, disjoint, so $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B-A)$.
  2. $B=(A\cap B)\cup(B-A)$, disjoint, so $P(B-A)=P(B)-P(A\cap B)$.
  3. Substitute: $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$. $\square$
EXAMPLE · four regions from three numbers
$P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.5$, $P(A\cap B)=0.3$.
  1. $P(A\cup B)=0.6+0.5-0.3=0.8$.
  2. $P(A\text{ only})=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=0.3$; $P(B\text{ only})=0.5-0.3=0.2$.
  3. $P(\text{neither})=1-P(A\cup B)=0.2$.
  4. Check: $0.3+0.3+0.2+0.2=1$ ✓.
THEOREM Splitting an event along another one— ⊡ ✕

The notes call this the law of total probability — its simplest form:

$$\boxed{P(A)=P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c)}$$

The part of $A$ inside $B$ plus the part of $A$ outside $B$. The two pieces cannot overlap (an outcome cannot be both in $B$ and in $B^c$), so additivity does the rest. The full proof and the general version with many pieces — which feeds straight into Bayes — are module 08.

Reading it backwards is just as useful: $P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B)$, "A but not B".

?check yourselfAddition rule backwards
$P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.4$, $P(A\cup B)=0.7$. Find $P(A\cap B)$ and $P(A\cap B^c)$.
Answer
$P(A\cap B)=0.5+0.4-0.7=0.2$. Then $P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=0.3$.
