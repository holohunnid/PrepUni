19

Bernoulli & binomial

lecture 3, notes p.4–6 · slides: Special Discrete Distributions 5–22
READ FIRST The question this module answers— ⊡ ✕

A yes/no experiment is repeated $n$ times, independently. How likely is each possible number of "yes" outcomes?

That one question covers a surprising amount of finance and statistics: how many of 100 loans default, on how many of 250 trading days the loss breaks the Value-at-Risk limit, how many of 10 die tosses show a six. The answer is the binomial distribution, and it is built from one tiny brick, the Bernoulli variable (a single yes/no trial). The plan:

  1. One trial (Bernoulli): the value is 1 with probability $p$ and 0 with probability $q=1-p$. Its pmf, cdf, mean $p$ and variance $pq$.
  2. Three trials by hand (the slides' coin examples): list the outcomes, group them by the number of successes, and see where the count $C_{n,k}$ comes from.
  3. $n$ trials (binomial): the general pmf $C_{n,k}\,p^kq^{n-k}$, its cdf, and what the shape does as $p$ and $n$ change.
  4. The key structural fact: a binomial is a sum of $n$ independent Bernoullis. That gives $\mathbb{E}(X)=np$ and $\mathbb{V}(X)=npq$ in two lines.
  5. The die example: four probabilities worked out to the last digit, plus the R commands that give them.

The slides open (slides 3–4) with a recap of the pmf, $\mathbb{E}$, $\mathbb{V}$ and their rules. All of that is in modules 15–17. The one rule this module leans on hard is $\mathbb{V}(aX+bY+c)=a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)+b^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)$, valid only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

The three conditions behind every binomial
Before you write "Binomial", check: (i) a fixed number $n$ of trials; (ii) each trial has two outcomes, with the same success probability $p$ every time; (iii) the trials are independent. If any one fails, the formulas of this module do not apply.
LECTURE 3 What the lecturer did and stressed (notes p.4–6)— ⊡ ✕

New to all of this? Read module 18 first — it shows where Bernoulli and binomial sit in the family, and a simulation builds the binomial from coin flips.

Bernoulli in one line

The notes write the two cases $P(X=1)=p$ ("success probability", the parameter) and $P(X=0)=1-p$ ("failure probability") as a single formula:

$$\boxed{f(x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x},\qquad x\in\{0,1\}}$$

Check it: $x=1$ gives $p^1(1-p)^0=p$; $x=0$ gives $p^0(1-p)^1=1-p$. The exponents just switch the right factor on. You will meet this exact form again in point estimation, when the likelihood of a 0/1 sample is written down — worth getting used to now.

The lecture counts heads

(The slides count tails — same thing.) $n=3$ fair flips, $|\Omega|=2^3=8$ outcomes, each $\tfrac18$:

$$P(X=0)=P(\{TTT\})=\tfrac18,\quad P(X=1)=P(\{TTH\}\cup\{THT\}\cup\{HTT\})=3\cdot\tfrac18,$$ $$P(X=2)=3\cdot\tfrac18,\quad P(X=3)=\tfrac18\ \Rightarrow\ P(X=k)=\binom3k\Big(\tfrac12\Big)^3.$$

The binomial coefficient $\binom3k=\frac{3!}{k!\,(3-k)!}$ is, in the notes' words, the number of ways to place the $k$ successes among the $n$ trials. With a biased coin, $P(H)=\tfrac13$, each such arrangement has probability $(\tfrac13)^k(\tfrac23)^{3-k}$, so $P(X=k)=\binom3k(\tfrac13)^k(\tfrac23)^{3-k}$: that is $\tfrac8{27},\tfrac{12}{27},\tfrac6{27},\tfrac1{27}$ for $k=0,1,2,3$.

Exam flag: no proof — remember the link
The notes: "Exam: No proof!! Just remember link: sum of Bernoullis are binomial." If $Y_1,\dots,Y_n$ are independent Bernoulli$(p)$ with the same $p$, then $X=Y_1+\dots+Y_n\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)$. Special case: $\text{Bin}(1,p)=\text{Bernoulli}(p)$.

What you do compute, as in the notes:

$$\mathbb E(Y_i)=0\cdot(1-p)+1\cdot p=p,\qquad \mathbb E(Y_i^2)=0^2(1-p)+1^2p=p$$ $$\mathbb V(Y_i)=\mathbb E(Y_i^2)-\mathbb E(Y_i)^2=p-p^2=p(1-p)$$

and then, adding up the $n$ trials (independence is needed for the variance, module 17):

$$\mathbb E(X)=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E(Y_i)=np,\qquad \mathbb V(X)=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb V(Y_i)=np(1-p).$$

Reading R, as annotated in the notes

"Being discrete, included or not changes P"
The notes' last line: $P(X<1)=P(X=0)$ — not $P(X\le1)$. In R, dbinom(0, 10, 1/6) and pbinom(0, 10, 1/6) both give 0.1615. For a discrete $X$, "<" drops the endpoint's whole probability (module 13).
DEF Bernoulli trial (slide 5)— ⊡ ✕

A Bernoulli trial is a random experiment with only two possible outcomes. By convention they are called success and failure. The slides give four examples:

  • flip a coin: heads or tails;
  • an earthquake of magnitude 6 or more hits a given city, or it does not;
  • a power station's CO$_2$ emissions $X$ (millions of metric tons): is $X\le5$ or $X>5$? A continuous quantity becomes a trial once you only ask which side of a threshold it lands on;
  • tomorrow's loss on a bank's portfolio exceeds the VaR estimated today, or it does not.

So the sample space is $\Omega=\{s,f\}$, with

$$P(\{s\})=p,\qquad P(\{f\})=1-p=q$$

Now attach numbers: let $X$ map success to 1 and failure to 0,

$$X:\{s,f\}\to\{0,1\},\qquad X(s)=1,\quad X(f)=0$$

This $X$ is a Bernoulli random variable. Coding success as 1 is the trick of the whole module: adding up 0/1 variables counts successes.

Finance: the VaR exceedance
A 99% one-day VaR is, by construction, a loss level that should be exceeded on only 1% of days. So each day is a Bernoulli trial with "success" = "the loss beat the VaR", and $p=0.01$ if the risk model is right. Backtesting a VaR model means counting these successes over, say, 250 days. That count is binomial (see the check-yourself question at the end of this module).
"Success" is just a label
An earthquake or a VaR breach is hardly a success. The word only marks the outcome you have chosen to count (coded 1). Choose whichever outcome the question asks about.
DEF Bernoulli random variable and its pmf (slide 6)— ⊡ ✕

A random variable that takes the value 1 with probability $p$ and 0 with probability $1-p=:q$ is a Bernoulli r.v. with probability of success $p$. We write

$$X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)$$

Its pmf (module 15: the pmf lists $P(X=k)$ for every $k$) is

$$\boxed{\;f(k)=P(X=k)=\begin{cases}p&k=1\\1-p=q&k=0\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\;}$$

Check the two pmf rules: every value is $\ge0$ ✓ and $p+q=1$ ✓. The single parameter $p$ can be anything in $[0,1]$.

DEF The cdf of a Bernoulli (slide 7)— ⊡ ✕

The cdf $F(k)=P(X\le k)$ (module 13) accumulates the bars from the left. Below 0 nothing has been collected yet. At 0 we pick up the bar $q=1-p$. At 1 we pick up the bar $p$ and reach 1:

$$\boxed{\;F(k)=P(X\le k)=\begin{cases}0&k<0\\1-p&0\le k<1\\1&1\le k\end{cases}\;}$$

Read it as a staircase with two steps. Each jump sits at a value $X$ can take, and the size of the jump equals the pmf bar there: a jump of $q$ at 0 and a jump of $p$ at 1.

The filled dot at the left end of each step and the open circle at its right end show that $F$ is right-continuous: at exactly $k=0$ the value is already $q$, because $P(X\le0)$ includes the event $X=0$.

The slides draw this for $p=0.2,\,0.5,\,0.8$. The middle step sits at $1-p$, i.e. at $0.8$, $0.5$ and $0.2$.

PROOF $\mathbb{E}(X)=p$ and $\mathbb{V}(X)=pq$ (slide 8)— ⊡ ✕

Claim. If $X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)$, then $\boxed{\mathbb{E}(X)=p}$ and $\boxed{\mathbb{V}(X)=pq}$.

  1. Mean: the pmf-weighted sum of the values (module 16), over the two values 0 and 1: $$\mathbb{E}(X)=\sum_{k=0}^{1}f(k)\,k=f(0)\cdot0+f(1)\cdot1=q\cdot0+p\cdot1=p$$
  2. Variance: use the shortcut $\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2$ (module 17). First the second moment: $$\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\sum_{k=0}^{1}f(k)\,k^2=q\cdot0^2+p\cdot1^2=p$$
  3. Subtract the squared mean: $$\mathbb{V}(X)=p-p^2=p(1-p)=pq\qquad\square$$
The trick hiding in step 2
Since $0^2=0$ and $1^2=1$, a 0/1 variable satisfies $X^2=X$. So $\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\mathbb{E}(X)=p$ immediately. The same fact makes indicator variables the easiest objects in probability.

Interpretation. The mean of a 0/1 variable is the probability of a 1, i.e. the long-run fraction of successes. If you average many Bernoulli outcomes you estimate $p$, which is exactly what a sample proportion does.

IDEA When is a coin flip most uncertain?— ⊡ ✕

So a fair coin is the most unpredictable yes/no experiment. A trial that almost always succeeds ($p$ near 1) or almost always fails ($p$ near 0) carries little uncertainty. The maximum standard deviation of a single trial is $\sqrt{1/4}=\tfrac12$.

LECTURE 3 · notes p.4 · slide 10 · a fair coin flipped 3 times

Repeat a Bernoulli trial $n$ times, independently, and let $X$ be the number of successes. Then $X$ can be anything from 0 to $n$, i.e. its support is $\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}$. First a small case: flip a fair coin $n=3$ times, and let $X$ = number of tails ("success" = tails).

  1. List the sample space. Each flip is H or T, so there are $2^3=8$ outcomes: $$\Omega=\{HHH,\,HHT,\,HTH,\,THH,\,HTT,\,THT,\,TTH,\,TTT\}$$ (The slide lists HTT twice. The third outcome with two tails is TTH.)
  2. Probability of each outcome. The coin is fair and the flips are independent, so every sequence has probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac18$.
  3. Group the outcomes by the value of $X$ and add their probabilities: $$\begin{aligned} f(0)&=P(\{HHH\})=1\times\tfrac18=\tfrac18\\ f(1)&=P(\{HHT,HTH,THH\})=3\times\tfrac18=\tfrac38\\ f(2)&=P(\{HTT,THT,TTH\})=3\times\tfrac18=\tfrac38\\ f(3)&=P(\{TTT\})=1\times\tfrac18=\tfrac18 \end{aligned}$$
  4. Check it is a pmf: $\tfrac18+\tfrac38+\tfrac38+\tfrac18=1$ ✓
  5. Spot the pattern. The multipliers $1,3,3,1$ count how many sequences have $k$ tails. That count is $C_{3,k}$, so $$f(k)=P(X=k)=C_{3,k}\left(\tfrac12\right)^3=\frac{3!}{k!\,(3-k)!}\left(\tfrac12\right)^3,\qquad k=0,1,2,3$$ Check: $C_{3,1}=\frac{3!}{1!\,2!}=\frac{6}{2}=3$ ✓
Insight
Every binomial probability has two factors: (how many sequences give $k$ successes) × (probability of one such sequence). With a fair coin the second factor is the same $\tfrac18$ for every sequence, so the pmf is simply the counts $1,3,3,1$ divided by 8.
LECTURE 3 · notes p.5 · slide 11 · a biased coin, $P(\{T\})=\tfrac13$

Same experiment, $n=3$ flips, $X$ = number of tails, but now $P(T)=\tfrac13$ and $P(H)=\tfrac23$.

  1. The outcomes are no longer equally likely. By independence, multiply the flip probabilities along each sequence. Only the number of T's matters, not their order: $$\begin{aligned} P(\{HHH\})&=\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac23=\left(\tfrac23\right)^3=\tfrac{8}{27}\\ P(\{HHT\})=P(\{HTH\})=P(\{THH\})&=\left(\tfrac13\right)^1\left(\tfrac23\right)^2=\tfrac{4}{27}\\ P(\{HTT\})=P(\{THT\})=P(\{TTH\})&=\left(\tfrac13\right)^2\left(\tfrac23\right)^1=\tfrac{2}{27}\\ P(\{TTT\})&=\left(\tfrac13\right)^3=\tfrac{1}{27} \end{aligned}$$
  2. Group by $X$. The counts are the same $1,3,3,1$ as before: $$\begin{aligned} f(0)&=1\times\left(\tfrac23\right)^3=\tfrac{8}{27}\approx0.2963\\ f(1)&=3\times\left(\tfrac13\right)^1\left(\tfrac23\right)^2=\tfrac{12}{27}\approx0.4444\\ f(2)&=3\times\left(\tfrac13\right)^2\left(\tfrac23\right)^1=\tfrac{6}{27}\approx0.2222\\ f(3)&=1\times\left(\tfrac13\right)^3=\tfrac{1}{27}\approx0.0370 \end{aligned}$$
  3. Check: $8+12+6+1=27$, so the four probabilities add to $\tfrac{27}{27}=1$ ✓
  4. General form: $$f(k)=C_{3,k}\left(\tfrac13\right)^k\left(\tfrac23\right)^{3-k}=\frac{3!}{k!\,(3-k)!}\left(\tfrac13\right)^k\left(\tfrac23\right)^{3-k},\qquad k=0,1,2,3$$
Insight
Changing $p$ leaves the counting factor $C_{3,k}$ untouched and changes only the probability of each sequence, $p^kq^{3-k}$. Tails are now rarer, so the pmf leans towards few tails: the most likely value is 1, and 3 tails happens once in 27 tries.
IDEA Seeing both examples at once— ⊡ ✕
IDEA Where $C_{n,k}$ comes from: count the arrangements— ⊡ ✕

The question. In $n$ trials, how many different sequences contain exactly $k$ successes? A sequence is completely determined once you say which $k$ of the $n$ positions hold the successes. So we are counting the ways to choose $k$ positions out of $n$.

  1. Choose in order first. $n$ choices for the first success position, $n-1$ for the second, …, $n-k+1$ for the $k$-th: $$n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\ \text{ordered choices}$$
  2. Forget the order. Each set of $k$ positions was produced $k!$ times, once for each order in which you could have picked the same positions. Divide it out: $$\boxed{\;C_{n,k}=\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\;}$$
  3. Check on the tree: $n=3$, $k=2$: $\frac{3\cdot2}{2!}=3$ ✓. And for later use, $n=10$, $k=2$: $C_{10,2}=\frac{10\cdot9}{2}=45$.

Useful facts: $C_{n,0}=C_{n,n}=1$ (one way to have no successes, one way to have all), $C_{n,1}=n$, and the symmetry $C_{n,k}=C_{n,n-k}$ (choosing the successes is the same as choosing the failures). The same number is also written $\binom nk$ ("$n$ choose $k$"). In R it is choose(n, k). Summing over $k$ counts every sequence exactly once: $\sum_kC_{n,k}=2^n$ (here $1+3+3+1=8$).

DEF The binomial random variable (slide 12)— ⊡ ✕

Repeat a Bernoulli trial $n$ times, independently, with the same success probability $p$ each time. Let $X$ be the number of successes in $n$ trials. Then $X$ is a binomial random variable with size parameter $n$ and probability of success $p$:

$$X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)$$

Its support is $\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}$. Putting the two factors together (number of sequences × probability of each) gives the pmf, and summing it gives the cdf:

$$\boxed{\;f(k)=P(X=k)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\,p^kq^{n-k}&k\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n\}\\[6pt]0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\;}$$ $$F(t)=P(X\le t)=\begin{cases}0&t<0\\[2pt]\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}f(i)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor t\rfloor}\frac{n!}{i!\,(n-i)!}\,p^iq^{n-i}&0\le t\le n\\[10pt]1&t>n\end{cases}$$

Here $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the floor of $x$: the largest integer $\le x$. For example $\lfloor2.7\rfloor=2$, $\lfloor3\rfloor=3$, $\lfloor0.4\rfloor=0$.

Why the floor? $X$ only takes whole-number values, so "at most 2.7 successes" is the same event as "at most 2 successes". The cdf only adds the bars at integers $i\le t$, and the last such integer is $\lfloor t\rfloor$. That is why the cdf is flat between integers and jumps at each one.

Why it sums to 1 (not on the slides, but it explains the name). By the binomial theorem, $(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}C_{n,k}\,a^kb^{n-k}$. With $a=p$, $b=q$:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n}C_{n,k}\,p^kq^{n-k}=(p+q)^n=1^n=1\ \checkmark$$

So the binomial probabilities are exactly the terms in the expansion of $(p+q)^n$.

IDEA What the shape does: $p$ skews, $n$ spreads (slides 13–16)— ⊡ ✕

Slides 13–16 plot the pmf and cdf for many $(n,p)$. You do not need to memorise pictures, only two effects.

  • Skew. For $p<\tfrac12$ the pmf is right-skewed, for $p=\tfrac12$ symmetric, for $p>\tfrac12$ left-skewed. Binomial$(n,p)$ and Binomial$(n,1-p)$ are mirror images, because "$k$ successes" for one is "$k$ failures", i.e. $n-k$ successes, for the other: $P(X=k)=P(Y=n-k)$ with $Y\sim\text{Binomial}(n,1-p)$.
  • Location and spread. The peak sits near the mean $np$. The spread grows like $\sqrt n$, so relative to $n$ the distribution gets narrower: the fraction of successes concentrates around $p$ (the law of large numbers in miniature).
  • The cdf plots on the slides are staircases whose steepest part is near $np$. For $n=50$ the steps are so small that the cdf looks like a smooth S-curve. That bell-and-S shape is the normal distribution of module 25 appearing inside the binomial.

THEOREM Bernoulli and binomial are the same family (slide 17)— ⊡ ✕

1. A Bernoulli is a binomial with one trial. Put $n=1$ in the binomial pmf: $f(0)=C_{1,0}\,p^0q^1=q$ and $f(1)=C_{1,1}\,p^1q^0=p$. That is exactly the Bernoulli pmf:

$$\text{Binomial}(1,p)\equiv\text{Bernoulli}(p)$$

2. A binomial is a sum of independent Bernoullis. Let $Y_1,Y_2,\dots,Y_n$ be independent with $Y_i\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ for $i=1,\dots,n$, and define

$$X:=Y_1+Y_2+Y_3+\cdots+Y_n$$

Then $X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)$. Why: each $Y_i$ is 1 on a success and 0 on a failure, so the sum counts the successes in $n$ independent trials with common $p$. That is word for word the definition of the binomial.

Variables that are independent and identically distributed are called i.i.d. So: a binomial is the sum of $n$ i.i.d. Bernoulli$(p)$ variables.

PICTURE Counting sixes by adding 0s and 1s— ⊡ ✕

This picture is the proof strategy for the next panel: anything that is additive (means always, variances under independence) can be computed for one $Y_i$ and multiplied by $n$.

PROOF $\mathbb{E}(X)=np$ and $\mathbb{V}(X)=npq$ (slide 18)— ⊡ ✕

Claim. If $X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)$ then $\boxed{\mathbb{E}(X)=np}$ and $\boxed{\mathbb{V}(X)=npq}$.

  1. Rewrite $X$ as a sum. By slide 17 we may take $X=Y_1+Y_2+\cdots+Y_n$ with $Y_i$ i.i.d. Bernoulli$(p)$, so $\mathbb{E}(Y_i)=p$ and $\mathbb{V}(Y_i)=pq$ (slide 8).
  2. Mean, by linearity (module 16: $\mathbb{E}$ of a sum is the sum of the $\mathbb{E}$'s): $$\mathbb{E}(X)=\mathbb{E}(Y_1)+\cdots+\mathbb{E}(Y_n)=\underbrace{p+p+\cdots+p}_{n\text{ times}}=np$$
  3. Variance, by independence (module 17: for independent variables the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances): $$\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{V}(Y_1)+\cdots+\mathbb{V}(Y_n)=\underbrace{pq+pq+\cdots+pq}_{n\text{ times}}=npq\qquad\square$$

Computing $\sum_k k\,C_{n,k}p^kq^{n-k}$ directly is possible but painful. The sum-of-Bernoullis view turns it into two lines, and that is the reason slide 17 exists.

Sanity check on Example 1 (fair coin, $n=3$, $p=\tfrac12$). Directly from the pmf $\tfrac18,\tfrac38,\tfrac38,\tfrac18$:

$$\mathbb{E}(X)=0\cdot\tfrac18+1\cdot\tfrac38+2\cdot\tfrac38+3\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac{12}{8}=1.5=3\cdot\tfrac12\ \checkmark$$ $$\mathbb{E}(X^2)=0+1\cdot\tfrac38+4\cdot\tfrac38+9\cdot\tfrac18=\tfrac{24}{8}=3$$ $$\mathbb{V}(X)=3-1.5^2=0.75=3\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\ \checkmark$$
TRAP: the variance needs independence, and sd ≠ variance
Step 2 holds for any $Y_i$. Step 3 needs independence, because without it covariance terms appear (slide 4's "only if independent"). Twenty loans in the same industry, which tend to default together, do not give a variance of $20pq$: the true variance is larger. And the standard deviation is $\sqrt{npq}$, not $npq$. For Binomial(50, 0.2) that is $\sqrt8\approx2.83$ defaults, not 8.
LECTURE 3 · notes p.6 · slides 19–21 · ten tosses of a die, counting sixes

We toss a die $n=10$ times. Let $X$ be the number of times we observe a 6. How is $X$ distributed? Give its pmf. Find $P(X=5)$, $P(X\le1)$, $P(X<3)$ and $P(X>8)$.

  1. Identify the model. One toss is a Bernoulli trial with success = "6", so $p=\tfrac16$ and $q=\tfrac56$. There are $n=10$ tosses, a fixed number, and they are independent. So $$X\sim\text{Binomial}\left(n=10,\ p=\tfrac16\right),\qquad\text{support }\{0,1,2,\dots,10\}$$
  2. The pmf. $$f(k)=C_{10,k}\left(\tfrac16\right)^k\left(\tfrac56\right)^{10-k}=\frac{10!}{k!\,(10-k)!}\left(\tfrac16\right)^k\left(\tfrac56\right)^{10-k}$$
  3. An arithmetic shortcut. Every term has the same denominator $6^{10}=60{,}466{,}176$: $$\left(\tfrac16\right)^k\left(\tfrac56\right)^{10-k}=\frac{5^{10-k}}{6^{10}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(k)=\frac{C_{10,k}\cdot5^{10-k}}{60{,}466{,}176}$$
  4. $P(X=5)$. $C_{10,5}=\frac{10!}{5!\,5!}=252$ and $5^5=3125$: $$P(X=5)=\frac{10!}{5!\,5!}\left(\tfrac16\right)^5\left(\tfrac56\right)^5=\frac{252\times3125}{60{,}466{,}176}=\frac{787{,}500}{60{,}466{,}176}=\mathbf{0.0130}$$
  5. $P(X\le1)=f(0)+f(1)$. $C_{10,0}=1$, $C_{10,1}=10$: $$f(0)=\left(\tfrac56\right)^{10}=\frac{9{,}765{,}625}{60{,}466{,}176}=0.1615,\qquad f(1)=10\left(\tfrac16\right)\left(\tfrac56\right)^9=\frac{19{,}531{,}250}{60{,}466{,}176}=0.3230$$ $$P(X\le1)=\frac{29{,}296{,}875}{60{,}466{,}176}=\mathbf{0.4845}$$
  1. $P(X<3)$. "Fewer than 3" means $0$, $1$ or $2$, so $P(X<3)=P(X\le2)=f(0)+f(1)+f(2)$. The new term uses $C_{10,2}=45$: $$f(2)=45\left(\tfrac16\right)^2\left(\tfrac56\right)^8=\frac{45\times390{,}625}{60{,}466{,}176}=\frac{17{,}578{,}125}{60{,}466{,}176}=0.2907$$ $$P(X<3)=\left(\tfrac56\right)^{10}+10\left(\tfrac16\right)\left(\tfrac56\right)^9+45\left(\tfrac16\right)^2\left(\tfrac56\right)^8=\frac{46{,}875{,}000}{60{,}466{,}176}=\mathbf{0.7752}$$
  2. $P(X>8)$. "More than 8" means $9$ or $10$. $C_{10,9}=10$, $C_{10,10}=1$: $$P(X>8)=10\left(\tfrac16\right)^9\left(\tfrac56\right)+\left(\tfrac16\right)^{10}=\frac{10\cdot5+1}{6^{10}}=\frac{51}{60{,}466{,}176}=\mathbf{8.4344\cdot10^{-7}}$$ That is about 1 chance in 1.19 million.
  3. Mean and spread (not asked on the slide, but it puts the answers in context): $$\mathbb{E}(X)=np=\tfrac{10}{6}=\tfrac53\approx1.67,\qquad\mathbb{V}(X)=npq=10\cdot\tfrac16\cdot\tfrac56=\tfrac{25}{18}\approx1.39,\qquad\text{sd}\approx1.18$$
Insight
Two habits make these questions mechanical. (1) Translate the words into a set of integers before computing: "fewer than 3" is $\{0,1,2\}$ and "more than 8" is $\{9,10\}$. (2) Add pmf values over that set, or use the complement when the set is long: $P(X\ge3)=1-P(X\le2)=1-0.7752=0.2248$ takes one subtraction instead of eight terms.
R corner (standard R, not on the slides)
dbinom(k, size, prob) is the pmf $f(k)$ and pbinom(k, size, prob) is the cdf $F(k)=P(X\le k)$:
dbinom(5, size = 10, prob = 1/6) → 0.01302381
pbinom(1, size = 10, prob = 1/6) → 0.4845167
pbinom(2, size = 10, prob = 1/6) → 0.7752268 (this is $P(X<3)$)
1 - pbinom(8, size = 10, prob = 1/6) → 8.434468e-07 (same as pbinom(8, 10, 1/6, lower.tail = FALSE))
choose(10, 5) → 252; sum(dbinom(0:10, 10, 1/6)) → 1.
A Bernoulli is dbinom(k, 1, p); rbinom(20, 1, 0.3) simulates 20 Bernoulli(0.3) trials, and rbinom(5, 10, 1/6) simulates five repetitions of the whole ten-toss experiment.
TRAP: strict versus non-strict inequalities
pbinom(k, …) always means $P(X\le k)$. So $P(X<3)$ is pbinom(2, …), not pbinom(3, …), which would add $f(3)=0.1550$ and give 0.9303. Likewise $P(X>8)=1-P(X\le8)$ and $P(X\ge8)=1-P(X\le7)$. For a discrete variable, $<$ versus $\le$ changes the answer. Only for continuous variables (module 22) does it stop mattering.

?check yourselfBacktesting a 99% VaR
A bank's 99% one-day VaR model is right, and days are independent. Over 250 trading days, let $X$ be the number of days the loss exceeds the VaR. Give the distribution of $X$, its mean and standard deviation, $P(X=0)$, and $P(X\ge5)$.
Answer
  1. Each day is a Bernoulli(0.01) trial, $n=250$ is fixed, and the days are independent, so $X\sim\text{Binomial}(250,\,0.01)$.
  2. $\mathbb{E}(X)=250\times0.01=2.5$ exceedances; $\mathbb{V}(X)=250\times0.01\times0.99=2.475$, so sd $\approx1.57$.
  3. $P(X=0)=0.99^{250}\approx0.0811$: even a perfect model has an exceedance-free year only about 8% of the time.
  4. $P(X\ge5)=1-P(X\le4)=1-0.8922=\mathbf{0.1078}$ (R: 1 - pbinom(4, 250, 0.01)). So a correct model still shows 5 or more exceedances in about 1 year out of 9. This is why regulators treat 0–4 exceedances as the "green zone" and do not penalise a bank for a single unlucky year.
?check yourselfBinomial or not?
Which of these are binomial? (a) The number of heads in 20 flips of a fair coin. (b) The number of flips until the first head. (c) The number of aces among 5 cards dealt from one deck. (d) The number of defaults among 100 loans to firms in the same industry.
Answer
  1. (a) Yes: $\text{Binomial}(20,\,0.5)$, with $\mathbb{E}=10$ and $\mathbb{V}=5$.
  2. (b) No: the number of trials is not fixed, it is the random quantity. This is the geometric distribution of module 21.
  3. (c) No: cards are dealt without replacement, so after an ace the chance of the next ace drops ($p$ is not constant and the trials are dependent). With replacement it would be $\text{Binomial}(5,\,1/13)$.
  4. (d) Only as a rough first model: firms in one industry tend to default together, which breaks independence. The count is then more spread out than $\text{Binomial}(100,p)$ predicts. This is the reason credit models add correlation.
