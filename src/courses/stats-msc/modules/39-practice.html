HOW TO USE Practise in the exam's own format

The exam is 1.5 hours, closed book: one open question (5 points), five multiple-choice questions on theory (5 points) and five multiple-choice questions on R (5 points). This module mirrors that: click an option — the first click counts — and the explanation appears. Part A and Part B now cover the whole course so far, probability and estimation and confidence intervals (A17–A30, R13–R24). Part C adds an MLE question in the format of the exam's open question (O4). The last panel indexes every example of modules 01–38 with its answer and where it is solved.

These questions are written for practice from the course material; they are not past exam questions. The R questions use standard R functions (the R corners in modules 15–38 introduce them). R13–R20 are taken from the three R question sheets.

PART A Multiple choice — theory— ⊡ ✕
A1$A$ and $B$ are independent with $P(A)=0.5$ and $P(B)=0.4$. Then $P(A\cup B)$ equals
Addition rule with the product for the overlap: $0.5+0.4-0.5\cdot0.4=0.7$. "0.9" forgets the overlap (modules 05, 09).
A2$A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $P(A)=0.3$, $P(B)=0.4$. Which statement is true?
Disjoint ⇒ $P(A\cap B)=0$ ⇒ $P(A\mid B)=0/0.4=0$. They are not independent ($0\neq0.12$), and $P(A\cup B)=0.7$ (module 09 trap).
A3An event $E$ has $P(E)=0$. Then
$P(E)=0\not\Rightarrow E=\emptyset$: a single point drawn from $(0,1)$ has probability 0 but is not empty (modules 04, 06). $P(E^c)=1$ always follows from the complement rule.
A4$P(D)=0.01$, $P(+\mid D)=0.9$, $P(+\mid D^c)=0.1$. Then $P(D\mid +)$ is closest to
Bayes: $\frac{0.9\cdot0.01}{0.9\cdot0.01+0.1\cdot0.99}=\frac{0.009}{0.108}\approx0.083$. The base rate drags it down (module 10).
A5A discrete $X$ with support $\{1,2,3\}$ has $F(1)=0.3$, $F(2)=0.7$, $F(3)=1$. $P(X=2)$ is
Point probability = jump of the CDF: $F(2)-F(2^-)=F(2)-F(1)=0.7-0.3=0.4$ (module 13).
A6Which of these is not a property of every CDF?
Discrete CDFs jump, so they are not continuous — but they are always right-continuous (module 13).
A7$X$ is continuous. Compare $P(X\le2)$ and $P(X<2)$.
$P(X=2)=0$ for continuous $X$. (For discrete $X$ they differ by the jump $P(X=2)$; $f(2)$ is a density height, not a probability — modules 13, 22.)
A8$A,B,C$ are pairwise independent. Then
The two-coin example: first coin H, same face, second coin H — pairwise independent, but $P(A\cap B\cap C)=\tfrac14\neq\tfrac18$ (module 09).
A9$X$ takes $-1,0,1$ with probability $\tfrac13$ each, and $Y=X^2$. $P(Y=1)$ is
Both $-1$ and $1$ map to 1: $\tfrac13+\tfrac13=\tfrac23$ (module 14 — merge the preimage).
A10$\mathbb V(X)=2$. Then $\mathbb V(3-2X)$ equals
Shifts don't matter, scales enter squared: $(-2)^2\cdot2=8$ (module 17).
A11$X\sim$ Binomial$(10,\,0.3)$. Its mean and variance are
$np=3$, $npq=10\cdot0.3\cdot0.7=2.1$ (module 19). "3 and 3" is the Poisson answer.
A12$X\sim$ Poisson$(2)$. $P(X=0)$ is
$e^{-\lambda}\lambda^0/0!=e^{-2}$ (module 20). $2e^{-2}$ is $P(X=1)$ (and also $P(X=2)$).
A13$f(x)=cx$ on $(0,2)$ and 0 elsewhere is a density when $c$ equals
Normalisation: $\int_0^2cx\,dx=c\cdot\tfrac{2^2}{2}=2c=1$ (module 22).
A14$X\sim$ Exponential with rate $\lambda=4$. Then $\mathbb E(X)$ and $P(X>1)$ are
$\mathbb E(X)=1/\lambda$; $P(X>x)=e^{-\lambda x}$ (module 24).
A15$X\sim N(10,25)$. $P(X<15)$ equals
The second parameter is the variance: $\sigma=5$, $z=(15-10)/5=1$ (module 25).
A16As the degrees of freedom $\nu$ grow, the Student-t distribution
$t_\nu\to N(0,1)$ as $\nu\to\infty$; small $\nu$ means heavier tails (module 26).

Estimation and confidence intervals (modules 27–38)

A17$X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. $\mathbb V(\bar X)$ equals
$\mathbb V(\frac1n\sum X_i)=\frac1{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2$, using independence. $\sigma/\sqrt n$ is the standard deviation of $\bar X$ (module 29).
A18Which of these is not a sample statistic?
A statistic may use only the data. With $\mu$ unknown the third expression cannot be computed (notes p.40, module 29).
A19$\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$. Then $\mathbb E(\hat\sigma^2)$ equals
$\hat\sigma^2=\frac{n-1}nS^2$ and $\mathbb E(S^2)=\sigma^2$. Bias $-\sigma^2/n$: it underestimates (modules 31, 35; exercise set 2, Q2c).
A20$X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, sample of size $n$. The distribution of $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$ is
A sum of squared standardised deviations from $\bar X$. One degree of freedom is lost to $\bar X$ (module 30).
A21$f(x;\theta)=\frac12(1+\theta x)$ on $[-1,1]$. The method-of-moments estimator of $\theta$ is
$\mathbb E(X)=\theta/3$; equate to $\bar X$ and solve (exercise set 2, Q3; module 32).
A22$X\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda)$, $\hat\lambda=\bar X$. The MLE of $P(X=0)$ is
$P(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}$; by the invariance property the MLE is $e^{-\hat\lambda}=e^{-\bar X}$ (notes p.54, module 34). The proportion of zeros is the analogy estimator, not the MLE.
A23Estimator $T_1$ is preferred to $T_2$ because it has
The lecture's exam warning: compare overall MSE, which includes both variability and bias. A biased estimator can win, e.g. $\hat\sigma^2$ vs $S^2$ (module 35).
A24The mean squared error of an estimator $T$ of $\theta$ decomposes as
The cross term vanishes because $\mathbb E(T-\mathbb E T)=0$ (notes p.55, module 35).
A25For normal data, $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ is
Bias $-\sigma^2/n\to0$ and MSE $\frac{2n-1}{n^2}\sigma^4\to0$ (lecture 8, module 36).
A26A 95% confidence interval for $\mu$ is $[249.22,\,251.18]$. Which statement is correct?
The level is a property of the procedure. A computed interval either contains $\mu$ or not (module 37, CI deck).
A27$X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ unknown, $n=10$. The 95% CI for $\mu$ uses the quantile
Pivot $\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1}$, and $\alpha/2=0.025$ in each tail (lecture 9, module 38).
A28$\sigma$ known. Doubling the sample size multiplies the width of the CI for $\mu$ by
$A=2z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n$. To halve the width you need $4n$ (module 37).
A29For large $n$, the MLE $\hat\theta$ is approximately distributed as
Asymptotic normality and efficiency of the MLE: the variance is the inverse Fisher information (lecture 8, module 36).
A30$X\sim U(0,\theta)$. The maximum likelihood estimator of $\theta$ is
$L(\theta)=\theta^{-n}$ for $\theta\ge\max x_i$ and 0 below, so it is maximised at the jump. $2\bar X$ is the MME. $\ell'=-n/\theta$ never vanishes (module 34).
PART B Multiple choice — R— ⊡ ✕
R1dbinom(2, 5, 0.5) returns
d… = pmf/density at a point: $\binom52/2^5=10/32=0.3125$. p… would give $P(X\le2)=16/32=0.5$.
R2For $X\sim$ Poisson(2), which command gives $P(X>3)$?
$P(X>3)=1-P(X\le3)$ and ppois(3, 2) is $P(X\le3)$. For discrete $X$, "> 3" and "≥ 4" are the same, "> 4" is not.
R3$X\sim N(5,4)$ (variance 4). $P(X<7)$ in R is
R's normal functions take the standard deviation: $\sqrt4=2$. pnorm(7, 5, 2) $=\Phi(1)=0.8413$ (module 25 trap).
R4qnorm(0.975) returns approximately
q… = quantile (inverse CDF): the point with 97.5% of $N(0,1)$ below it, $z_{0.975}\approx1.96$ (module 26).
R5x <- c(2, 4, 6); var(x) returns
Mean 4, squared deviations 4, 0, 4, sum 8 — and var divides by $n-1=2$: 4. Dividing by $n$ would give 8/3.
R6Cards are drawn with replacement until the first ace ($p=1/13$). dgeom(2, 1/13) is
R's geometric counts failures before the first success — the slides' Geometric II: $p q^2=\tfrac1{13}(\tfrac{12}{13})^2\approx0.0655$ (module 21).
R7pexp(1, rate = 2) returns
CDF of the exponential with rate $\lambda=2$ at 1: $1-e^{-2\cdot1}$. The third option is what you get by confusing rate with mean.
R8sum(dbinom(0:10, 10, 1/6)) returns
Summing the pmf over the whole support: the normalisation rule (module 15). pbinom(10, 10, 1/6) gives the same.
R9x <- c(0, 1, 2); f <- c(0.25, 0.5, 0.25); sum(x * f) computes
$\sum x\,f(x)$ is $\mathbb E(X)$ for heads in two flips (module 16). The variance would be sum((x - 1)^2 * f) = 0.5.
R10Which command gives $P(0.2<U<0.7)$ for $U$ uniform on $(0,1)$?
$F(0.7)-F(0.2)=0.7-0.2=0.5$. dunif gives density heights (both 1, difference 0; and dunif(0.5) is 1, not a probability). punif(0.7, 0.2) is the CDF at 0.7 of a uniform on $(0.2,1)$ — the second argument is the lower end, not a second point.
R11set.seed(1); mean(runif(100000)) prints a number close to
The average of many uniform draws approaches $\mathbb E(U)=\tfrac12$ (modules 16, 23). $\tfrac1{12}$ is the variance. set.seed just makes the random draws reproducible.
R12integrate(function(x) 3*x^3, 0, 1) returns 0.75. What was computed?
$\int_0^1x\cdot3x^2\,dx=\tfrac34$ — the slides' example (module 23).

R14x has length 10. Which removes its first and last elements? (R Questions 1)
Negative indices drop elements. x[c(1,10)] selects the first and last; x[x>1] filters by value.
R15Which pair of commands and outputs is consistent? (R Questions 1)
qnorm takes a probability and returns a quantile; pnorm is its inverse and always returns a number in $[0,1]$. A negative "probability" is impossible.
R16k <- 0:100; pdf <- exp(-lam)*lam^k/factorial(k); sum(k*pdf) returns approximately (R Questions 1)
pdf is the Poisson pmf, and $\sum k\,f(k)=\mathbb E(X)=\lambda$ (module 20). sum(pdf) would be the total mass, 1.
R17X <- rbinom(T, n, 0.2); sd(X) is approximately (R Questions 1)
$T$ draws of $\text{Bin}(n,0.2)$: sd $=\sqrt{n\cdot0.2\cdot0.8}=0.4\sqrt n$, and the sample sd is close to it only when there are many draws ($T$ high).
R18$X\sim N(\mu=2,\ \sigma^2=0.0625)$. The pdf $f_X(0.5)$ in R is (R Questions 2)
R wants the sd, $\sqrt{0.0625}=0.25$. The standardised version needs the Jacobian: dnorm((0.5-2)/0.25)/0.25 is also right, while the third option forgets the division.
R19L <- price > th; D[L,] does what? (R Questions 3)
Rows of a data frame are statistical units, columns are variables (module 27). L is a logical vector selecting rows.
R20sum(abs(returns) > th) returns (R Questions 3)
abs(returns) > th is TRUE/FALSE; sum counts the TRUEs. mean would give the share.
R21$X\sim N(10,20)$, $n=25$. What does 1 - pchisq(12, 24) compute?
$1.2S^2\sim\chi^2_{24}$ and $S^2>10\Leftrightarrow1.2S^2>12$ (notes p.43, module 30). The cut-off would be qchisq(0.95,24)/1.2.
R22mean(x) + c(-1,1) * qt(0.975, length(x)-1) * sd(x) / sqrt(length(x)) is
$\bar x\mp t_{0.025,n-1}\,s/\sqrt n$, the same as t.test(x)$conf.int (module 38). 0.975 is $1-\alpha/2$ for $\alpha=0.05$.
R23optim minimises a function. To get the MLE from a log-likelihood ll(th, x), pass
Maximising $\ell$ is minimising $-\ell$. Run it from several starting values, as the lecture recommends (module 34).
R24var(replicate(10000, mean(rpois(50, 3)))) prints a number close to
10,000 sample means of size 50 from Pois(3): $\mathbb V(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n=3/50=0.06$ (module 29).
PART C Open questions — write the full solution before opening it— ⊡ ✕
O1●●modules 08–10Two suppliers
A firm buys components from supplier A (70%) and supplier B (30%). Defect rates: 2% for A, 5% for B.
(a) What is the probability that a random component is defective?
(b) A component is defective. What is the probability it came from B?
(c) A machine uses 3 components from A, independently. What is the probability that at least one is defective?
Solution
  1. (a) Total probability over the partition $\{A,B\}$: $$P(D)=P(D\mid A)P(A)+P(D\mid B)P(B)=0.02\cdot0.7+0.05\cdot0.3=0.014+0.015=0.029.$$
  2. (b) Bayes: $P(B\mid D)=\dfrac{0.05\cdot0.3}{0.029}=\dfrac{0.015}{0.029}\approx0.517$. B supplies only 30% but causes over half the defects.
  3. (c) Complement + independence: $P(\text{none defective})=0.98^3=0.941192$, so $P(\text{at least one})=1-0.941192\approx0.0588$.
O2●●modules 13, 14, 22, 23From a CDF to everything else
$F(t)=0$ for $t<0$, $F(t)=t^2/4$ for $0\le t\le2$, $F(t)=1$ for $t>2$.
(a) Check that $F$ is a CDF. (b) Find $P(X\le1)$, $P(0.5<X\le1.5)$ and $P(X=1)$. (c) Find the density $f$ and $\mathbb E(X)$. (d) Find the distribution of $Y=X^2$.
Solution
  1. (a) Values in $[0,1]$; non-decreasing ($t^2/4$ increases on $[0,2]$); $F\to0$ and $F\to1$ at the ends; continuous (at 0: $0$; at 2: $4/4=1$), hence right-continuous ✓.
  2. (b) $P(X\le1)=F(1)=\tfrac14$. $P(0.5<X\le1.5)=F(1.5)-F(0.5)=\tfrac{2.25-0.25}{4}=0.5$. $P(X=1)=0$ — no jump.
  3. (c) $f(t)=F'(t)=t/2$ on $(0,2)$, 0 elsewhere. $\mathbb E(X)=\int_0^2t\cdot\tfrac t2\,dt=\big[\tfrac{t^3}{6}\big]_0^2=\tfrac86=\tfrac43$.
  4. (d) CDF method: for $0\le y\le4$, $F_Y(y)=P(X^2\le y)=P(X\le\sqrt y)=\tfrac{(\sqrt y)^2}{4}=\tfrac y4$. So $Y$ is uniform on $(0,4)$ — a random point in $(0,4)$.
O3●modules 15–17A pmf with a constant
$X$ takes the values $0,1,2,3$ with $f(k)=c(k+1)$.
(a) Find $c$. (b) Find $P(X\ge2)$. (c) Find $\mathbb E(X)$ and $\mathbb V(X)$. (d) Find $\mathbb E(2X-1)$ and $\mathbb V(2X-1)$.
Solution
  1. (a) $c(1+2+3+4)=10c=1\Rightarrow c=\tfrac1{10}$: probabilities $0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4$.
  2. (b) $P(X\ge2)=0.3+0.4=0.7$.
  3. (c) $\mathbb E(X)=0(0.1)+1(0.2)+2(0.3)+3(0.4)=2$. $\mathbb E(X^2)=0+0.2+1.2+3.6=5$, so $\mathbb V(X)=5-2^2=1$.
  4. (d) $\mathbb E(2X-1)=2\cdot2-1=3$; $\mathbb V(2X-1)=2^2\cdot1=4$.
O4●●●modules 33–36 · the exam's open questionA full MLE exercise
$X_1,\dots,X_n$ i.i.d. with pdf $f(x;\theta)=\dfrac x\theta\,e^{-x^2/(2\theta)}$, $x>0$, $\theta>0$. You may use $\mathbb E(X^2)=2\theta$.
(a) Write the log-likelihood. (b) Find the MLE of $\theta$, checking the second-order condition and the boundaries. (c) Is it unbiased? (d) Find the MLE of $\sqrt\theta$. (e) Is $\hat\theta$ consistent, given $\mathbb V(X^2)=4\theta^2$?
Solution
  1. (a) $\ln f(x;\theta)=\ln x-\ln\theta-\frac{x^2}{2\theta}$, so $\ell(\theta)=\sum\ln x_i-n\ln\theta-\frac{\sum x_i^2}{2\theta}$.
  2. (b) FOC. $\ell'(\theta)=-\frac n\theta+\frac{\sum x_i^2}{2\theta^2}=0\Rightarrow\hat\theta=\frac{\sum x_i^2}{2n}$.
    SOC. $\ell''(\theta)=\frac n{\theta^2}-\frac{\sum x_i^2}{\theta^3}$; at $\hat\theta$, $\sum x_i^2=2n\hat\theta$, so $\ell''(\hat\theta)=\frac n{\hat\theta^2}-\frac{2n}{\hat\theta^2}=-\frac n{\hat\theta^2}<0$ ✓.
    Boundaries. $\theta\to0^+$: $-\frac{\sum x_i^2}{2\theta}\to-\infty$ faster than $-n\ln\theta\to+\infty$, so $\ell\to-\infty$. $\theta\to\infty$: $-n\ln\theta\to-\infty$ and the last term $\to0$, so $\ell\to-\infty$. Global maximum.
  3. (c) $\mathbb E(\hat\theta)=\frac{n\,\mathbb E(X^2)}{2n}=\frac{2\theta}2=\theta$: unbiased.
  4. (d) Invariance: $\widehat{\sqrt\theta}=\sqrt{\hat\theta}=\sqrt{\sum x_i^2/(2n)}$.
  5. (e) $\mathbb V(\hat\theta)=\frac{n\cdot4\theta^2}{4n^2}=\frac{\theta^2}n\to0$ and the bias is 0, so the MSE $\to0$: strongly, hence weakly, consistent.
O5●●modules 32, 34, 35Moments versus likelihood for U(0, θ)
$X\sim U(0,\theta)$. (a) Find the MME $\tilde\theta$. (b) Show it is unbiased and compute its MSE. (c) For the sample $(1,2,9)$ compute $\tilde\theta$ and the MLE $\hat\theta$, and comment.
Solution
  1. (a) $\mathbb E(X)=\theta/2=\bar X\Rightarrow\tilde\theta=2\bar X$.
  2. (b) $\mathbb E(2\bar X)=2\cdot\frac\theta2=\theta$. $\mathbb V(2\bar X)=4\cdot\frac{\theta^2/12}n=\frac{\theta^2}{3n}$. Unbiased, so $\text{MSE}=\frac{\theta^2}{3n}$.
  3. (c) $\tilde\theta=2\cdot4=8$, but $\hat\theta=\max x_i=9$. The MME contradicts the data: an observation of 9 is impossible if $\theta=8$. The MLE always respects the support.
O6●●modules 37–38Two intervals and a sample size
16 normal observations give $\bar x=102$ and $s=8$.
(a) 95% CI for $\mu$. (b) The same if $\sigma=8$ were known. (c) With $\sigma=8$ known, how many observations for a 95% CI narrower than 4?
Solution
  1. (a) $t_{0.025,15}=2.131$: $102\mp2.131\cdot\frac8{4}=102\mp4.26=[97.74,\ 106.26]$.
  2. (b) $102\mp1.96\cdot2=[98.08,\ 105.92]$: narrower, because $z_{0.025}<t_{0.025,15}$.
  3. (c) $2\cdot1.96\cdot\frac8{\sqrt n}<4\Rightarrow n>\big(\frac{2\cdot1.96\cdot8}4\big)^2=61.47$, so $n=62$.
