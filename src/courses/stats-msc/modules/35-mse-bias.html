MSE, bias & efficiency

lecture 7, notes pp.54–57 · Point Estimation deck §6–7
READ FIRST Several estimators for one parameter: which one is best?— ⊡ ✕

For $\sigma^2$ we now have two candidates, $S^2$ (unbiased) and $\hat\sigma^2$ (MLE, MME, analogy, but biased). For a Poisson $\lambda$ we have $\bar X$, $S^2$ and every mix of them. We need a score for estimators. The course uses the mean squared error:

$$\text{MSE}_\theta(T)=\mathbb E\big[(T-\theta)^2\big]=\underbrace{\mathbb V(T)}_{\text{variability}}+\underbrace{B_\theta(T)^2}_{\text{distortion}^2}.$$

Lower is better. The decomposition is the key idea. An estimator can miss because it scatters (variance) or because it aims at the wrong place (bias). A small bias can be worth paying for a large cut in variance, and that is exactly what happens with $\hat\sigma^2$ versus $S^2$.

EXAM (lecturer, notes pp.55–56)
MCQ: "Two estimators: $T_1$ is better than $T_2$ because it has… higher MSE / lower MSE / …". And: "Choose one estimator because of lower bias? No: overall MSE, which also has variability. Preferred even if biased."
LECTURE 7 MSE and efficiency (notes pp.54–55 · deck §6)— ⊡ ✕

We compare estimators of $\theta$ in terms of the MSE (mean squared error):

$$\boxed{\;\text{MSE}_\theta(T)=\mathbb E\big[(T-\theta)^2\big]\;}$$

An estimator $T^*$ of $\theta$ is more efficient than $T$ if

$$\text{MSE}_\theta(T^*)\le\text{MSE}_\theta(T)\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta,$$

with a strict $\text{MSE}_\theta(T^*)<\text{MSE}_\theta(T)$ for at least one $\theta$. "For all $\theta$" matters: we don't know the true $\theta$, so $T^*$ must win (or tie) whatever it is.

Alternative measures (notes p.55):

  • Mean absolute error $\text{MAE}=\mathbb E\big[|T-\theta|\big]$: harder to work with mathematically (the absolute value has no derivative at 0). It weights large deviations less than the square, so it "hides" them.
  • Root MSE $\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\text{MSE}_\theta(T)}$: expressed in the same unit as $T$, since we undo the square. If $T$ is in euros, MSE is in euros², RMSE in euros.

The deck prefers MSE because it is "easier to treat analytically", and because of the decomposition below.

THEOREM MSE = variance + bias² (notes p.55)— ⊡ ✕

Add and subtract $\mathbb E(T)$ inside the square:

$$\begin{aligned}\text{MSE}_\theta(T)&=\mathbb E\Big[\big((T-\mathbb E(T))+(\mathbb E(T)-\theta)\big)^2\Big]\\ &=\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(T-\mathbb E(T))^2\big]}_{\mathbb V(T)}+\underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(\mathbb E(T)-\theta)^2\big]}_{\text{not random}}+2\big(\mathbb E(T)-\theta\big)\underbrace{\mathbb E\big(T-\mathbb E(T)\big)}_{=0}\\ &=\mathbb V(T)+\big(\mathbb E(T)-\theta\big)^2\end{aligned}$$ $$\boxed{\;\text{MSE}_\theta(T)=\mathbb V(T)+B_\theta(T)^2\;}$$
  • $\mathbb V(T)$: variability of the estimator, the source of randomness;
  • $B_\theta(T)=\mathbb E(T)-\theta$: the bias, the distortion of the estimator.
$B_\theta(T)>0$overestimation: on average $T$ lands above $\theta$
$B_\theta(T)=0$$T$ is unbiased: $\mathbb E(T)=\theta$, and then $\text{MSE}=\mathbb V(T)$
$B_\theta(T)<0$underestimation
LECTURE 7 · notes p.56 · deck pp.53–55 · $S^2$ versus $\hat\sigma^2$ for $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$

Two estimators of $\sigma^2$: $S^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\frac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=\frac{n-1}nS^2$.

For $S^2$. $\mathbb E(S^2)=\sigma^2$, so $B_{\sigma^2}(S^2)=0$: "$S^2$ unbiased for $\sigma^2$". And $\mathbb V(S^2)=\frac2{n-1}\sigma^4$ (module 29, normal data). So

$$\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(S^2)=\mathbb V(S^2)+\underbrace{B^2}_{0}=\boxed{\frac2{n-1}\sigma^4}$$

For $\hat\sigma^2$.

  1. $\mathbb E(\hat\sigma^2)=\mathbb E\big(\frac{n-1}nS^2\big)=\frac{n-1}n\sigma^2$.
  2. $B_{\sigma^2}(\hat\sigma^2)=\frac{n-1}n\sigma^2-\sigma^2=\big(1-\frac1n\big)\sigma^2-\sigma^2=-\frac1n\sigma^2<0$: underestimation of $\sigma^2$.
  3. $\mathbb V(\hat\sigma^2)=\big(\frac{n-1}n\big)^2\mathbb V(S^2)=\frac{(n-1)^2}{n^2}\cdot\frac2{n-1}\sigma^4=\frac{2(n-1)}{n^2}\sigma^4$.
  4. $\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(\hat\sigma^2)=\frac{2n-2}{n^2}\sigma^4+\frac1{n^2}\sigma^4=\boxed{\frac{2n-1}{n^2}\sigma^4}$

Ratio.

$$\frac{\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(\hat\sigma^2)}{\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(S^2)}=\frac{2n-1}{n^2}\cdot\frac{n-1}2=\underbrace{\Big(\frac{2n-1}{2n}\Big)}_{<1}\underbrace{\Big(\frac{n-1}n\Big)}_{<1}<1$$

for every $n$ and every $\sigma^2$. Therefore

$$\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(\hat\sigma^2)<\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(S^2)$$

⇒ we prefer $\hat\sigma^2$ to $S^2$, even if $\hat\sigma^2$ is biased. It is more efficient: it gives up a little accuracy (bias) for a larger gain in precision (variance).

LECTURE 7 No uniformly best estimator, so restrict to unbiased ones: the UMVUE (notes p.57 · deck §7)— ⊡ ✕

Example. $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. Two estimators of $\sigma^2$: $T_1=S^2$ and $T_2=40$, a number that ignores the data.

  1. $\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(S^2)=\frac2{n-1}\sigma^4$ (previous example).
  2. $\text{MSE}_{\sigma^2}(T_2)=\mathbb V(T_2)+B_{\sigma^2}(T_2)^2=0+(40-\sigma^2)^2=(\sigma^2-40)^2$. A constant has zero variance.
  3. In a region around $\sigma^2=40$, $T_2$ is preferred: if the truth happens to be near 40, guessing 40 is excellent. Elsewhere $S^2$ wins.

Generally: an estimator with uniformly minimum MSE does not exist. Which one is best depends on the "state of nature", the unknown true $\theta$.

The way out, as the notes put it:

  • ⇒ the class of all estimators is too large to find a $T$ that is best for all $\theta$'s (constants like $T_2$ always win somewhere);
  • ⇒ it is possible if we restrict to unbiased estimators, where $\text{MSE}=\mathbb V(T)$;
  • ⇒ the $T$ with minimum MSE among all unbiased estimators is called the UMVUE, the uniform minimum variance unbiased estimator: $$\mathbb E(T^*)=\theta\ \text{ and }\ \mathbb V(T^*)\le\mathbb V(T)\ \ \forall\theta,\ \forall\text{ unbiased }T.$$
Deck examples of unbiased estimators
$\bar X$ is unbiased for $\mu$ in every model, so $\text{MSE}_\mu(\bar X)=\mathbb V(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}n$. $S^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$ in every model. For $X\sim\text{Pois}(\lambda)$, $\lambda$ is both the mean and the variance, so $\bar X$, $S^2$, and every mix $a\bar X+(1-a)S^2$ are unbiased for $\lambda$: infinitely many. The UMVUE is the one with the smallest variance (it turns out to be $\bar X$).
CI DECK · insurance survey · how large must n be for a small MSE?

$X\sim\text{Bern}(p)$: is a customer interested in a new product? Estimator $\hat p=\bar X$. Which $n$ guarantees $\text{MSE}<0.005$?

  1. $\hat p$ is unbiased, so $\text{MSE}=\mathbb V(\bar X)=\frac{p(1-p)}n$.
  2. $p$ is unknown, but $p(1-p)\le\frac14$ (a downward parabola with its maximum at $p=\frac12$). So $\text{MSE}\le\frac1{4n}$.
  3. $\frac1{4n}<0.005\Rightarrow n>50$.
  4. With $n=100$ and $\hat p=0.10$: estimated MSE $=\frac{0.1\cdot0.9}{100}=0.0009<0.005$ ✓, and estimated standard error $0.03$.
TRAP Reading MSE questions— ⊡ ✕
  • "Better" means lower MSE, not lower bias and not lower variance taken alone.
  • MSE depends on $\theta$. "$T_1$ is more efficient" needs $\text{MSE}_\theta(T_1)\le\text{MSE}_\theta(T_2)$ for every $\theta$, not at one value.
  • Bias squared, not bias. $\text{MSE}=\mathbb V+B^2$. A negative bias still adds a positive amount.
  • Constants pass through bias. $B(cT)=c\,\mathbb E(T)-\theta$, and $\mathbb V(cT)=c^2\mathbb V(T)$ (module 17).
?check yourselfTwo estimators of a mean
$X_1,X_2$ i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Compare $T_1=\bar X$ and $T_2=\frac23X_1+\frac13X_2$.
Answer
Both unbiased ($\frac23+\frac13=1$). $\mathbb V(T_1)=\frac{\sigma^2}2$; $\mathbb V(T_2)=\frac49\sigma^2+\frac19\sigma^2=\frac59\sigma^2>\frac12\sigma^2$. $T_1$ has the lower MSE for every $\sigma^2$, so it is more efficient. Equal weights minimise the variance.
?check yourselfA shrunk mean
$T=0.9\bar X$ for $\mu$, with $\mathbb V(X)=\sigma^2$. Bias, variance, MSE? When does it beat $\bar X$?
Answer
$B=-0.1\mu$, $\mathbb V=0.81\frac{\sigma^2}n$, $\text{MSE}=0.81\frac{\sigma^2}n+0.01\mu^2$. It beats $\bar X$ when $0.01\mu^2<0.19\frac{\sigma^2}n$, i.e. for $\mu$ near 0. Not uniformly better: the same story as $T_2=40$.
