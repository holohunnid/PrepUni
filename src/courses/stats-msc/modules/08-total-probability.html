08

Total probability

lecture 1, notes p.12 · slides: Conditional 10–11
READ FIRST Break a hard probability into easy pieces— ⊡ ✕

Often $P(A)$ is hard to see directly, but easy once you know which "scenario" you are in: the chance that a chip is defective depends on which factory made it; the chance that a test is positive depends on whether you are ill. The law of total probability says: list the scenarios (they must not overlap and must cover everything), work out $A$'s probability inside each one, and add.

$$P(A)=\sum_{\text{scenarios }i}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$$

It is the denominator of Bayes' theorem (module 10), so it is worth getting completely comfortable with it here.

THEOREM Two pieces: B and its complement— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{P(A)=P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c)}$$

Proof (notes p.12) — every step has a reason:

  1. $P(A)=P(A\cap\Omega)$ — because $A\subseteq\Omega$, so $A\cap\Omega=A$.
  2. $\phantom{P(A)}=P\big(A\cap(B\cup B^c)\big)$ — because $B\cup B^c=\Omega$.
  3. $\phantom{P(A)}=P\big((A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c)\big)$ — distributive law (module 02).
  4. $\phantom{P(A)}=P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c)$ — the two pieces are mutually exclusive (nothing is in both $B$ and $B^c$), so finite additivity applies. $\square$

With the multiplication law (module 07) each piece becomes "conditional × probability of the scenario":

$$P(A)=P(A\mid B)P(B)+P(A\mid B^c)P(B^c)$$
EXAMPLE · traffic
It rains on 30% of days. A traffic jam happens with probability 0.6 on rainy days and 0.2 on dry days. Then $$P(\text{jam})=0.6\cdot0.3+0.2\cdot0.7=0.18+0.14=0.32.$$ The overall probability is a weighted average of the scenario probabilities 0.6 and 0.2, weighted by how often each scenario occurs. So it must lie between them.
DEF A partition of Ω— ⊡ ✕

Instead of just $B$ and $B^c$ we can cut $\Omega$ into any number of pieces. A partition of $\Omega$ is a collection of events $B_1,B_2,\dots,B_m$ such that

$$B_i\cap B_j=\emptyset\ \ \forall i\neq j\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}B_i=\Omega$$

— they never overlap, and together they cover everything (the slides also ask each $B_i$ to be non-empty). Every outcome lands in exactly one piece. $\{B,B^c\}$ is the simplest partition.

THEOREM General law of total probability— ⊡ ✕

If $B_1,\dots,B_m$ is a partition of $\Omega$ and $A$ is any event,

$$\boxed{P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}P(A\cap B_i)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}P(A\mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}$$

Why: exactly as in the two-piece proof. $A=A\cap\Omega=A\cap(B_1\cup\dots\cup B_m)=(A\cap B_1)\cup\dots\cup(A\cap B_m)$; the pieces are disjoint because the $B_i$ are; finite additivity adds them up. The second form is the multiplication law applied to each term.

The tree picture

Draw the scenarios as first-level branches (weights $P(B_i)$), then $A$ as a second-level branch inside each (weights $P(A\mid B_i)$). Multiply along each path to $A$, then add the paths. The example on the right is the three-factory problem of module 10.

Checklist before using it
1. Do the $B_i$ really not overlap? 2. Do they really cover every possibility? If either fails, the sum is wrong. Complements and "which of $m$ sources" are the classic safe partitions.
WHERE IT IS USED · the denominators of module 10–11
  1. Rare disease. Partition: ill ($D$) or not ($D^c$). $$P(+)=P(+\mid D)P(D)+P(+\mid D^c)P(D^c)=0.99\cdot0.001+0.05\cdot0.999=0.05094$$
  2. Monty Hall. Partition: the car is behind door 1, 2 or 3 ($A,B,C$, each $\tfrac13$). You picked door 1; the host opens door 2 with probability $\tfrac12$, $0$, $1$ in the three cases: $$P(\text{opens }2)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13+0\cdot\tfrac13+1\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac12$$
  3. Stock tree (module 07). Partition: first move up or down. $$P(P_2=P_0)=P(\text{down}\mid\text{up})P(\text{up})+P(\text{up}\mid\text{down})P(\text{down})=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23+\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac49$$
TRAP Forgetting a scenario, or overlapping ones— ⊡ ✕

Writing $P(\text{jam})=0.6\cdot0.3$ and stopping forgets the dry days: that's $P(\text{jam}\cap\text{rain})$, not $P(\text{jam})$. And using scenarios like "rain" and "cold" (which can happen together) double counts the cold rainy days. Always check: every outcome in exactly one scenario.
