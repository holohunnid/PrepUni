Confidence intervals: σ known

lecture 8 pp.5–7 · lecture 9 pp.1–7 · Confidence Intervals deck
READ FIRST From one number to a range, with a guarantee attached— ⊡ ✕

We move from point estimation to interval estimation. A point estimate is a single value "reasonably" assigned to $\theta$. Example: the unknown $p$ = % of people voting for a political party, say $\hat p=0.4$. An interval estimate gives a range of reasonable values for $\theta$, e.g. $p\in(0.35,\,0.45)$, and so it also provides a quantification of the uncertainty.

The interval always comes with a number called the confidence level, which expresses how confident we are that the interval contains the true $\theta$. Typical levels: 90%, 95%, 99%. This module builds the first and most important interval, for the mean of a normal population with known $\sigma$:

$$\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu)=\Big[\bar X\mp z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big]=\big[\text{point estimator}\mp\text{margin of error}\big].$$
What you need for the exam
The definition $P(L_1<\theta<L_2)=1-\alpha$, and the correct interpretation (a favourite MCQ). The derivation from the pivotal quantity. Computing $z_{\alpha/2}$ with qnorm(1-α/2). The width $2z_{\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n$ and how it reacts to $n$, $\sigma$ and the level. The sample-size calculation.
LECTURE 8 Confidence level: meaning and first intuitions (lecture 8, pp.5–7)— ⊡ ✕

Interpretation of the confidence level. Say 90% means that if we collect a high number of samples (from the same population) and compute a confidence interval in each of the samples, 90% of them will include the true $\theta$.

In practice we have a unique sample, and we don't know if we have one of those 90% of cases where the interval includes the true $\theta$.

Notation: confidence level $1-\alpha\ \Rightarrow\ \text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\theta)$. Level 90% ⇒ $\alpha=0.10$; level 95% ⇒ $\alpha=0.05$; level 99% ⇒ $\alpha=0.01$.

Some intuitions on CIs. $X\sim\text{Bern}(p)$, $\hat p=0.4$:

$$\text{CI}_{95\%}(p)=[0.38,\,0.42]\quad\text{or}\quad\text{CI}_{99\%}(p)=[0.36,\,0.44]$$

We are more confident that the 99% CI contains $p$, since it is wider, at the cost of less precision. ⇒ At a given $1-\alpha$, a tighter CI is better (more informative).

  • if $(1-\alpha)\uparrow$ ⇒ more confident ⇒ wider CI;
  • if $n\uparrow$ ⇒ more data ⇒ more info ⇒ more informative CI ⇒ smaller CI;
  • if $\sigma^2\uparrow$ ⇒ more uncertainty ⇒ wider CI.
DEF Confidence interval (lecture 9, p.1 · deck)— ⊡ ✕

$\underline X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ i.i.d. sample, $X_i\sim\text{Population}(\theta)$. We estimate $\theta$ with the interval $[L_1,L_2]$ (lower bound, upper bound), with confidence level $1-\alpha$, if

$$\boxed{\;P(L_1<\theta<L_2)=1-\alpha\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta\;}$$

$L_1=L_1(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ and $L_2$ are functions of the sample, so they are random variables. The probability is about the random interval catching the fixed $\theta$. $A=L_2-L_1$ is the width (or size) of the interval.

TRAP · the interpretation (CI deck)
A 95% CI for mean weekly consumption is $[150,200]$. Can we say $P(150<\mu<200)=0.95$? No. Once computed, $[150,200]$ has fixed endpoints and $\mu$ is a fixed number: the statement "$\mu$ is inside" is either true or false, with probability 1 or 0. A priori (before drawing the sample) the probability of drawing a sample whose interval includes $\mu$ is $1-\alpha$. A posteriori we are confident that we drew one of those samples. That is why we say "with confidence level 95%", never "with probability 95%".
LECTURE 9 · pp.1–4 · deck · the product-weight machine: X ~ N(μ, σ²), σ = 2.5 known

$X$ = product weight (a cup of margarine, in the deck). $\mu$ unknown; $\sigma=2.5$ g is known from history. The machine should be calibrated on 250 g: is it? We will observe deviations from 250 g either because of a wrong calibration or because of sample variability with the right calibration. We collect $n=25$ products: $\bar x=250.2$ g.

  1. Start from the pivot. $\bar X\sim N\big(\mu,\frac{2.5^2}n\big)$, i.e. $\frac{\sigma^2}n$, so $Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)$.
  2. Put $1-\alpha=95\%$ in the middle of $N(0,1)$: find $z_1<z_2$ with $P(z_1<Z<z_2)=1-\alpha$, with $\alpha/2=2.5\%$ in each tail.
  3. Invert to reach the form $P(L_1<\theta<L_2)=1-\alpha$: $$\begin{aligned}&P\Big(z_1<\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}<z_2\Big)=1-\alpha\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &P\Big(z_1\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}<\bar X-\mu<z_2\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\Big)=1-\alpha\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &P\Big(-\bar X+z_1\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}<-\mu<-\bar X+z_2\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\Big)=1-\alpha\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &P\Big(\underbrace{\bar X-z_2\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}}_{L_1}<\mu<\underbrace{\bar X-z_1\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}}_{L_2}\Big)=1-\alpha\end{aligned}$$ (multiplying by $-1$ flips the inequalities).
  1. Lower bound. $z_2$ is the quantile of $N(0,1)$ with $\frac\alpha2$ probability on its right, denoted $z_{\alpha/2}=$ qnorm(1-α/2) (R wants the probability on the left). At 95%: $z_{0.025}=$ qnorm(1-0.025) $=+1.96$. So $L_1=\bar X-1.96\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}$.
  2. Upper bound. $z_1$ has $1-\frac\alpha2$ on its right: $z_{1-\alpha/2}=$ qnorm(α/2) $=$ qnorm(1-0.975) $=-1.96$. So $L_2=\bar X-(-1.96)\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}=\bar X+1.96\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}$.
  3. Overall, exploiting the symmetry $-z_{\alpha/2}=z_{1-\alpha/2}$: $$\boxed{\text{CI}_{95\%}(\mu)=\Big[\bar X\mp1.96\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\Big],\quad X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\ \sigma^2\text{ known}}$$
  4. Realised value. $\big[250.2\mp1.96\cdot\frac{2.5}{\sqrt{25}}\big]=[250.2\mp0.98]=\mathbf{[249.22,\ 251.18]}$.
  5. Conclusion. The CI is the set of "reasonable" values for $\mu$. 250 g belongs to it, so 250 g is among the reasonable values: the deviation of the observed 250.2 g from 250 g is due to sample variability, not to a wrong calibration.

Width: $A=L_2-L_1=\Big(\bar X+z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\Big)-\Big(\bar X-z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\Big)=\boxed{2z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}}$

  • if $n\uparrow$ ⇒ $A\downarrow$, smaller CI (sometimes feasible: collect more data);
  • if $\sigma^2\uparrow$ ⇒ $A\uparrow$, wider CI (not under control);
  • if the level $\uparrow$ ⇒ $(1-\alpha)\uparrow$ ⇒ $\alpha\downarrow$ ⇒ $z_{\alpha/2}\uparrow$ ⇒ $A\uparrow$, wider CI (free to modify).

Note that $A$ here does not depend on the data at all: it is fixed before sampling. That changes when $\sigma$ is unknown (module 38).

Deck: a non-central interval $[\bar X-z_1\frac\sigma{\sqrt n},\bar X+z_2\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}]$ with unequal tails also has level $1-\alpha$, but it is wider. That is why we split $\alpha$ equally, $\alpha/2$ per tail.

INTERACTIVE Fifty samples, fifty intervals: what "95%" means— ⊡ ✕

Try this. At 95% about 2–3 of 50 bars are red. Switch to 99%: fewer red bars, all bars longer. Raise $n$: the bars shrink like $1/\sqrt n$ but the share of red stays the same. The level is about coverage, not about width. Finally, "σ unknown but z used, n = 5" plugs $S$ into the normal formula: coverage drops to about 88%, well below the advertised 95%. That is why module 38 needs the Student-t.

METHOD The pivotal quantity (lecture 9, pp.5–6 · deck)— ⊡ ✕

The way we build CIs is based on the method of the pivotal quantity (PQ). In the example the PQ was $Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$. A PQ is:

  1. a function of $\underline X$ and of the $\theta$ we want to learn (in the example: of $\underline X$ through $\bar X$ and $n$, and of $\mu$);
  2. with a distribution that does not depend on $\theta$ (in the example $Z\sim N(0,1)$, which does not depend on $\mu$);
  3. invertible with respect to $\theta$ (in the example, $Z$ was inverted to find $\mu$).

The PQ for a location parameter has the form

$$\boxed{\;PQ=\frac{\hat\theta-\theta}{\hat\sigma_{\hat\theta}}=\frac{\text{point estimator}-\text{parameter}}{\text{(estimated) st. dev. of the point estimator}}\;}$$

In the example, $PQ=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$. Module 36's asymptotic normality of the MLE, $\frac{\hat\theta-\theta}{\sqrt{1/I(\hat\theta)}}\approx N(0,1)$, is a PQ of exactly this form.

Remark: CIs for functions of θ. If we have $[L_1,L_2]$ for $\theta$ at level $1-\alpha$, then we have a CI for $g(\theta)$ for any monotone $g$:

  • $g$ increasing: $[g(L_1),g(L_2)]$;
  • $g$ decreasing: $[g(L_2),g(L_1)]$, with the bounds swapped.

Example: $\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\sigma^2)=[L_1,L_2]$ gives

$$\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\sigma)=\big[\sqrt{L_1},\sqrt{L_2}\big]$$

since $g(\sigma^2)=\sqrt{\sigma^2}$, a CI for the standard deviation, and

$$\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(1/\sigma^2)=\big[1/L_2,\ 1/L_1\big]$$

since $g(\sigma^2)=1/\sigma^2$, a CI for the precision parameter (decreasing, so swapped).

Why it works: $L_1<\sigma^2<L_2\Leftrightarrow\sqrt{L_1}<\sigma<\sqrt{L_2}$, the same event, so the same probability $1-\alpha$.

LECTURE 9 · pp.6–7 · deck · how many observations for a CI of a given width?

Monthly IT expenditure of firms, $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2=400$. What is the minimum $n$ to have a $\text{CI}_{95\%}(\mu)$ of width below 10?

  1. $1-\alpha=0.95\Rightarrow\alpha=0.05$, $z_{0.025}=$ qnorm(1-0.025) $=1.96$.
  2. $A=2z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}<10\Rightarrow2z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{10}<\sqrt n\Rightarrow n>\Big(\frac{2z_{\alpha/2}\sigma}{10}\Big)^2$.
  3. $=\Big(\frac{2\cdot1.96\cdot\sqrt{400}}{10}\Big)^2=7.84^2=61.46$.
  4. Round up: we need to collect at least $n=\mathbf{62}$ observations. (Rounding down to 61 would give a width just above 10.)

Now we collect $n=64$ with $\sum x_i=768$. $\bar x=768/64=12$:

$$\text{CI}_{95\%}(\mu)=\Big[12\mp1.96\frac{\sqrt{400}}{\sqrt{64}}\Big]=[12\mp4.9]=\mathbf{[7.1,\ 16.9]}$$

$A=16.9-7.1=9.8<10$, as expected since $n>62$.

CI DECK · follow-ups and one more
  1. Same data at 99%. $z_{0.005}=$ qnorm(0.995) $=2.576$: $[12\mp2.576\cdot2.5]=[5.56,\ 18.44]$, width $12.88$. Higher level ⇒ wider.
  2. σ = 10 instead of 20, at 95%. $[12\mp1.96\cdot\frac{10}8]=[9.55,\ 14.45]$, width 4.9: halving $\sigma$ halves the width. (The deck's numbers $(8.79,15.21)$ for this question use $z=2.57$, so they are the 99% interval.)

?check yourselfHalve the width
A 95% CI with known $\sigma$ from $n=25$ has width 2. What $n$ gives width 1? And what level would width 2 have with $n=25$ if $z$ were 2.576?
Answer
Width $\propto1/\sqrt n$: halving it needs $4n=100$. With $z=2.576$ the width scales by $2.576/1.96$, to $2.63$, at level 99%. Width 2 with $n=25$ is only possible at 95%.

A 95% CI for $\mu$ is $[3.1, 4.7]$. (a) $P(3.1<\mu<4.7)=0.95$. (b) 95% of the data lie in $[3.1,4.7]$. (c) The procedure catches $\mu$ in 95% of samples. (d) $\bar x$ lies in the interval with probability 0.95.

(c). (a) treats the fixed $\mu$ as random. (b) confuses the CI for the mean with the spread of the data. (d) $\bar x$ is the centre of the interval, so it is always inside.
