Independence

lecture 1, notes p.13–14 · lecture 2, notes p.1–2 · slides: Conditional 12–17
READ FIRST When knowing one thing tells you nothing about the other— ⊡ ✕

Module 07 showed that information can change probabilities. Independence is the special case where it does not: learning that $A$ happened leaves the probability of $B$ exactly where it was. Two tosses of a coin, the returns of two unrelated firms (as a first approximation), the failures of two separately built components.

Independence is what lets you multiply probabilities: $P(\text{both})=P(\text{first})\,P(\text{second})$. Almost every model in this course — the binomial, the sample mean, the likelihood in estimation — is built on it. The lecture also shows its subtle side: three events can be independent in pairs and still not independent together.

DEF Independent events— ⊡ ✕

Two events $A$ and $B$ are independent, written $A\perp\!\!\!\perp B$, if and only if

$$\boxed{P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)}$$

Why this is the right definition

By the law of multiplication (module 07), always $P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B\mid A)$. Compare with the definition: the two agree exactly when

$$P(B\mid A)=P(B)\qquad\text{"knowledge of }A\text{ does not affect the probability of }B\text{"}$$

and, using the other form $P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)$, exactly when

$$P(A\mid B)=P(A)\qquad\text{"knowledge of }B\text{ does not affect the probability of }A\text{"}$$

The product form is preferred as the definition because it is symmetric and still makes sense when $P(A)=0$ or $P(B)=0$ (where the conditional forms would divide by zero).

SEE IT Independence is a rectangle— ⊡ ✕
Trap: independent ≠ disjoint
Disjoint events are as dependent as can be: if $A$ happens, $B$ certainly does not. Formally, $P(A\cap B)=0$ while $P(A)P(B)>0$ whenever both have positive probability. Coin: "heads" and "tails" on the same toss are disjoint and not independent.
RULE Complements inherit independence— ⊡ ✕

If $A\perp\!\!\!\perp B$, then also

$$A^c\perp\!\!\!\perp B^c:\ P(A^c\cap B^c)=P(A^c)P(B^c),\quad A^c\perp\!\!\!\perp B,\quad A\perp\!\!\!\perp B^c.$$

It would be strange otherwise: if knowing "A" tells you nothing about B, knowing "not A" shouldn't either. Proof of one of them (the others are the same idea):

$$\begin{aligned}P(A\cap B^c)&=P(A)-P(A\cap B)&&\text{(module 05)}\\&=P(A)-P(A)P(B)&&\text{(independence)}\\&=P(A)\,[1-P(B)]=P(A)\,P(B^c).\ \square\end{aligned}$$
DEF Three or more events: joint independence— ⊡ ✕

Three events $A,B,C$ are jointly (mutually) independent if

  1. any two of them are independent, and
  2. $P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(A)P(B)P(C)$.

$m$ events $A_1,\dots,A_m$ are jointly independent if every sub-collection $A_{i_1},\dots,A_{i_k}$ ($k\le m$) satisfies

$$P(A_{i_1}\cap\dots\cap A_{i_k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}P(A_{i_j}).$$

Neither condition alone is enough. The coins below have (1) without (2). For (2) without (1): on $\Omega=\{1,\dots,8\}$ with equally likely outcomes, take $A=\{1,2,3,4\}$, $B=\{1,2,3,5\}$, $C=\{1,6,7,8\}$. Then $P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(\{1\})=\tfrac18=\big(\tfrac12\big)^3$, but $P(A\cap B)=\tfrac38\neq\tfrac14$.

EX LECTURE 1 p.14 + LECTURE 2 p.1 · slides Conditional 14 — two fair coins— ⊡ ✕

Flip two fair coins: $\Omega=\{HH,HT,TH,TT\}$, each outcome $\tfrac14$.

  1. $A=\{\text{1st coin }H\}=\{HH,HT\}$, $P(A)=\tfrac24=\tfrac12$. $\ B=\{\text{both same face}\}=\{HH,TT\}$, $P(B)=\tfrac12$.
  2. $P(A\cap B)=P(\{HH,HT\}\cap\{HH,TT\})=P(\{HH\})=\tfrac14$ and $P(A)P(B)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14$. The same ⇒ $A\perp\!\!\!\perp B$. Surprising at first — "same face" sounds linked to the first coin — but knowing the first coin is H leaves "same face" at exactly 50%.
  3. Lecture 2 adds $C=\{\text{2nd coin }H\}=\{HH,TH\}$, $P(C)=\tfrac12$. Are $A,B,C$ jointly independent?
  4. Pairs: $B\cap C=\{HH\}$, $P=\tfrac14=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$ ⇒ $B\perp\!\!\!\perp C$. Similarly $A\cap C=\{HH\}$ ⇒ $A\perp\!\!\!\perp C$.
  5. Triple: $P(A\cap B\cap C)=P(\{HH\})=\tfrac14$, but $P(A)P(B)P(C)=\tfrac18$. Different.
  6. So $A,B,C$ are not jointly independent — only pairwise independent.
Insight
Any two of the events tell you nothing about the third on their own — but two together tell you everything: if the first coin is H and the coins match, the second coin must be H. Joint independence forbids exactly this kind of teamwork.
EX LECTURE 2 · notes p.1–2 · slides Conditional 15–17 — a machine with three independent components— ⊡ ✕

A machine has three independent components $K_1,K_2,K_3$ and works as long as at least one of them works (the slides: a rocket's redundant system — if $K_1$ fails it is bypassed and $K_2$ used, and so on). Failure probabilities: $P(K_1\text{ fails})=0.10$, $P(K_2)=0.20$, $P(K_3)=0.05$. Probability that the machine fails?

  1. Translate. The machine fails only if all three fail: $\{\text{fail}\}=\{K_1\text{ F}\}\cap\{K_2\text{ F}\}\cap\{K_3\text{ F}\}$. ("At least one works" ⇒ use the complement.)
  2. Independence turns the intersection into a product: $$P(\text{fail})=0.10\cdot0.20\cdot0.05=0.001=0.1\%.$$
  3. $P(\text{machine works})=1-0.001=0.999=99.9\%$ — very high reliability from three mediocre parts.
  4. Same answer from the tree (notes): the machine works if $K_1$ works (0.90); or $K_1$ fails and $K_2$ works ($0.1\cdot0.8=0.08$); or both fail and $K_3$ works ($0.1\cdot0.2\cdot0.95=0.019$): $$0.90+0.08+0.019=0.999\ \checkmark$$

Slides extra: exactly two components fail

Harder, because it can happen in three ways — $\{1,2\}$, $\{1,3\}$ or $\{2,3\}$ fail, the remaining one working. These are disjoint, so add them:

$$\begin{aligned}P(\text{two fail})&=0.1\cdot0.2\cdot0.95+0.1\cdot0.8\cdot0.05+0.9\cdot0.2\cdot0.05\\&=0.019+0.004+0.009=0.032\end{aligned}$$
?check yourselfAt least one six
A fair die is rolled 4 times (independent rolls). Probability of at least one six?
Answer
Complement + independence: $P(\text{no six})=(5/6)^4\approx0.482$, so $P(\text{at least one})\approx0.518$ — the same "fails only if all fail" logic as the machine.
?check yourselfIndependent or not?
One die. $A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{1,2,3,4\}$. Independent?
Answer
$P(A)=\tfrac12$, $P(B)=\tfrac23$, $P(A\cap B)=P(\{2,4\})=\tfrac13=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23$ ✓ — independent. (Try $B=\{1,2,3\}$: $P(A\cap B)=\tfrac16\neq\tfrac14$, dependent.)
