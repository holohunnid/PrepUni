Variance & moments

lecture 3, notes p.2–3 · slides: Distribution Functions 30–36
READ FIRST The question this module answers— ⊡ ✕

Two bets can have the same expected value and still feel completely different. Take $\$0$ for sure, or play module 12's $\pm\$2$ game: both have $\mathbb{E}(X)=0$. The first never surprises you. The second lands $\$2$ away from its mean half of the time. The expected value (module 16) says where a distribution sits. This module measures how spread out it is, meaning how far $X$ typically lands from its mean. In finance, that spread is risk.

Why squares? The obvious idea is to average the deviations $X-\mathbb{E}(X)$, but it fails. Positive and negative deviations cancel exactly, for every random variable (slide 36 proves $\mu_1=0$). Squaring makes every deviation count as positive, and makes large deviations count a lot. The price is the unit, which becomes squared dollars. The standard deviation takes the square root to get back to dollars.

The plan: definition (slide 30), the shortcut formula (31–32), what $a+bX$ and sums of independent variables do to the variance (33–34), and finally the coefficient of variation and higher moments (35–36).

LECTURE 3 What the lecturer stressed (notes p.2–3)— ⊡ ✕
Exam flag: know this proof
The notes mark the theorem $\mathbb V(X)=\mathbb E(X^2)-\mathbb E(X)^2$ with "know that proof!!" and the proof with "know for exam". Here it is in the form to reproduce, with the reason for every line.
  1. $\mathbb V(X)=\mathbb E\big[(X-\mathbb E(X))^2\big]$ — the definition.
  2. $\phantom{\mathbb V(X)}=\mathbb E\big[X^2+\mathbb E(X)^2-2\,\mathbb E(X)\,X\big]$ — expand the square $(a-b)^2=a^2+b^2-2ab$.
  3. $\phantom{\mathbb V(X)}=\mathbb E(X^2)+\mathbb E(X)^2-2\,\mathbb E(X)\,\mathbb E(X)$ — linearity. The key remark in the notes: $\mathbb E(X)$ is a number, not random, so $\mathbb E(X)^2$ is a constant (its expectation is itself) and $2\mathbb E(X)$ is a constant that comes out of the expectation.
  4. $\phantom{\mathbb V(X)}=\mathbb E(X^2)-\mathbb E(X)^2$. $\square$

Check on the lecture's example (heads in two flips): $\mathbb E(X^2)=0^2\cdot\tfrac14+1^2\cdot\tfrac12+2^2\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac32$, so $\mathbb V(X)=\tfrac32-1^2=\tfrac12$ — the same as the definition gives, $(0-1)^2\tfrac14+(1-1)^2\tfrac12+(2-1)^2\tfrac14=\tfrac12$.

Also from the notes: $\sigma=\sqrt{\mathbb V(X)}$ is in the same unit as $X$; the variance is in squared units.

Properties: use them, don't derive them
The notes: "math derivation not needed for exam" for $$\mathbb V(X+b)=\mathbb V(X),\quad\mathbb V(aX)=a^2\mathbb V(X),\quad\mathbb V(a+bX)=b^2\mathbb V(X).$$ The reason to remember: "variance is indifferent to a translation of the distribution — it shifts, but the shape is the same."

The notes' warning on $\mathbb V(X-Y)$

With $X,Y$ independent (the $U,V,W$ example below):

$$\mathbb V(X-Y)=\mathbb V\big(X+(-1)Y\big)=\mathbb V(X)+(-1)^2\,\mathbb V(Y)=3+4=7\ \neq\ \mathbb V(X)-\mathbb V(Y).$$

"We square the constant ($-1$), so it becomes $+$. Also, a variance can't be negative" — and $\mathbb V(X)-\mathbb V(Y)=3-4$ would be.

Coefficient of variation $CV(X)=\sigma/|\mu|$ is unit-free (unit of $X$ over unit of $X$): "useful to compare random variables in different orders of magnitude" — a stock at 10 CHF with $\sigma=2$ ($CV=0.2$) is relatively riskier than an index at 5000 with $\sigma=50$ ($CV=0.01$).

Last result of the lecture: the variance is the 2nd central moment $\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2]$, and the 1st central moment is always 0: $\mathbb E(X-\mu)=\mathbb E(X)-\mu=0$.

DEF Variance and standard deviation (slide 30)— ⊡ ✕

Let $X$ be a random variable with mean $\mu=\mathbb{E}(X)$. The variance and the standard deviation of $X$ are written $\mathbb{V}(X)$ and $\sigma_X$, and are defined as

$$\boxed{\;\begin{aligned}\mathbb{V}(X)&=\mathbb{E}\Big[(X-\mathbb{E}(X))^2\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[(X-\mu)^2\Big]=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(x_i)\,(x_i-\mu)^2\\ \sigma_X&=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(X)}\end{aligned}\;}$$

The variance is sometimes written $\sigma_X^2$.

Reading the formula in three steps

  1. Deviation $x_i-\mu$: how far value $i$ is from the centre, with a sign.
  2. Square it: $(x_i-\mu)^2\ge0$, so it no longer matters which side the value is on, and far values are penalised heavily.
  3. Average with the probabilities as weights. The last equality is module 16's theorem $\mathbb{E}(g(X))=\sum_i f(x_i)\,g(x_i)$, with $g(x)=(x-\mu)^2$.

Units

The unit of $\mathbb{V}(X)$ is the square of the unit of $X$. If $X$ is measured in meters, $\mathbb{V}(X)$ is in square meters. The standard deviation $\sigma_X$ has the same unit as $X$ (meters). That is why $\sigma$ is the number people report: "the game's standard deviation is $\$4.61$" means something, while "its variance is 21.21 squared dollars" does not.

Always $\mathbb{V}(X)\ge0$
It is an average of squares. It equals $0$ only when every value with positive probability equals $\mu$, i.e. for a constant random variable (module 15). A negative variance is always a computing error.
EXTRA EXAMPLE (module 12's (0,1) game) · variance as an average of squares

$X$ pays $1$, $2$ or $5$ dollars with probabilities $0.1$, $0.6$, $0.3$, and module 16 found $\mu=2.8$. Find $\mathbb{V}(X)$ and $\sigma_X$.

  1. Deviations from the mean: $1-2.8=-1.8$, $2-2.8=-0.8$, $5-2.8=2.2$.
  2. Squared deviations: $(-1.8)^2=3.24$, $(-0.8)^2=0.64$, $2.2^2=4.84$.
  3. Weight by the probabilities and add: $$\mathbb{V}(X)=0.1\cdot3.24+0.6\cdot0.64+0.3\cdot4.84=0.324+0.384+1.452=2.16\ \text{dollars}^2$$
  4. Back to dollars: $\sigma_X=\sqrt{2.16}\approx1.47$ dollars.
Insight
The value $5$ is less likely than $2$, yet it contributes most of the variance ($1.452$ of $2.16$), because squaring punishes large deviations. Variance is driven by the tails, which is exactly why risk managers watch it.
THEOREM The computational formula (slide 31)— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}\Big[(X-\mathbb{E}(X))^2\Big]=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2\;}$$

In words: the mean of the square minus the square of the mean.

Proof (slide 31), one justification per line

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb{V}(X)&=\mathbb{E}\Big[(X-\mathbb{E}(X))^2\Big]\\&=\mathbb{E}\Big[X^2-2\,\mathbb{E}(X)\cdot X+\mathbb{E}(X)^2\Big]\\&=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-2\,\mathbb{E}(X)\cdot\mathbb{E}(X)+\mathbb{E}(X)^2\\&=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2\end{aligned}$$
  1. Definition of the variance.
  2. Expand the square, $(u-v)^2=u^2-2uv+v^2$, with $u=X$ and $v=\mathbb{E}(X)$.
  3. Linearity (module 16). $\mathbb{E}(X)$ is just a number, so $\mathbb{E}\big(2\,\mathbb{E}(X)\cdot X\big)=2\,\mathbb{E}(X)\,\mathbb{E}(X)$, and the expectation of the constant $\mathbb{E}(X)^2$ is itself.
  4. Collect terms: $-2[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2+[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2=-[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2$. $\square$

On the slide, $\mathbb{E}(X)^2$ means $[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2$, the square of the mean. It does not mean $\mathbb{E}(X^2)$.

Why it is useful

You only need $\sum_i f(x_i)\,x_i^2$ and $\sum_i f(x_i)\,x_i$, and no deviations. Check it on the $(0,1)$ game: $\mathbb{E}(X^2)=0.1\cdot1+0.6\cdot4+0.3\cdot25=0.1+2.4+7.5=10$, so $\mathbb{V}(X)=10-2.8^2=10-7.84=2.16$ ✓, the same as the squares picture.

A free inequality
Since $\mathbb{V}(X)\ge0$, the formula gives $\mathbb{E}(X^2)\ge[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2$ for every random variable. The gap flagged in module 16's trap ($\frac32$ versus $1$) is the variance.
Trap
Three classic slips: writing $\mathbb{E}(X^2)-\mathbb{E}(X)$ (forgetting to square the mean), computing $[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2-\mathbb{E}(X^2)$ (the wrong order, which gives a negative "variance"), and using $\mathbb{E}(X^2)$ alone as the variance. Check the sign of your answer every time.
LECTURE 3 · notes p.2 · slide 32 · heads in two fair flips, two ways

A fair coin is flipped $n=2$ times. Let $X$ be the number of heads. Find $\mathbb{V}(X)$.

From modules 15–16: $f(0)=\frac14$, $f(1)=\frac12$, $f(2)=\frac14$ and $\mathbb{E}(X)=1$.

Way 1: the definition

  1. Substitute the mean: $\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}(X))^2\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-1)^2\big]$.
  2. One term per value: $$\mathbb{V}(X)=f(0)\cdot(0-1)^2+f(1)\cdot(1-1)^2+f(2)\cdot(2-1)^2$$
  3. Substitute and add: $$\mathbb{V}(X)=\tfrac14\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac14\cdot1=\tfrac12$$

Way 2: the computational formula

  1. $\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2=\big[f(0)\cdot0^2+f(1)\cdot1^2+f(2)\cdot2^2\big]-1^2$
  2. Substitute: $=\big[\tfrac14\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot4\big]-1$
  3. Add inside the bracket: $=\big[0+\tfrac12+1\big]-1=\tfrac32-1=\tfrac12$

Standard deviation (not on the slide): $\sigma_X=\sqrt{1/2}\approx0.707$ heads.

Insight
The two ways must agree, so on an exam compute one and use the other as a check. Way 1 shows the meaning (only the outer values $0$ and $2$ deviate, each by $1$). Way 2 is faster when the mean is an awkward decimal.
THEOREM Variance of $aX+b$ (slide 33)— ⊡ ✕

Let $X$ be a random variable, and let $a$ and $b$ be real numbers. Then

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{V}(X+b)=\mathbb{V}(X),\qquad\mathbb{V}(aX)=a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X),\qquad\mathbb{V}(aX+b)=a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)\;}$$

Proof (all three at once)

  1. Let $Z=aX+b$. By linearity (module 16), $\mathbb{E}(Z)=a\,\mathbb{E}(X)+b$.
  2. Subtract: $Z-\mathbb{E}(Z)=aX+b-a\,\mathbb{E}(X)-b=a\big(X-\mathbb{E}(X)\big)$. The $b$ cancels.
  3. Square: $\big(Z-\mathbb{E}(Z)\big)^2=a^2\big(X-\mathbb{E}(X)\big)^2$.
  4. Take expectations, pulling out the constant $a^2$: $\mathbb{V}(aX+b)=a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)$. Setting $a=1$ gives the first rule, and setting $b=0$ gives the second. $\square$

Why a shift does not change the spread

Adding $b$ moves every value and the mean by the same amount, so every distance to the mean stays the same. Paying a fixed entry fee changes your expected gain, not your risk.

Why the scale enters squared

Multiplying by $a$ multiplies every deviation by $a$, so it multiplies every squared deviation by $a^2$. The standard deviation goes back to a plain factor:

$$\sigma_{aX+b}=\sqrt{a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)}=|a|\,\sigma_X$$

with an absolute value, because a spread is never negative.

Trap
$\mathbb{V}(-X)=(-1)^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{V}(X)$, not $-\mathbb{V}(X)$: a mirror image is exactly as spread out. And $\mathbb{V}(2X)=4\,\mathbb{V}(X)$, not $2\,\mathbb{V}(X)$, while $\sigma_{2X}=2\sigma_X$.
TRY IT Shift and stretch a distribution— ⊡ ✕
Notation alert
The widget writes $Y=a+bX$, with $a$ the shift and $b$ the scale, matching $\mathbb{E}(a+bX)$ on slide 29. Slide 33 writes $aX+b$, with the letters swapped. The rule is the same either way: the scale factor gets squared and the shift disappears.

What to try.

  • "Y = X + 3". The bars slide 3 to the right. The bracket keeps its width, and $\mathbb{V}(Y)$ stays $\frac12$.
  • "Y = 2X". The values become $0,2,4$. The bracket doubles in width ($\sigma_Y=\sqrt2\approx1.41$), but the variance becomes $4\cdot\frac12=2$, four times as large.
  • "Y = −X". A mirror image around $0$: the mean becomes $-1$, and the variance is unchanged.
  • "Y = 2X − 2". The values are $-2,0,2$ with probabilities $\frac14,\frac12,\frac14$, exactly module 12's $\pm\$2$ game. So that game has $\mathbb{E}=0$ and $\mathbb{V}=2$.
  • Drag $b$ through $0$. At $b=0$, $Y=a$ is a constant, and the bracket collapses to $\mathbb{V}(Y)=0$.
THEOREM Sums of independent random variables (slide 33, second part)— ⊡ ✕

Moreover, assume $Y$ is a random variable that is independent of $X$. Then

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{V}(aX+bY)=a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)+b^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)\;}$$

Here "independent" means that knowing the value of one tells you nothing about the other. Formally, $P(X=x,\,Y=y)=P(X=x)\,P(Y=y)$ for all $x,y$, which is module 09's product rule applied to the events $\{X=x\}$ and $\{Y=y\}$.

Why it holds

  1. Write $\mu_X=\mathbb{E}(X)$ and $\mu_Y=\mathbb{E}(Y)$. By linearity, $aX+bY-\mathbb{E}(aX+bY)=a(X-\mu_X)+b(Y-\mu_Y)$.
  2. Square it: $$a^2(X-\mu_X)^2+b^2(Y-\mu_Y)^2+2ab\,(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)$$
  3. Take expectations: $$\mathbb{V}(aX+bY)=a^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)+b^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)+2ab\,\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]$$
  4. For independent variables, the expectation of a product is the product of the expectations. So the last term is $2ab\,\mathbb{E}(X-\mu_X)\,\mathbb{E}(Y-\mu_Y)=2ab\cdot0\cdot0=0$, since each factor is a first central moment and equals zero (slide 36). $\square$

If $X$ and $Y$ are dependent, the cross term stays. It is $2ab$ times the covariance of $X$ and $Y$, a later topic.

Finance: diversification
Put half your money in each of two independent assets with the same variance $\sigma^2$. Then $\mathbb{V}\big(\tfrac12X+\tfrac12Y\big)=\tfrac14\sigma^2+\tfrac14\sigma^2=\tfrac12\sigma^2$, so the standard deviation drops by a factor $\sqrt2$ at no cost in expected return. With $n$ independent assets the variance falls to $\sigma^2/n$. This is the mathematical core of diversification.
TRAP $\mathbb{V}(X-Y)=\mathbb{V}(X)+\mathbb{V}(Y)$, never minus— ⊡ ✕

Take $a=1$, $b=-1$ in the rule: $\mathbb{V}(X-Y)=1^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)+(-1)^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)=\mathbb{V}(X)+\mathbb{V}(Y)$. The minus sign gets squared away. Subtracting an independent random quantity adds uncertainty, just as adding one does.

And the rule needs independence

Take $Y=X$, which is as dependent as it gets. Then $\mathbb{V}(X-X)=\mathbb{V}(0)=0$, not $2\,\mathbb{V}(X)$. And $\mathbb{V}(X+X)=\mathbb{V}(2X)=4\,\mathbb{V}(X)$, not $2\,\mathbb{V}(X)$. The rule for means, $\mathbb{E}(aX+bY)=a\,\mathbb{E}(X)+b\,\mathbb{E}(Y)$, needs no independence. The rule for variances does.

LECTURE 3 · notes p.3 · slide 34 · expectations and variances of $U$, $V$, $W$

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two independent random variables with $\mathbb{E}(X)=0$, $\mathbb{E}(Y)=1$, $\mathbb{V}(X)=3$ and $\mathbb{V}(Y)=4$. Define $U=2X+3$, $V=2X+Y$ and $W=X-Y$. Find the expectation and the variance of $U$, $V$ and $W$.

Expectations (linearity, no independence needed)

  1. $\mathbb{E}(U)=\mathbb{E}(2X+3)=2\,\mathbb{E}(X)+3=2\cdot0+3=3$
  2. $\mathbb{E}(V)=\mathbb{E}(2X+Y)=2\,\mathbb{E}(X)+\mathbb{E}(Y)=2\cdot0+1=1$
  3. $\mathbb{E}(W)=\mathbb{E}(X-Y)=\mathbb{E}(X)-\mathbb{E}(Y)=0-1=-1$

Variances (squared coefficients; independence for $V$ and $W$)

  1. $\mathbb{V}(U)=\mathbb{V}(2X+3)=2^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)=4\cdot3=12$. The $+3$ disappears.
  2. $\mathbb{V}(V)=\mathbb{V}(2X+Y)=2^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)+1^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)=4\cdot3+1\cdot4=16$
  3. $\mathbb{V}(W)=\mathbb{V}(X-Y)=1^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)+(-1)^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)=1\cdot3+1\cdot4=7$

Standard deviations (not on the slide): $\sigma_U=\sqrt{12}\approx3.46$, $\sigma_V=\sqrt{16}=4$, $\sigma_W=\sqrt7\approx2.65$.

On the slide, the letter V names both the random variable $V=2X+Y$ and the variance operator $\mathbb{V}$ (blackboard bold). So $\mathbb{V}(V)=16$ reads "the variance of $V$ is 16". The slide writes $1^2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)$ in the last line, because the coefficient $-1$ squares to $1$.

Insight
Means follow linearity blindly, minus signs included. Variances square the coefficients, so minus signs vanish, shifts vanish, and every independent piece adds its variance. That is why $W=X-Y$ has variance $7$, more than either $\mathbb{V}(X)=3$ or $\mathbb{V}(Y)=4$.
DEF Coefficient of variation (slide 35)— ⊡ ✕

The coefficient of variation of the random variable $X$ is

$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{CV}(X)=\frac{\sigma_X}{|\mathbb{E}(X)|}\;}$$

It is a normalised measure of dispersion: it measures the spread relative to the size of the mean. It is dimensionless, because $\sigma_X$ and $\mathbb{E}(X)$ have the same unit, which cancels. Measure a gain in cents instead of dollars and both $\sigma_X$ and $|\mathbb{E}(X)|$ are multiplied by 100, so the CV does not change.

Finance: risk per unit of expected return
If $X$ is the payoff of an investment, $\mathrm{CV}(X)$ is the amount of risk (standard deviation) you carry for each unit of expected payoff. It lets you compare investments of very different sizes. Its reciprocal, $|\mathbb{E}(X)|/\sigma_X$, is expected return per unit of risk, the idea behind the Sharpe ratio.

The three CVs, computed:

  1. Heads in two flips: $\mathrm{CV}=\sqrt{1/2}\,/\,1\approx0.71$.
  2. The $(0,1)$ game: $\mathrm{CV}=1.47/2.8\approx0.52$.
  3. Slide 23's game ($P(H)=0.1$): $\mathbb{E}(X^2)=0.01\cdot1+0.18\cdot100+0.81\cdot4=0.01+18+3.24=21.25$, so $\mathbb{V}(X)=21.25-0.19^2=21.25-0.0361=21.2139$, $\sigma_X\approx4.61$ and $\mathrm{CV}\approx4.61/0.19\approx24.2$.
Trap
The CV is undefined when $\mathbb{E}(X)=0$ (the $\pm\$2$ game) and explodes when $\mathbb{E}(X)$ is close to 0. Use it for quantities whose mean is clearly away from zero, typically positive ones such as prices, lifetimes and sizes. The absolute value in the denominator keeps it positive when the mean is negative.
DEF Moments (slide 35)— ⊡ ✕

The $k$-th moment and the $k$-th central moment of the random variable $X$ are

$$\boxed{\;\mu_k'=\mathbb{E}(X^k),\qquad\mu_k=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}(X))^k\big]\;}$$

Raw moments measure powers of the values themselves. Central moments measure powers of the deviations from the mean. For a discrete random variable, module 16's theorem with $g(x)=x^k$ or $g(x)=(x-\mathbb{E}(X))^k$ gives

$$\mu_k'=\mathbb{E}(X^k)=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}f(x_i)\,x_i^k\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu_k=\mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mathbb{E}(X))^k\big]=\sum_{i=1}^{+\infty}f(x_i)\,[x_i-\mathbb{E}(X)]^k$$

A dictionary of what you already know

$\mu_1'=\mathbb{E}(X)$the mean
$\mu_1=0$always (slide 36, below)
$\mu_2=\mathbb{V}(X)$the variance
$\mu_2=\mu_2'-(\mu_1')^2$the computational formula, in moment language
$\mu_3$sign shows the skew: $>0$ means a long right tail
$\mu_4$how much weight sits in the tails

Heads in two flips, first four moments

$k$1234
$\mu_k'=\frac14\cdot0^k+\frac12\cdot1^k+\frac14\cdot2^k$$1$$\frac32$$\frac52$$\frac92$
$\mu_k=\frac14(-1)^k+\frac12\cdot0^k+\frac14\cdot1^k$$0$$\frac12$$0$$\frac12$

For example, $\mu_3'=\frac14\cdot0+\frac12\cdot1+\frac14\cdot8=\frac12+2=\frac52$. The odd central moments vanish because the pmf is symmetric about its mean: every deviation $-d$ is matched by $+d$ with the same probability.

A skewed example. For the $(0,1)$ game (deviations $-1.8$, $-0.8$, $2.2$):

$$\mu_3=0.1(-1.8)^3+0.6(-0.8)^3+0.3(2.2)^3=-0.5832-0.3072+3.1944=2.304>0$$

This is right-skewed: the long tail points toward the payoff of $5$.

Finance (beyond the slides)
Dividing by powers of $\sigma$ gives unit-free versions. Skewness is $\mu_3/\sigma^3$ (about $2.304/1.47^3\approx0.73$ for the $(0,1)$ game), and kurtosis is $\mu_4/\sigma^4$. Asset returns typically show negative skewness and high kurtosis ("fat tails"), which is why the first two moments alone understate crash risk.
LECTURE 3 · notes p.3 · slide 36 · the first central moment is zero

A fair coin is flipped $n=2$ times. Let $X$ be the number of heads. Prove that the first central moment of $X$ is zero.

  1. Ingredients (modules 15–16): $f(0)=\frac14$, $f(1)=\frac12$, $f(2)=\frac14$, and $\mathbb{E}(X)=1$.
  2. Apply the definition with $k=1$: $$\mu_1=\mathbb{E}[X-\mathbb{E}(X)]=f(0)\cdot(0-1)+f(1)\cdot(1-1)+f(2)\cdot(2-1)$$
  3. Substitute and add: $$\mu_1=\tfrac14\cdot(-1)+\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac14\cdot1=-\tfrac14+0+\tfrac14=0$$
  4. The general statement. For any random variable with a finite mean, linearity with $a=-\mathbb{E}(X)$ and $b=1$ gives $$\mu_1=\mathbb{E}[X-\mathbb{E}(X)]=\mathbb{E}(X)-\mathbb{E}(X)=0$$
Insight
This is the balance point of module 16 in algebraic form. The probability-weighted deviations on the left of the mean exactly cancel those on the right (in the $(0,1)$ game, $0.66$ on each side). It is also why variance has to square the deviations: averaging them unsquared always gives $0$, whatever the spread.

In R (slide 36): sum(x-mean(x)) returns 0. This is the sample version: the deviations of any data set from its own sample mean sum to zero. With decimal data R may print a tiny number such as 1e-16 instead. That is floating-point rounding, and it should be read as zero.

TIP R corner and summary— ⊡ ✕
R corner (standard R, not on the slides)

A pmf is two vectors, and both variance formulas are one line each:

x <- 0:2; f <- c(1/4, 1/2, 1/4)
mu <- sum(x * f) stores the mean, 1 (an assignment prints nothing)
sum((x - mu)^2 * f) gives [1] 0.5 (the definition)
sum(x^2 * f) - mu^2 gives [1] 0.5 (the shortcut)
sqrt(sum((x - mu)^2 * f)) gives [1] 0.7071068

R trap: var(x) gives [1] 1, not $\mathbb{V}(X)=0.5$. var treats its input as a sample of equally weighted observations and divides by $n-1$: $\big((0-1)^2+(1-1)^2+(2-1)^2\big)/2=1$. It knows nothing about f. The same applies to sd(x), which gives 1 here.

To estimate $\mathbb{V}(X)$ from simulated data, use a large sample: set.seed(1); s <- rbinom(100000, 2, 0.5); var(s) prints a number close to 0.5.

$\mathbb{V}(X)=0.5$. Find $\mathbb{V}(3-2X)$ and the standard deviation of $3-2X$.
Answer

The shift $3$ disappears and the scale $-2$ is squared: $\mathbb{V}(3-2X)=(-2)^2\cdot0.5=2$. The standard deviation is $|{-2}|\cdot\sqrt{0.5}=\sqrt2\approx1.41$.

?check yourselfSlide 34, one step further
With the data of slide 34 ($X,Y$ independent, $\mathbb{V}(X)=3$, $\mathbb{V}(Y)=4$), find $\mathbb{V}(X-2Y)$.
Answer
$$\mathbb{V}(X-2Y)=1^2\cdot3+(-2)^2\cdot4=3+16=19$$

Not $3-16$, and not $3-8$: the coefficient $-2$ is squared.
