READ FIRST Same recipe, different pivot— ⊡ ✕

Module 37 assumed $\sigma$ known, which is rarely true. Every interval in this module follows the same three steps: (1) pick the pivotal quantity "estimator minus parameter, over its (estimated) standard deviation"; (2) read its quantile; (3) invert. Only the pivot and its distribution change:

LECTURE 9 $\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu)$, $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma^2$ unknown (pp.7–9)— ⊡ ✕

$Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}$ can't be used anymore, since its denominator can't be computed. ⇒ Replace $\sigma$ with $S$. The PQ is now

$$T=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\sim t_{n-1},$$

Student-t with $n-1$ degrees of freedom (module 30). Same steps as before, with $t_1=-t_{\alpha/2}$ and $t_2=t_{\alpha/2}=$ qt(1-α/2, n-1):

$$\begin{aligned}&P\Big(-t_{\alpha/2}<\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}<+t_{\alpha/2}\Big)=1-\alpha\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &P\Big(-t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}<\bar X-\mu<t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}\Big)=1-\alpha\\ \Leftrightarrow\ &P\Big(\underbrace{\bar X-t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}}_{L_1}<\mu<\underbrace{\bar X+t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}}_{L_2}\Big)=1-\alpha\end{aligned}$$ $$\boxed{\;\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu)=\Big[\bar X\mp t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}\Big],\quad X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2),\ \sigma^2\text{ unknown}\;}$$

$t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}$ is the margin of error.

Width $A=L_2-L_1=2t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}$.

  • if $\sigma\uparrow$ ⇒ wider CI in expectation (not for sure, as it was with known $\sigma$);
  • if $n\uparrow$ ⇒ smaller CI in expectation;
  • if $(1-\alpha)\uparrow$ ⇒ wider CI (not only in expectation: $t_{\alpha/2}$ is a fixed number).

Compared with known $\sigma^2$. The problem is more difficult since we have fewer info. ⇒ We expect the CI to be wider than before, because of the t-Student quantile: $t_{\alpha/2}>z_{\alpha/2}$, since the t has heavier tails. We have a higher margin of error (in expectation).

A second difference. $A=2t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}$ now depends on $\underline X$, so it is random (before, $2z_{\alpha/2}\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}$ was not). ⇒ We can't be sure anymore that a certain $n$ guarantees a margin of error below a given threshold: in $n>\big(2t_{\alpha/2}\frac S{10}\big)^2$ the right side is unknown in advance.

LECTURE 9 · pp.9–10 · deck · effect of mergers on revenues

$X$ = difference post- minus pre-merger revenue growth. $n=17$, $\bar x=0.105$, $s=0.44$. Find $\text{CI}_{95\%}(\mu)$ assuming $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$.

  1. $\sigma$ unknown, normal population ⇒ t-interval with $n-1=16$ df.
  2. $1-\alpha=0.95$, $\alpha/2=0.025$: $t_{0.025,16}=$ qt(1-0.025, 16) $=2.12$.
  3. $\Big[0.105\mp2.12\cdot\frac{0.44}{\sqrt{17}}\Big]=[0.105\mp0.226]=\mathbf{[-0.121,\ +0.331]}$.
  4. Conclusion. We see no evidence of better or worse revenues, since both positive and negative values belong to the CI.

LECTURE 9 $\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu)$, $X\sim$ unknown, $n$ large (pp.10–11)— ⊡ ✕

"Large" means $n\ge30$ if $\sigma$ is known, $n\ge50$ if $\sigma$ is unknown. The PQ is

$$Z=\frac{\bar X-\mu}{S/\sqrt n}\approx N(0,1),$$

approximately true thanks to the central limit theorem (module 30). Same steps:

$$P\Big(-z_{\alpha/2}<Z<+z_{\alpha/2}\Big)\approx1-\alpha\ \Rightarrow\ P\Big(\bar X-z_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}<\mu<\bar X+z_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}\Big)\approx1-\alpha$$ $$\boxed{\;\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(\mu)\approx\Big[\bar X\mp z_{\alpha/2}\frac S{\sqrt n}\Big],\quad X\sim\text{unknown},\ n\text{ large}\;}$$

with $z_{\alpha/2}=$ qnorm(1-α/2). If $\sigma$ is known, use it instead of $S$.

LECTURE 9 · pp.10–11 · deck · "job security" questionnaire

$X$ = answer on a scale $\text{Supp}(X)=\{1,2,\dots,5\}$ (1 = not important, 5 = very important). $n=172$, $\bar x=4.38$, $s=0.7$. Find $\text{CI}_{99\%}(\mu)$.

  1. $X$ takes 5 integer values, so it is certainly not normal. But $n=172$ is large: CLT.
  2. $1-\alpha=0.99$, $\alpha=0.01$, $\alpha/2=0.005$: $z_{0.005}=$ qnorm(0.995) $=2.575$.
  3. $\Big[4.38\mp2.575\cdot\frac{0.7}{\sqrt{172}}\Big]=[4.38\mp0.137]=\mathbf{[4.24,\ 4.52]}$.
DECK Proportions: Bernoulli population, large n— ⊡ ✕

$X\sim\text{Bern}(p)$: $\bar X=\hat p$ is the sample proportion, with $\mathbb V(\bar X)=\frac{p(1-p)}n$. Estimate the unknown $p$ in the standard error by $\bar X$:

$$P\Bigg(-z_{\alpha/2}<\frac{\bar X-p}{\sqrt{\bar X(1-\bar X)/n}}<z_{\alpha/2}\Bigg)\approx1-\alpha$$ $$\boxed{\;\text{CI}_{1-\alpha}(p)\approx\Big[\hat p\mp z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n}\Big]\;}$$

It is the large-$n$ interval with $S$ replaced by the Bernoulli standard deviation $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)}$.

  1. Gifts from vendors: 83 of 344 buyers refuse gifts, so $\hat p=0.241$. 90%: $z_{0.05}=1.645$, $\sqrt{\frac{0.241\cdot0.759}{344}}=0.0231$: $\mathbf{[0.203,\ 0.279]}$.
  2. University reform: 20 of 100 students satisfied, $\hat p=0.2$, $\sqrt{0.2\cdot0.8/100}=0.04$. 95%: $[0.2\mp1.96\cdot0.04]=\mathbf{[0.1216,\ 0.2784]}$.
  3. Insurance product (module 35): $\hat p=0.10$, $n=100$, standard error $0.03$. 99%: $[0.1\mp2.576\cdot0.03]=\mathbf{[0.0227,\ 0.1773]}$.
DECK Two populations: CI for a difference (beyond lecture 9)— ⊡ ✕

Independent samples of sizes $n_1,n_2$, with $\mathbb V(\bar X-\bar Y)=\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}$ (module 30). Same recipe, estimator $\bar X-\bar Y$:

normal, $\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2$ known$\bar X-\bar Y\mp z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}$
normal, $\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2$ unknown$\bar X-\bar Y\mp t^{(n_1+n_2-2)}_{\alpha/2}\,S_c\sqrt{\frac1{n_1}+\frac1{n_2}}$, pooled $S_c^2=\frac{(n_1-1)S_1^2+(n_2-1)S_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2}$
normal, unequal unknown$\bar X-\bar Y\mp t^{(\nu)}_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{S_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{S_2^2}{n_2}}$, $\nu$ from Welch–Satterthwaite
any, large $n_1,n_2$$\bar X-\bar Y\mp z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{S_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{S_2^2}{n_2}}$
proportions, large$\hat p_1-\hat p_2\mp z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat p_2(1-\hat p_2)}{n_2}}$
  1. Bread prices, Milano vs Modena. $n_1=25$, $\bar x=3.5$, $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=2.7$; $n_2=22$, $\bar y=3.8$, $\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2=1.35$. Assume normal, equal variances. $S_c^2=\frac{2.7+1.35}{45}=0.09$, $S_c=0.3$, $t^{(45)}_{0.025}=2.014$: $$-0.3\mp2.014\cdot0.3\sqrt{\tfrac1{25}+\tfrac1{22}}=\mathbf{[-0.477,\ -0.123]}$$ Entirely negative: with 95% confidence bread costs more on average in Modena. (Variability: coefficients of variation $s/\bar x$ are 0.096 in Milano and 0.067 in Modena.)
  2. Lega voters, Varese vs Rome. $\hat p_1=0.4$ ($n_1=30$), $\hat p_2=0.05$ ($n_2=100$). $0.35\mp1.96\sqrt{\frac{0.4\cdot0.6}{30}+\frac{0.05\cdot0.95}{100}}=\mathbf{[0.17,\ 0.53]}$. Entirely positive, so the proportion is higher in Varese.
TRAP · a difference CI that contains 0
The decision rule in all these examples: if the interval contains 0 (mergers), the data show no evidence of a difference. If it lies entirely on one side, the sign is supported at that confidence level. This is the bridge to hypothesis testing.

?check yourselfCompute it
$n=10$ normal observations, $\bar x=20$, $s=3$. Give the 95% CI for $\mu$ (use $t_{0.025,9}=2.262$). Then explain why using $z=1.96$ instead would be wrong.
Answer
$20\mp2.262\cdot\frac3{\sqrt{10}}=20\mp2.146=[17.85,\ 22.15]$. With 1.96 the interval would be $[18.14,\ 21.86]$: too narrow. It ignores the extra uncertainty from estimating $\sigma$, so its real coverage would be below 95%.
