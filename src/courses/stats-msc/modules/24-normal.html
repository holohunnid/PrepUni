The normal distribution

slides: Special Continuous RVs 13–19, 29–30 · ahead of the lecture notes
READ FIRST One bell, one table, every normal question— ⊡ ✕

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the one you will use most: measurement errors, heights, test scores, and — as a first approximation — asset returns. Sums and averages of many small independent effects tend to be normal, which is why it appears everywhere in inference.

It comes with a practical problem: its cdf has no formula. You cannot integrate the bell with pen and paper. The whole module is about how we get around that with three tools:

1. one function, $\Phi$the cdf of the standard normal $Z\sim N(0,1)$, read from a table or from R
2. standardisationevery normal is $Y=\mu+\sigma Z$, so $F_Y(x)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)$ — one table serves all normals
3. symmetry$\Phi(-x)=1-\Phi(x)$ — the table only needs $x\ge0$

You already have every ingredient: the cdf method for $Y=\mu+\sigma Z$ is module 14, $f=F'$ is module 21, and $\mathbb{E}(a+bX)=a+b\,\mathbb{E}(X)$, $\mathbb{V}(a+bX)=b^2\,\mathbb{V}(X)$ are module 22.

The recipe you will apply in every exercise
(i) Read off $\mu$ and $\sigma$ — careful, $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ gives the variance. (ii) Convert each boundary to a z-score $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$. (iii) Write the probability with $\Phi$, using $1-\Phi$ for "greater than" and symmetry for negative $z$. (iv) Look up the numbers.
DEF The standard normal $Z$ (slide 13)— ⊡ ✕

The standard normal random variable is usually denoted by $Z$. Its density function is

$$\boxed{\,f_Z(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}\qquad-\infty<x<\infty\,}$$

$f_Z(x)$ is defined and positive for all $-\infty<x<\infty$, hence $Z$ takes on all real values: it has no minimum or maximum, and its range is the entire real line.

The graph decreases on both sides of its top point, and very fast (slide 13):

$x$012345
$f_Z(x)$0.3990.2420.0540.00440.00013$\approx10^{-6}$

For larger $x$ the factor $e^{-x^2/2}$ approaches zero dramatically fast, so for all practical purposes $f_Z(x)=0$ for $|x|>5$.

Where the constants come from
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ is there only to make the area 1: the Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}dx=\sqrt{2\pi}$ (a classic result, proved with polar coordinates). And differentiating twice, $f_Z'(x)=-x\,f_Z(x)$ and $f_Z''(x)=(x^2-1)f_Z(x)$, which changes sign at $x=\pm1$: the inflection points in the figure.
DEF $\Phi$, the distribution function of $Z$ (slide 13)— ⊡ ✕

The cdf of $Z$ has its own letter:

$$\boxed{\,\Phi(x)=P(Z\le x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-\frac{u^2}{2}}\,du\,}$$

Unfortunately, there is no formula for $\Phi(x)$ in terms of elementary functions — no combination of powers, exponentials, logs or trig functions has $e^{-u^2/2}$ as its derivative. The integral above is the best one can write. So $\Phi$ is computed numerically, once, and tabulated.

RULE Symmetry: $\Phi(-x)=1-\Phi(x)$ — used in every example below— ⊡ ✕

Picture. The bell is the mirror image of itself in the y-axis, so the area left of $-x$ equals the area right of $+x$. The area right of $x$ is $1-\Phi(x)$.

Proof (not on the slides). Substitute $u=-v$ (so $du=-dv$, and the limits $-\infty,\,-x$ become $+\infty,\,x$), then use $f_Z(-v)=f_Z(v)$:

$$\Phi(-x)=\int_{-\infty}^{-x}f_Z(u)\,du=\int_{x}^{\infty}f_Z(-v)\,dv=\int_x^\infty f_Z(v)\,dv=1-\Phi(x).$$

Consequences you will use:

  • $\Phi(0)=\tfrac12$ (take $x=0$: $\Phi(0)=1-\Phi(0)$);
  • $P(Z>x)=1-\Phi(x)=\Phi(-x)$;
  • $P(|Z|\le a)=\Phi(a)-\Phi(-a)=2\Phi(a)-1$, and $P(|Z|>a)=2\big(1-\Phi(a)\big)$.
DEF The general normal $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ (slide 15)— ⊡ ✕

It has two parameters, $\mu$ and $\sigma>0$, and is defined in terms of the standard normal $Z$ by

$$\boxed{\,Y=\mu+\sigma Z\,}$$

In words: $Y$ is obtained by rescaling (multiplying by $\sigma$) and shifting (adding $\mu$) the standard normal. We write $Y\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$; in particular $Z\sim N(0,1)$.

The cdf in one line (the cdf method of module 14)

$$F_Y(x)=P(\mu+\sigma Z\le x)=P\!\left(Z\le\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\boxed{\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\,}\qquad-\infty<x<\infty$$

(subtract $\mu$, divide by $\sigma$ — dividing by a positive number keeps the direction of $\le$).

The density, by differentiating (module 21)

Chain rule, with inner derivative $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}=\frac1\sigma$:

$$f_Y(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\,f_Z\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}=\boxed{\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\,}$$

What the parameters are

By module 22, $\mathbb{E}(Y)=\mu+\sigma\,\mathbb{E}(Z)$ and $\mathbb{V}(Y)=\sigma^2\,\mathbb{V}(Z)$. With $\mathbb{E}(Z)=0$, $\mathbb{V}(Z)=1$ (proved below): $\mathbb{E}(Y)=\mu$, $\mathbb{V}(Y)=\sigma^2$. The parameters are the mean and the variance.

TRAP · the second parameter is the variance
$N(5,4)$ means $\sigma^2=4$, so $\sigma=2$. The standardisation divides by $\sigma=2$, never by 4.

EXAMPLE (slide 16) · company earnings $Y\sim N(5,4)$

Let a company's earnings be $Y\sim N(5,4)$. Compute $P(Y<7)$ and $P(3<Y<6)$.

  1. Read the parameters. $\mu=5$ and $\sigma^2=4$, hence $\sigma=2$.
  2. First probability. Use $F_Y(x)=\Phi\big((x-\mu)/\sigma\big)$: $$P(Y<7)=F_Y(7)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{7-5}{2}\right)=\Phi(1)=\mathbf{0.8413}$$
  3. Second probability as a difference of cdf values (module 13): $$\begin{aligned}P(3<Y<6)&=F_Y(6)-F_Y(3)\\&=\Phi\!\left(\frac{6-5}{2}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{3-5}{2}\right)\\&=\Phi(0.5)-\Phi(-1)\end{aligned}$$
  4. Symmetry for the negative argument: $\Phi(-1)=1-\Phi(1)=1-0.8413$. So $$P(3<Y<6)=0.6915-1+0.8413=\mathbf{0.5328}$$
TRAP · pnorm wants the standard deviation
R's pnorm(q, mean, sd) takes the sd. Correct: pnorm(7, 5, 2) → 0.8413447 and pnorm(6, 5, 2) - pnorm(3, 5, 2) → 0.5328072. Wrong: pnorm(7, 5, 4) → 0.6914625 and pnorm(6, 5, 4) - pnorm(3, 5, 4) → 0.2901688 — plausible-looking numbers for a different distribution, $N(5,16)$. This exact slip is a favourite multiple-choice distractor.
Insight
After standardising, an earnings figure of 7 is "one standard deviation above the mean", and $\Phi(1)=0.8413$ is the same for every normal. That is why a single table was enough for a century.
EXAMPLE (slide 17) · the paternity suit

An expert witness in a paternity suit testifies that the length (in days) of a pregnancy is approximately normal with $\mu=270$ and $\sigma^2=100$. The defendant can prove he was out of the country during a period that began 290 days before the birth and ended 240 days before the birth. If the defendant was in fact the father, what is the probability that the mother could have had the very long or very short pregnancy indicated by the testimony?

  1. Name the variable. Let $X$ = length of the pregnancy, assuming the defendant is the father. $\mu=270$, $\sigma^2=100$, so $\sigma=10$.
  2. Translate the alibi. Conception happened $X$ days before the birth. He was away from day 290 to day 240 before the birth, so if he is the father, conception was either more than 290 days before birth or less than 240 days before: $X>290$ or $X<240$. The events are disjoint, so the probabilities add.
  3. Standardise both boundaries: $\frac{290-270}{10}=2$ and $\frac{240-270}{10}=-3$. $$P(X>290)+P(X<240)=P\!\left(\frac{X-270}{10}>2\right)+P\!\left(\frac{X-270}{10}<-3\right)$$
  4. Write with $\Phi$: a "greater than" is $1-\Phi$, and by symmetry $\Phi(-3)=1-\Phi(3)$: $$=1-\Phi(2)+1-\Phi(3)$$
  5. Numbers: $1-\Phi(2)=0.02275$ and $1-\Phi(3)=0.00135$, so the total is $$\approx\mathbf{0.0241}$$

R: 1 - pnorm(290, 270, 10) + pnorm(240, 270, 10) → 0.02410003.

Insight · and a famous fallacy
0.0241 is $P(\text{such an extreme pregnancy}\mid\text{he is the father})$ — about 1 in 41. It is not the probability that he is the father. Turning one into the other needs Bayes' rule (module 10) and a prior; confusing them is the "prosecutor's fallacy". What the number does say: if he is the father, something unusual happened.
EXAMPLE (slide 18) · a binary message over a noisy wire

A binary message, 0 or 1, is sent by wire from A to B. To reduce errors, the value 2 is sent when the message is 1 and $-2$ when it is 0. If $x=\pm2$ is sent, the value received at B is $R=x+N$, where $N$ is the channel noise. The receiver decodes: if $R\ge0.5$, conclude 1; if $R<0.5$, conclude 0. What are the error probabilities when $N$ is a unit normal random variable, $N\sim N(0,1)$?

  1. Two kinds of error. A 1 decoded as 0, or a 0 decoded as 1.
  2. Error when 1 is sent. Then $R=2+N$, and it is misread when $2+N<0.5$, i.e. $N<-1.5$: $$P(\text{error}\mid\text{message is 1})=P(N<-1.5)=\Phi(-1.5)=1-\Phi(1.5)\approx1-0.9332=\mathbf{0.0668}$$
  3. Error when 0 is sent. Then $R=-2+N$, misread when $-2+N\ge0.5$, i.e. $N\ge2.5$: $$P(\text{error}\mid\text{message is 0})=P(N\ge2.5)=1-\Phi(2.5)\approx1-0.9938=\mathbf{0.0062}$$
  4. R check: pnorm(-1.5) → 0.0668072; 1 - pnorm(2.5) → 0.006209665. Equivalently in the original units: pnorm(0.5, mean = 2, sd = 1) and 1 - pnorm(0.5, mean = -2, sd = 1).
Insight (numbers not on the slides)
The errors are unequal because the threshold 0.5 is not halfway between $-2$ and $2$: it sits closer to the "1" bell, so 1s are misread more often. With the symmetric threshold 0 both errors would be $1-\Phi(2)=0.0228$. If 0s and 1s are sent equally often, the overall error rate is $\frac12(0.0668+0.0062)=0.0365$ with threshold 0.5, against $0.0228$ with threshold 0.
THEOREM $\mathbb{E}(Z)=0$ by symmetry; $\mathbb{E}=\mu$, $\mathbb{V}=\sigma^2$ (slide 19)— ⊡ ✕

Let $Z\sim N(0,1)$. Then

$$\mathbb{E}(Z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,f_Z(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}\,dx.$$
  1. Even times odd. The density is even, $f_Z(x)=f_Z(-x)$, so the product $x\,f_Z(x)$ is odd. (Slide 19 footnote: $f$ is odd if $-f(x)=f(-x)$ for all $x$ in its domain.)
  2. Symmetry. An odd integrand has the area on the left equal and opposite to the area on the right, so the integral is zero — which it is: $\mathbf{\mathbb{E}(Z)=0}$.
  3. Direct check. $-e^{-x^2/2}$ is an antiderivative of $x\,e^{-x^2/2}$, so $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xe^{-x^2/2}dx=\big[-e^{-x^2/2}\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=0-0=0$ ✓.

$\mathbb{V}(Z)=1$ (not on the slides)

Since $\mathbb{E}(Z)=0$, $\mathbb{V}(Z)=\mathbb{E}(Z^2)=\int x\cdot x f_Z(x)\,dx$. Parts with $u=x$ and $dv=x f_Z(x)\,dx$, $v=-f_Z(x)$ (because $f_Z'(x)=-xf_Z(x)$):

$$\mathbb{E}(Z^2)=\big[-x\,f_Z(x)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_Z(x)\,dx=0+1=1.$$

Generally, for $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, i.e. $X=\mu+\sigma Z$, module 22 gives

$$\boxed{\,\mathbb{E}(X)=\mu\,}\qquad\boxed{\,\mathbb{V}(X)=\sigma^2\,}$$
Symmetry needs a finite integral
"Symmetric, so the mean is the centre" works only if $\int|x|f(x)dx<\infty$. Here $\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2/2}dx=1$ is finite, so all is well. The Student-t with $\nu=1$ (module 25) is symmetric too, yet has no mean.
RULE Standardisation of a random variable (slides 29–30)— ⊡ ✕

The basic idea (slide 29). Modify a random variable $X$ so that the new random variable

$$Y\equiv\frac{X-\mathbb{E}(X)}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(X)}}$$

i.e. subtract the mean and rescale by dividing by the standard deviation. (Some random variables have no finite mean or variance; we will not deal with such cases.)

For a normal (slide 30). Let $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $Y\equiv\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}$. Then, by linearity (module 22):

$$\mathbb{E}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\!\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X-\mu)}{\sigma}=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X)-\mu}{\sigma}=\frac{\mu-\mu}{\sigma}=0,$$ $$\mathbb{V}(Y)=\mathbb{V}\!\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac{\mathbb{V}(X-\mu)}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\mathbb{V}(X)}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}=1.$$

Hence $Y\sim N(0,1)$ is a standard normal. (Mean 0 and variance 1 come from the algebra; normality comes from the fact that $Y$ just undoes $X=\mu+\sigma Z$, so $Y=Z$.)

Reading a z-score. $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$ counts how many standard deviations $x$ lies from the mean. Earnings of 7 under $N(5,4)$: $z=1$. A 290-day pregnancy: $z=2$.

TRAP · standardised is not the same as normal
Standardising any variable gives mean 0 and variance 1, but it keeps the shape. If $X\sim$ exponential$(1)$ (mean 1, sd 1, module 23), then $Y=X-1$ has $\mathbb{E}(Y)=0$ and $\mathbb{V}(Y)=1$, yet it lives on $(-1,\infty)$ and is skewed — nothing like $N(0,1)$. You may use $\Phi$ on a z-score only when $X$ itself is normal.
EXTRA The 68–95–99.7 rule— ⊡ ✕

Not on the slides, but worth knowing by heart. For any normal, the probability of landing within $k$ standard deviations of the mean is $P(|Z|\le k)=2\Phi(k)-1$:

withinprobabilityoutside
$\mu\pm\sigma$$2(0.8413)-1=0.6827$about 1 in 3
$\mu\pm1.96\sigma$$2(0.975)-1=0.95$1 in 20

Finance reading
If a stock's daily return were normal with $\sigma=1\%$, a move of more than 3% either way would happen on about 1 day in 370 — roughly once every year and a half of trading. Real markets produce such days far more often: returns have fat tails. The Student-t of module 25 is the standard fix.

TRAP · the direction of the inequality
For a continuous variable $P(Y<7)=P(Y\le7)$, so "<" versus "≤" never matters. What matters is the side: "more than" needs $1-\Phi$, and forgetting it turns $0.0228$ into $0.9772$.
