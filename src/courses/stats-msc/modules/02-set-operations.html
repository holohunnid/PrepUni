Set operations

lecture 1, notes p.2–5 · slides: Axioms 3–4, 7–9
READ FIRST Words become symbols— ⊡ ✕

Because events are sets (module 01), we can combine them with set operations. Each operation is simply a precise version of an everyday word:

you say…you write…which outcomes are in it?
"A or B"$A\cup B$those in $A$, in $B$, or in both (inclusive "or")
"A and B"$A\cap B$ (slides also write $AB$)those in both at once
"not A"$A^c$ or $\bar A$those outside $A$
"B but not A"$B-A$those in $B$ and outside $A$

Almost every probability exercise starts by translating a sentence into one of these expressions. Get the translation right and the rest is arithmetic.

DEF The four operations, as the lecture draws them— ⊡ ✕
$$A\cup B=\{\omega:\ \omega\in A\ \text{or}\ \omega\in B\}\qquad A\cap B=\{\omega:\ \omega\in A\ \text{and}\ \omega\in B\}$$ $$A^c=\{\omega:\ \omega\notin A\}\qquad\qquad B-A=\{\omega:\ \omega\in B\ \text{and}\ \omega\notin A\}$$
A handy identity
"B but not A" is the same as "B and not-A": $\;B-A=B\cap A^c$. The lecture's definition $\{\omega:\omega\in B,\ \omega\notin A\}$ says exactly this. It is how differences get turned into intersections in proofs (module 05).
TRY IT Shade any expression on one roll of a die— ⊡ ✕

Pick an operation and see which faces survive. Switch to the second pair of events to replay the "even / prime" example from the notes. Compare (A∪B)ᶜ with Aᶜ∩Bᶜ, and (A∩B)ᶜ with Aᶜ∪Bᶜ — they always shade the same region. That is De Morgan's law, below.

RULES Set laws (from the PDF notes)— ⊡ ✕

For any sets $A,B,C$ in $\Omega$:

  • double complement $\;(A^c)^c=A$ — "not not A" is A.
  • commutative $\;A\cup B=B\cup A,\quad A\cap B=B\cap A$ — order does not matter.
  • associative $\;A\cup(B\cup C)=(A\cup B)\cup C$, same for $\cap$ — so we can drop brackets and write $A\cup B\cup C$.
  • distributive $$A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)$$ $$A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$$ Like $a(b+c)=ab+ac$, with $\cap$ playing "times" and $\cup$ playing "plus" — except here it works both ways round.
  • De Morgan's laws $$\boxed{(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\qquad(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c}$$

The lecture: proofs not required. The pictures on the right are the proof you need to believe them.

De Morgan in words
"Not (rain or snow)" = "no rain and no snow". "Not (rich and famous)" = "not rich or not famous". The complement flips $\cup\leftrightarrow\cap$.
SEE IT De Morgan, one shading at a time— ⊡ ✕
LECTURE 1 · notes p.3–4 · slides Axioms 7–8 — coin and die

Coin. $\Omega=\{H,T\}$, $E_1=\{\text{heads}\}=\{H\}$, $E_2=\{\text{tails}\}=\{T\}$.

  1. $E_1^c=\{H\}^c=\{T\}=E_2$ and $E_2^c=\{\text{"not" }T\}=\{H\}=E_1$.
  2. $E_1\cap E_2=\{H\}\cap\{T\}=\emptyset$ — no outcome is both, the impossible event.
  3. $E_1\cup E_2=\{H\}\cup\{T\}=\Omega$ — the sure event.

Die. $E_1=\{2,4,6\}$ "even", $E_2=\{2,3,5\}$ "prime", $E_3=\{6\}$ "greater than 5".

  1. $E_1^c=\{\omega:\omega\notin E_1\}=\{1,3,5\}$.
  2. "Prime and even" $=E_1\cap E_2=\{2,4,6\}\cap\{2,3,5\}=\{2\}$ — the only even prime.
  3. "Prime and greater than 5" $=E_2\cap E_3=\{2,3,5\}\cap\{6\}=\emptyset$.
Insight
$E_1$ and $E_2$ in the coin example have empty intersection and fill $\Omega$ between them: they are complements. Two events with $A\cap B=\emptyset$ are called mutually exclusive (disjoint) — the key word of Kolmogorov's third axiom in module 04.
LECTURE 1 · notes p.5 · slides Axioms 9 — light-bulb lifetimes

$\Omega=[0,+\infty)$ hours; $E_1=\{1000\}$, $E_2=\{\text{less than }3000\}=[0,3000)$, $E_3=\{\text{more than }2000\}=(2000,+\infty)$.

  1. $E_1\cap E_3=\emptyset$: lasting exactly 1000 h is not lasting more than 2000 h.
  2. $E_2\cap E_3$ = "more than 2000 h and less than 3000 h" $=[0,3000)\cap(2000,+\infty)=(2000,3000)$. Intersect intervals by taking the larger left end and the smaller right end.
  3. $E_2\cup E_3$ = "more than 2000 h or less than 3000 h" $=[0,3000)\cup(2000,+\infty)=\Omega$: every lifetime satisfies at least one condition.

The PDF slides phrase $E_3$ as "2000 hours or more" $=[2000,+\infty)$; then $E_2\cap E_3=[2000,3000)$, and they add $E_1\cup E_3=\{1000\}\cup[2000,+\infty)$. The endpoint changes nothing in the reasoning (and, as module 06 shows, nothing in the probability).

TRAP Three translation slips that cost exam points— ⊡ ✕
  • "Or" is inclusive. $A\cup B$ contains the outcomes in both. "Exactly one of A and B" is a different event: $(A-B)\cup(B-A)$.
  • $B-A\neq A-B$. With $A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{1,2\}$: $B-A=\{1\}$ but $A-B=\{4,6\}$.
  • Complement of a union is not the union of complements. $(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c$ — the operation flips. Writing $A^c\cup B^c$ is the most common De Morgan error.
?check yourselfDe Morgan on the die
With $A=\{2,4,6\}$ and $B=\{1,2\}$, compute $(A\cup B)^c$ both directly and as $A^c\cap B^c$.
Answer
Directly: $A\cup B=\{1,2,4,6\}$, so $(A\cup B)^c=\{3,5\}$. Via De Morgan: $A^c=\{1,3,5\}$, $B^c=\{3,4,5,6\}$, and $A^c\cap B^c=\{3,5\}$ ✓.
?check yourselfExactly one
Write "exactly one of $E_1$ (even) and $E_2$ (prime) happens" as a set, for one die.
Answer
$(E_1-E_2)\cup(E_2-E_1)=\{4,6\}\cup\{3,5\}=\{3,4,5,6\}$. Equivalently $(E_1\cup E_2)-(E_1\cap E_2)=\{2,3,4,5,6\}-\{2\}$.
