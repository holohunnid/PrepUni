04

Kolmogorov's axioms

lecture 1, notes p.6–7 · slides: Axioms 11–14
READ FIRST Three rules, and everything else is a consequence— ⊡ ✕

Any mathematical theory starts from axioms: a few simple, consistent statements accepted without proof. Everything else — the theorems — is derived from them by logic. In 1933 Andrei Kolmogorov did this for probability: he showed that three properties are enough, and every rule you have ever used ($P(A^c)=1-P(A)$, the addition rule, Bayes…) follows from them.

The easiest way to hold the axioms in your head is to think of probability as mass — one kilogram of sand spread over $\Omega$. $P(E)$ is the amount of sand lying on $E$. Then the three axioms say the obvious things about sand: an amount is never negative; there is exactly one kilogram in total; and if you split a region into pieces that do not overlap, the amounts on the pieces add up.

What an axiom does NOT tell you
The axioms don't say what $P(E)$ is for a particular experiment — a fair die and a loaded die both satisfy them. They only say what any sensible assignment must obey. Choosing the numbers is modelling ("the die is fair", module 05).
DEF The axioms of probability— ⊡ ✕

To each event $E$ we associate a number $P(E)$ such that

  1. $\boxed{P(E)\ge 0}$ for every event $E$;
  2. $\boxed{P(\Omega)=1}$;
  3. if $E_1,E_2,\dots$ is an infinite sequence of mutually exclusive events — that is, $E_i\cap E_j=\emptyset$ for all $i\neq j$ — then $$\boxed{P\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(E_i)}$$

A function $P$ satisfying these is a probability function, and $P(E)$ is the probability of $E$.

Reading axiom 3

"Mutually exclusive" means no two of the events can happen together — the pieces don't overlap. Then the probability that one of them happens is the sum of their probabilities. No overlap ⇒ nothing is double counted ⇒ just add (compare module 03, where overlap forced a correction).

SEE IT Probability as one unit of mass— ⊡ ✕

Why an infinite sequence?

Because sample spaces can be infinite (module 01). For the stubborn coin flipper with a fair coin, the outcomes $H, TH, TTH,\dots$ carry $\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\dots$ — infinitely many disjoint pieces, and axiom 3 is what lets us add them all up:

THEOREM The impossible event has probability 0— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{P(\emptyset)=0}$$

A first direct consequence — and a nice example of how theorems come out of axioms. The trick is to choose a clever sequence and feed it to axiom 3.

  1. Choose $E_1=\Omega$ and $E_2=E_3=\dots=\emptyset$. These are mutually exclusive: $\Omega\cap\emptyset=\emptyset$ and $\emptyset\cap\emptyset=\emptyset$. Their union is $\Omega\cup\emptyset\cup\emptyset\cup\dots=\Omega$.
  2. Axiom 3 gives $$P\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(E_i)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(\Omega)=P(\Omega)+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}P(\emptyset)$$
  3. Axiom 2 replaces $P(\Omega)$ by 1: $$1=1+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}P(\emptyset)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}P(\emptyset)=0$$
  4. An infinite sum of copies of the same non-negative number (axiom 1) can only be 0 if that number is 0. So $P(\emptyset)=0$. $\square$
Insight
Notice that we never "assumed" $P(\emptyset)=0$ — it would have been redundant. Good axiom systems are minimal: nothing that can be proved is taken as an axiom.
WARNING The converses are false— ⊡ ✕

We know $P(\Omega)=1$ and $P(\emptyset)=0$. But the arrows only go one way:

$$\begin{cases}E=\Omega\ \Longrightarrow\ P(E)=1\\ P(E)=1\ \not\Longrightarrow\ E=\Omega\end{cases}\qquad\begin{cases}E=\emptyset\ \Longrightarrow\ P(E)=0\\ P(E)=0\ \not\Longrightarrow\ E=\emptyset\end{cases}$$

A probability-zero event can be perfectly possible, and a probability-one event can fail. The example comes from module 06: pick a point at random from $(0,1)$.

THEOREM Finite additivity — the version you actually use— ⊡ ✕

If $E_1,E_2,\dots,E_m$ is a finite sequence of mutually exclusive events, then

$$\boxed{P\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}E_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}P(E_i)}$$

Special case ($m=2$, $E_1=A$, $E_2=B$): for mutually exclusive $A,B$,

$$\boxed{P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)}$$

Axiom 3 talks about infinite sequences, but in practice you split events into two or three pieces. This theorem says axiom 3 covers that too.

Proof (notes p.7): pad with empty sets

  1. Let $E_{m+1}=E_{m+2}=\dots=\emptyset$. The long sequence $E_1,\dots,E_m,\emptyset,\emptyset,\dots$ is still mutually exclusive.
  2. Axiom 3 on the long sequence: $$P\Big(\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}E_i\Big)\cup\Big(\bigcup_{i=m+1}^{\infty}E_i\Big)\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}P(E_i)+\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}P(E_i)$$
  3. The second union is empty, so the left side is just $P(\bigcup_{i=1}^mE_i)$. Each term of the second sum is $P(\emptyset)=0$ by the previous theorem.
  4. Hence $P\big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}E_i\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}P(E_i)$. $\square$
Trap
$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ needs $A\cap B=\emptyset$. For overlapping events it overcounts — the general rule, with the $-P(A\cap B)$ correction, is in module 05.
?check yourselfImpossible numbers
Someone claims $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive with $P(A)=0.6$ and $P(B)=0.5$. Why must they be wrong?
Answer
Disjoint ⇒ $P(A\cup B)=0.6+0.5=1.1$. But $A\cup B\subseteq\Omega$, and (module 05) no probability exceeds 1. So the events cannot be disjoint: they must overlap by at least $0.1$.
?check yourselfA loaded die
Is $P(\{1\})=0.5$, $P(\{2\})=\dots=P(\{6\})=0.1$ a valid probability on the die?
Answer
Yes. All numbers are $\ge0$ (axiom 1), they add to $0.5+5(0.1)=1$ (axiom 2), and every event's probability is the sum over its outcomes (axiom 3). It just isn't a fair die — the axioms allow any such assignment.
