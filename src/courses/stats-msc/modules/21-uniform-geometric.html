Discrete uniform & geometric

lecture 3, notes p.8 · slides: Special Discrete Distributions 31–38
READ FIRST The questions this module answers— ⊡ ✕

The last two named discrete distributions of the deck answer two very different questions:

  1. Discrete uniform. "Every whole number from $a$ to $b$ is equally likely. What are the probabilities, the mean and the spread?" A fair die, a random digit, a ticket drawn from a numbered hat. The distribution of complete ignorance over a range of integers.
  2. Geometric. "I repeat a yes/no trial until the first success. How long do I wait?" The first default in a loan book, the first day a VaR limit is breached, the first ace in a shuffled deck. The binomial (module 19) fixes the number of trials and counts successes. The geometric fixes the number of successes at one and counts the trials.

You have met a geometric already. In module 15 we checked that $f(k)=\tfrac15\left(\tfrac45\right)^{k-1}$, $k=1,2,\dots$ sums to 1. That pmf is exactly the Geometric distribution with $p=\tfrac15$ (version I below). This module gives it a name, a mean and a variance.

At the end: the complete summary table of all six discrete distributions in the deck, and a short guide to recognising which one a problem is about. That recognition is where most exam marks are won or lost.

The classic confusion, flagged up front
The slides give two geometric distributions under the same name: one counts the trials up to and including the first success (support $1,2,3,\dots$), the other counts the failures before it (support $0,1,2,\dots$). They differ by exactly 1. R's dgeom uses the second. Always check which one a question or a formula means.
LECTURE 3 What the lecturer said (notes p.8)— ⊡ ✕

Discrete uniform: "equal probability"

For integers $a<b$: $X\sim\text{DUnif}(a,b)$ if

$$f(k)=P(X=k)=\frac1{b-a+1},\qquad k=a,\dots,b$$

— a formula that does not depend on $k$: every value gets the same bar. The two moments, with the notes' reading of each:

$$\mathbb E(X)=\frac{a+b}2,\qquad \mathbb V(X)=\frac{(b-a+1)^2-1}{12}$$
  • $\frac{a+b}{2}$ is the midpoint between $a$ and $b$ — the balance point, because the support points are regularly spaced and equally weighted;
  • $b-a+1$ is the range of the distribution: the number of possible values. More values ⇒ more spread.

The die (the notes' example): $X\sim\text{DUnif}(1,6)$, $f(k)=\frac1{6-1+1}=\frac16$, $\mathbb E(X)=\frac{1+6}2=3.5$, $\mathbb V(X)=\frac{(6-1+1)^2-1}{12}=\frac{35}{12}$.

Geometric: "n is not fixed"

Bernoulli trials with success probability $p$, but — unlike the binomial — the number of trials is not fixed. The question is: how many trials until the first success?

$$X\sim\text{Geo}(p):\quad f(k)=(1-p)^{k-1}\,p,\ \ k=1,2,3,\dots\qquad \mathbb E(X)=\frac1p,\quad \mathbb V(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}$$

The last factor $p$ is the success on the last trial. On $\mathbb E(X)=1/p$ the notes write: higher success probability ⇒ fewer trials needed.

Alternative version: $X$ = number of trials before a success = number of failures up to the success — "not counting the last trial, when you have the success":

$$f(k)=(1-p)^k\,p,\ \ k=0,1,2,\dots\qquad \mathbb E(X)=\frac{1-p}p=\frac1p-1,\quad \mathbb V(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}$$
DEF Discrete uniform random variable (slide 31)— ⊡ ✕

Let $a$ and $b$ be integers with $a\le b$. A discrete random variable $X$ with support $\{a,a+1,a+2,\dots,b\}$ is a discrete uniform random variable, written $X\sim\text{DUniform}(a,b)$, if its pmf is

$$\boxed{\;f(k)=P(X=k)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{b-a+1}&k\in\{a,a+1,a+2,\dots,b\}\\[6pt]0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\;}$$

Why $b-a+1$? It is the number of integers from $a$ to $b$ inclusive: from 1 to 6 there are $6-1+1=6$ values, not 5. Giving each of them the same probability and requiring the total to be 1 forces each to be $1/(b-a+1)$.

Mean and variance (slide 31). If $X\sim\text{DUniform}(a,b)$ then

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}(X)=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{(b-a+1)^2-1}{12}\;}$$

The mean is the midpoint, by symmetry: the bars are flat, so the balance point is in the middle. The variance depends only on the number of values $m=b-a+1$, not on where they sit. Shifting all values by a constant moves the mean but not the spread (module 17: $\mathbb{V}(X+c)=\mathbb{V}(X)$).

Where the variance comes from (sketch, not on the slides). Shift to $Y=X-a+1$, uniform on $1,\dots,m$. Using $\sum_{k=1}^mk=\frac{m(m+1)}2$ and $\sum_{k=1}^mk^2=\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}6$: $\mathbb{E}(Y)=\frac{m+1}2$ and $\mathbb{E}(Y^2)=\frac{(m+1)(2m+1)}6$, so $\mathbb{V}(Y)=\frac{(m+1)(2m+1)}6-\frac{(m+1)^2}4=\frac{(m+1)(m-1)}{12}=\frac{m^2-1}{12}$.

LECTURE 3 · notes p.8 · slide 32 · a fair die

We toss a fair die. Let $X$ be the observed number. How is $X$ distributed? Find its mean and variance.

  1. Support and probabilities. The support is $\{1,2,\dots,6\}$ and the die is fair, so $$P(X=1)=P(X=2)=\cdots=P(X=6)=\tfrac16$$ Therefore $X$ is discrete uniform: $X\sim\text{DUniform}(a=1,\,b=6)$.
  2. Mean from the formula: $$\mathbb{E}(X)=\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{1+6}{2}=\frac72=3.5$$
  3. Variance from the formula: $$\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{(b-a+1)^2-1}{12}=\frac{(6-1+1)^2-1}{12}=\frac{36-1}{12}=\frac{35}{12}\approx2.917$$
  4. Verify directly with $\mathbb{V}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2)-[\mathbb{E}(X)]^2$ (module 17): $$\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6}=\frac{91}{6}$$ $$\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{91}{6}-\left(\frac72\right)^2=\frac{182}{12}-\frac{147}{12}=\frac{35}{12}\ \checkmark$$
  5. Standard deviation: $\sqrt{35/12}\approx1.708$.
Insight
The mean 3.5 is a value the die can never show. An expected value is a balance point (module 16), not a prediction of a single roll. A typical roll lands about 1.7 away from it.

TRAP: var(1:6) is not 35/12
var(1:6) returns 3.5, not 2.9167. R's var is the sample variance: it divides the sum of squared deviations ($17.5$) by $n-1=5$ instead of $n=6$. For the variance of a distribution whose values are all equally likely, divide by $n$: $17.5/6=35/12$. This $n$ versus $n-1$ difference is a favourite R multiple-choice question.

DEF Geometric, version I: trials up to the first success (slide 34)— ⊡ ✕

Repeat a Bernoulli trial with success probability $p$, independently. Let $X$ be the number of trials needed to obtain the first success, counting the successful trial itself. The support is $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$ (at least one trial is always needed), and

$$\boxed{\;f(k)=P(X=k)=\begin{cases}pq^{k-1}&k\in\{1,2,3,\dots\}\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\;}$$

$X$ is a geometric random variable with parameter $p$: $X\sim\text{Geometric}(p)$.

Why this pmf. "$X=k$" means: the first $k-1$ trials all fail, then trial $k$ succeeds. By independence that sequence has probability $\underbrace{q\cdot q\cdots q}_{k-1}\cdot p=pq^{k-1}$. There is exactly one such sequence, so no counting factor is needed, unlike the binomial.

Mean and variance (slide 34):

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}(X)=\frac1p,\qquad\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}\;}$$

Module 15's pmf $\tfrac15\left(\tfrac45\right)^{k-1}$ is version I with $p=\tfrac15$, so its mean is $\mathbb{E}(X)=5$ trials and its variance $\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{4/5}{1/25}=20$.

DEF Geometric, version II: failures before the first success (slide 35)— ⊡ ✕

Same experiment, different count. Let $X$ be the number of failures until the first success, not counting the success. The support is $\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$ (0 means the very first trial succeeded), and

$$\boxed{\;f(k)=P(X=k)=\begin{cases}pq^{k}&k\in\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\;}$$

The slides call this $X\sim\text{Geometric}(p)$ as well.

Why this pmf. "$X=k$" means $k$ failures then a success: probability $q^k\cdot p$.

Mean and variance (slide 35):

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}(X)=\frac{1-p}{p},\qquad\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}\;}$$

The link between the versions. Failures = trials $-\,1$. So if $X_{\rm I}$ is version I, then $X_{\rm II}=X_{\rm I}-1$. Subtracting a constant lowers the mean by exactly 1, $\frac1p-1=\frac{1-p}{p}$ ✓, and leaves the variance unchanged, which is why both versions share $\frac{1-p}{p^2}$ ✓.

The summary table writes $p\in[0,1]$. In practice $p>0$ is needed: with $p=0$ the first success never arrives and the pmf is identically zero.

TRAP: R's dgeom is version II
dgeom(k, prob) $=pq^k$ counts failures, and pgeom(k, prob) $=P(X\le k)=1-q^{k+1}$ is its cdf. For version I (trials) shift by one: $P(X_{\rm I}=k)$ is dgeom(k - 1, p). Check: dgeom(0, 1/5) → 0.2, which is $P(\text{success on the first trial})$: 0 failures in version II, 1 trial in version I.

Wording decides the version. "How many trials until / to get the first success" means version I. "How many failures, or draws before, the first success" means version II. The aces example below says "draws needed before an ace", so it is version II.

PROOF Why $\mathbb{E}(X)=1/p$ (version I)— ⊡ ✕

The slides state the mean and variance without proof. Here are two derivations of the mean, a short one and a careful one.

1. The first-step argument (intuition)

Let $E=\mathbb{E}(X)$. The first trial is always used. With probability $p$ it succeeds and we stop. With probability $q$ it fails, and then, because trials are independent, we are back at the start, needing on average another $E$ trials. So

$$E=1+q\,E\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E(1-q)=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E=\frac{1}{1-q}=\frac1p$$

A success with probability $\tfrac15$ takes 5 tries on average. A six on a die takes 6 rolls. A 1% event takes 100 days.

2. From the series (rigorous)

  1. Definition: $\mathbb{E}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\,pq^{k-1}=p\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\,q^{k-1}$.
  2. Geometric series (the same one module 15 used to check the total is 1): $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^k=\frac{1}{1-q}$ for $0\le q<1$.
  3. Differentiate both sides in $q$: $\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\,q^{k-1}=\frac{1}{(1-q)^2}=\frac{1}{p^2}$.
  4. Collect: $\mathbb{E}(X)=p\cdot\dfrac{1}{p^2}=\dfrac1p\qquad\square$

Variance, sketch. Differentiating once more gives $\mathbb{E}[X(X-1)]=\frac{2q}{p^2}$, so $\mathbb{E}(X^2)=\frac{2q}{p^2}+\frac1p$ and $\mathbb{V}(X)=\frac{2q}{p^2}+\frac1p-\frac1{p^2}=\frac{2q+p-1}{p^2}=\frac{q}{p^2}=\frac{1-p}{p^2}$.

The tail, which you will use constantly. "More than $k$ trials needed" (version I) means the first $k$ trials all fail, so $P(X_{\rm I}>k)=q^k$. In version II, "at least $k$ failures" means the same event, so $P(X_{\rm II}\ge k)=q^k$. No summation required. The aces example shows the long way round and then this shortcut.

EXAMPLE (slides 36–37) · drawing cards until the first ace

From a deck of cards we draw cards at random, with replacement, before drawing an ace. (1) Find the probability that 2 draws are needed before an ace. (2) Find the probability that at least 10 draws are needed before an ace.

  1. The trial. Each draw is a Bernoulli trial with success = "ace". With replacement, the draws are independent and $p$ never changes: $$p=\frac{4}{52}=\frac{1}{13},\qquad q=\frac{12}{13}$$
  2. The variable and the version. $X$ = number of draws needed before the first ace appears, i.e. the number of non-aces before the first ace. That counts failures, so it is version II: $$X\sim\text{Geometric}(p=1/13),\qquad f(k)=pq^k,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots$$
  3. (1) Two draws before an ace. $X=2$ means non-ace, non-ace, ace: $$P(X=2)=pq^2=\frac1{13}\left(\frac{12}{13}\right)^2=\frac{144}{2197}=\mathbf{0.06554392}$$
  4. (2) At least 10 draws before an ace. Add all the terms from 10 on: $$\begin{aligned} P(X\ge10)&=P(X=10)+P(X=11)+P(X=12)+\cdots\\ &=f(10)+f(11)+f(12)+\cdots\\ &=pq^{10}+pq^{11}+pq^{12}+\cdots\\ &=pq^{10}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^j\qquad\text{(factor out }pq^{10}\text{)}\\ &=\frac{pq^{10}}{1-q}\qquad\text{(geometric series)}\\ &=q^{10}\qquad\text{(since }1-q=p\text{)}\\ &=\left(\frac{12}{13}\right)^{10}\simeq\mathbf{0.4491371} \end{aligned}$$
  5. The shortcut, and what it means. "At least 10 non-aces before the first ace" is the same event as "the first 10 draws all fail" (all non-aces). By independence that has probability $q^{10}$ directly. The infinite sum in step 4 is just a longer route to the same place.
Insight
The pmf is largest at $k=0$: the single most likely outcome is an ace on the very first draw (probability $1/13\approx0.077$). Yet the distribution is so spread out that there is a 45% chance of 10 or more non-aces first. The mean is 12 and the standard deviation $\sqrt{156}\approx12.5$ is as large as the mean. Waiting times for rare events are long and very unpredictable.
TRAP: the wrong version gives a different answer
With version I (draws up to and including the ace), $X=2$ would mean "the ace comes on the 2nd draw": $pq=\frac{12}{169}=0.0710$. That answers a different question. The slide's answer $pq^2$ is version II. When a question is ambiguous, say which convention you use.

TEASER The geometric has no memory— ⊡ ✕

Suppose you have already drawn 10 non-aces. Is an ace now "due"? Compute, with $X$ version II and the tail formula $P(X\ge k)=q^k$:

$$P(X\ge10+5\mid X\ge10)=\frac{P(X\ge15)}{P(X\ge10)}=\frac{q^{15}}{q^{10}}=q^{5}=P(X\ge5)$$

(Conditional probability, module 07. The event $\{X\ge15\}$ is contained in $\{X\ge10\}$, so their intersection is $\{X\ge15\}$.) Having already waited 10 draws, the chance of waiting at least 5 more is exactly the same as it was at the very start. The past failures are forgotten: this is the memoryless property, and it follows directly from independence. The belief that an ace (or a market reversal, or a red number at roulette) becomes more likely after a long drought is the gambler's fallacy.

Module 24 meets the continuous version: the exponential distribution, the waiting time of a Poisson process, is the only continuous distribution with this property.

Connections worth remembering: Bernoulli = Binomial with $n=1$; Binomial = sum of $n$ i.i.d. Bernoullis; Poisson = limit of Binomial as $n\to\infty$, $p\to0$, $np=\lambda$; Geometric II = Geometric I $-\,1$. The binomial counts successes in a fixed number of trials, and the geometric counts trials until a fixed number (one) of successes.

GUIDE Which distribution is it?— ⊡ ✕
  1. Is there a fixed number $n$ of independent yes/no trials, and $X$ counts the successes? Binomial$(n,p)$. One trial: Bernoulli$(p)$. Keywords: "out of", "in $n$ tosses / loans / days".
  2. Does $X$ count events in a window of time or space, given only an average rate? Poisson$(\lambda)$, with $\lambda$ = expected count in that window. Also use it for a binomial with huge $n$ and tiny $p$. Keywords: "per hour", "on average", "rare".
  3. Does $X$ measure how long until the first success? Geometric$(p)$. Then decide: trials up to and including the success (I, starts at 1) or failures before it (II, starts at 0). Keywords: "until", "before", "first".
  4. Is every integer between $a$ and $b$ equally likely? DUniform$(a,b)$. Keywords: "fair", "at random from 1 to …".
Upper limit as a quick discriminator
Binomial: $X\le n$. DUniform: $a\le X\le b$. Poisson and geometric: no upper bound. If the question's variable could in principle be arbitrarily large, it is not binomial.

Give the distribution, with parameters, and the mean: (a) the number of trading days until the first exceedance of a correct 99% VaR; (b) the number of client complaints in a week, when complaints average 3 per week; (c) the number of the next 12 months with a positive return, if each month is independently positive with probability 0.6; (d) a digit drawn at random from 0 to 9.
Answer
  1. (a) Geometric I with $p=0.01$ (days until and including the exceedance): $\mathbb{E}=1/p=100$ days.
  2. (b) Poisson(3): $\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{V}=3$.
  3. (c) Binomial(12, 0.6): $\mathbb{E}=7.2$ months, $\mathbb{V}=12\times0.6\times0.4=2.88$.
  4. (d) DUniform(0, 9): $\mathbb{E}=4.5$, $\mathbb{V}=\frac{10^2-1}{12}=8.25$.
