05

Differentiation rules

the table, the three combination rules, and the two tricks
READ FIRST Never use the definition again— ⊡ ✕

Module 4 computed derivatives from the limit definition. That was necessary once, to know what a derivative is. It is far too slow to use again. This module replaces it with machinery: a short table of basic derivatives, plus rules for combining them, from which every function you will meet can be differentiated mechanically.

The structure is worth seeing before the details, because it explains why this is a short module and not an endless list:

  • A table of about eight basic functions. Memorise it.
  • Three combination rules — product, quotient, chain — which handle every way of building a function out of simpler ones.
  • Two tricks — implicit and logarithmic differentiation — for the cases where the function isn't handed to you in the form $y=f(x)$, or where taking logs first makes it far easier.

The one skill underneath all of it is recognising structure: before differentiating anything, ask "is this a sum, a product, a quotient, or a composition?" Get that right and the rule follows automatically. Get it wrong and no amount of care with the algebra saves you. This is why module 1 insisted you practise decomposing $e^{\sqrt{x+1}}$ into layers.

The power rule is more general than it looks

$n$ can be any real number — negative, fractional, irrational. The move that unlocks it is rewriting roots and reciprocals as powers before differentiating:

$\sqrt x=x^{1/2}$$\Rightarrow\;\tfrac12x^{-1/2}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$
$\dfrac1x=x^{-1}$$\Rightarrow\;-x^{-2}=-\dfrac1{x^2}$
$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt x}=x^{-1/2}$$\Rightarrow\;-\tfrac12x^{-3/2}$
$\sqrt[3]{x^2}=x^{2/3}$$\Rightarrow\;\tfrac23x^{-1/3}$

Both derivatives computed painfully from first principles in module 4 fall out of the power rule in one line each. That is the point of having a table.

Trap
$\dfrac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x$, but $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^e=ex^{e-1}$. Look at where the variable is: variable in the exponent → exponential rule; variable in the base → power rule. Confusing the two is the most common single error in this module. (And when the variable is in both, as in $x^x$, neither rule applies — that needs logarithmic differentiation.)

Check yourself: differentiate $f(x)=\frac{3}{x^2}$. (Answer: rewrite as $3x^{-2}$, giving $-6x^{-3}=-6/x^3$.)

IDEA 1 Linearity, product and quotient— ⊡ ✕

Linearity — the easy one

$$\big(cf\big)'=cf'\qquad\qquad \big(f\pm g\big)'=f'\pm g'$$

Constants slide out; sums differentiate term by term. So any polynomial is immediate: $\frac{d}{dx}(5x^3-2x+7)=15x^2-2$.

Product rule

$$\boxed{(fg)'=f'g+fg'}$$

Say it out loud as you use it: "first prime times second, plus first times second prime." A rhythm prevents dropped terms.

Why it is not $f'g'$. A rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$ has area $fg$. Grow both sides a little: the area gains a strip $\Delta f\cdot g$ along one side, a strip $f\cdot\Delta g$ along the other, and a tiny corner $\Delta f\Delta g$. Divide by $\Delta x$ and shrink: the two strips become $f'g$ and $fg'$, and the corner — being second-order small — vanishes. The two strips are the two terms.

$y=x^2e^x$

$f=x^2$, $f'=2x$; $g=e^x$, $g'=e^x$.

$y'=2xe^x+x^2e^x=xe^x(2+x)$ — always factor at the end; it is usually required for the next part of the question.

Quotient rule

$$\boxed{\left(\frac fg\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}}$$

"Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared." The order matters here — unlike the product rule, this one is not symmetric, and swapping the terms flips the sign of your answer.

$y=\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$

$f=x,f'=1$; $g=x^2+1,g'=2x$.

$y'=\dfrac{1\cdot(x^2+1)-x\cdot2x}{(x^2+1)^2}=\dfrac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$.

Note the numerator is zero at $x=\pm1$ — those are the turning points of this curve. Setting a quotient derivative to zero only ever needs the numerator.

Shortcut
If the denominator is a constant, don't use the quotient rule: $\frac{x^2}{5}=\frac15x^2$ differentiates to $\frac25x$. And if the numerator is constant, a negative power is often cleaner than the quotient rule.
IDEA 2 The chain rule — the one that decides your grade— ⊡ ✕

The chain rule differentiates a composition $f(g(x))$ — a function inside another function. It is used more than every other rule combined, and it is where most marks are lost.

$$\boxed{\big[f(g(x))\big]'=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)}$$

or in Leibniz notation, where it looks like cancellation:

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$$

The intuition. Rates multiply along a chain. If $y$ changes 3 times as fast as $u$, and $u$ changes 5 times as fast as $x$, then $y$ changes 15 times as fast as $x$. Gears in a gearbox: the ratios compound.

The procedure, which you should follow literally until it is automatic:

  1. Identify the outer function and the inner function.
  2. Differentiate the outer, leaving the inner untouched inside it.
  3. Multiply by the derivative of the inner.

Step 2 is where errors happen. The inner function is copied across unchanged; it is not differentiated inside the outer derivative.

Trap
The most common error in all of calculus is forgetting the $\cdot\,g'(x)$. $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x)$ is $3\cos(3x)$, not $\cos(3x)$. Build the habit: whenever you differentiate a bracket, immediately write "times the derivative of the inside" before doing anything else.

The common patterns, once and for all

$[g(x)]^n$$n[g(x)]^{n-1}g'(x)$
$e^{g(x)}$$e^{g(x)}g'(x)$
$\ln g(x)$$\dfrac{g'(x)}{g(x)}$
$\sqrt{g(x)}$$\dfrac{g'(x)}{2\sqrt{g(x)}}$
$\sin g(x)$$\cos\!\big(g(x)\big)g'(x)$

The $\ln$ line is worth staring at: derivative of the inside over the inside. It reappears constantly, and reversed it becomes the most useful integral in module 8.

Four in sequence
  1. $y=(3x^2+1)^5$. Outer $\square^5$, inner $3x^2+1$. $\;y'=5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot 6x=30x(3x^2+1)^4$.
  2. $y=e^{-x^2}$. Outer $e^\square$, inner $-x^2$. $\;y'=e^{-x^2}\cdot(-2x)=-2xe^{-x^2}$.
  3. $y=\ln(x^2+1)$. $\;y'=\dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}$.
  4. $y=\sin^3(2x)$ — two layers of chain. Read it as $[\sin(2x)]^3$: outer cube, then $\sin$, then $2x$. $\;y'=3\sin^2(2x)\cdot\cos(2x)\cdot 2=6\sin^2(2x)\cos(2x)$.
Combining rules: $y=x^2\sqrt{x^3+1}$

Outermost structure is a product, so the product rule governs; the chain rule handles the second factor.

$f=x^2\Rightarrow f'=2x$. $\;g=(x^3+1)^{1/2}\Rightarrow g'=\tfrac12(x^3+1)^{-1/2}\cdot3x^2=\dfrac{3x^2}{2\sqrt{x^3+1}}$.

$y'=2x\sqrt{x^3+1}+\dfrac{3x^4}{2\sqrt{x^3+1}}$.

Always ask which rule is outermost first. Getting the hierarchy right is more important than the algebra.

IDEA 3 Implicit differentiation— ⊡ ✕

Sometimes $y$ is not given explicitly as a formula in $x$; instead $x$ and $y$ are tangled in one equation, like $x^2+y^2=25$. Solving for $y$ may be ugly or impossible. Implicit differentiation avoids solving altogether.

The idea: treat $y$ as an unknown function of $x$, differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, and apply the chain rule every time a $y$ appears. Since $y=y(x)$, the derivative of any expression in $y$ picks up a factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big[y^2\big]=2y\frac{dy}{dx},\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big[e^y\big]=e^y\frac{dy}{dx},\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big[xy\big]=y+x\frac{dy}{dx}$$

The last one needs the product rule and the chain rule — a very common combination.

Tangent to the circle $x^2+y^2=25$ at $(3,4)$
  1. Differentiate both sides: $2x+2y\dfrac{dy}{dx}=0$.
  2. Solve for the derivative: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{x}{y}$.
  3. Evaluate at $(3,4)$: slope $=-\tfrac34$.
  4. Tangent line: $y-4=-\tfrac34(x-3)$.

Sanity check: the tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius. The radius to $(3,4)$ has slope $\tfrac43$, and $-\tfrac34$ is its negative reciprocal ✓. Note also that the answer depends on both $x$ and $y$ — that is normal and correct for implicit derivatives.

$x^3+y^3=6xy$ (the folium)

$3x^2+3y^2y'=6y+6xy'$. Gather the $y'$ terms: $y'(3y^2-6x)=6y-3x^2$, so

$$y'=\frac{6y-3x^2}{3y^2-6x}=\frac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}$$

This curve cannot be written as a single function $y=f(x)$ at all — it fails the vertical line test. Implicit differentiation does not care.

The routine: differentiate everything, attach $\frac{dy}{dx}$ wherever a $y$ was differentiated, collect all $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms on one side, factor, divide.

Check yourself: find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $xy=1$. (Answer: $y+xy'=0$, so $y'=-y/x$. Check against $y=1/x$: $y'=-1/x^2=-y/x$ ✓.)

TIP Logarithmic differentiation, and a decision procedure— ⊡ ✕

When a function is a big product, quotient, or has the variable in both base and exponent, take logs first. Logs turn products into sums and exponents into multipliers — precisely the operations differentiation handles well.

Method: write $y=f(x)$, take $\ln$ of both sides, simplify using the log rules from module 1, differentiate implicitly (the left side gives $\frac{y'}{y}$), then multiply through by $y$.

$y=x^x$ — neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies
  1. $\ln y=x\ln x$.
  2. Differentiate both sides. Left: $\dfrac{y'}{y}$. Right (product rule): $1\cdot\ln x+x\cdot\dfrac1x=\ln x+1$.
  3. So $y'=y(\ln x+1)=x^x(\ln x+1)$.

Alternative route: rewrite $x^x=e^{x\ln x}$ and use the chain rule. Same answer — and a good check.

$y=\dfrac{(x+1)^3\sqrt{x-2}}{(x+3)^4}$

Quotient plus product plus chain would be a nightmare. Take logs:

$$\ln y=3\ln(x+1)+\tfrac12\ln(x-2)-4\ln(x+3)$$

Differentiate — every term is now trivial:

$$\frac{y'}{y}=\frac{3}{x+1}+\frac{1}{2(x-2)}-\frac{4}{x+3}$$

Multiply by $y$ to finish. Nested rules avoided entirely.

Which rule? A decision procedure

Before writing anything, look at the outermost structure of the expression and answer in order:

  1. Is it a sum or difference? → differentiate term by term, then recurse into each term.
  2. Is it a constant times something? → pull the constant out.
  3. Is it a product? → product rule.
  4. Is it a quotient? → quotient rule (unless the denominator is constant, or a negative power is cleaner).
  5. Is it a composition (something inside a bracket, root, exponent, or log)? → chain rule.
  6. Is it a big product/quotient, or is $x$ in both base and exponent? → logarithmic differentiation.
  7. Is $y$ tangled with $x$ in an equation? → implicit differentiation.
Two habits worth forming
Simplify before differentiating. $\ln(x^2\sqrt{x+1})$ becomes $2\ln x+\tfrac12\ln(x+1)$ — three seconds of algebra saves two minutes of chain rule. Factor after differentiating. The next part of the question is almost always "now set $f'=0$", which is only tractable in factored form.
