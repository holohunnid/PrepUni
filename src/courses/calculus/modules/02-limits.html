02

Limits

where a function is heading, regardless of where it actually lands
READ FIRST The one idea, in plain words— ⊡ ✕

Here is the whole subject in one sentence: the limit of $f$ at $a$ is the value $f$ is heading towards as $x$ gets close to $a$ — and it deliberately ignores what happens at $a$ itself.

That last clause is the strange part, and it is the point. Why would we build a tool that refuses to look at the value we care about? Because the two most important objects in calculus are fractions that are illegal at the point of interest:

  • The derivative is $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ as $h\to0$. At $h=0$ exactly, this is $\tfrac00$ — meaningless. But near $h=0$ it is perfectly well-behaved, and it heads somewhere specific. That somewhere is the derivative.
  • The integral is a sum of rectangles whose widths shrink to zero. At width exactly zero there is nothing to add. Near zero, the total heads somewhere. That somewhere is the integral.

So limits are not a preliminary hurdle before the real material — they are the machine that makes the real material possible. Both of calculus's big ideas are "the value this expression approaches but never reaches".

FIGURE Four functions, four fates at $x=a$— ⊡ ✕

The slider controls $\delta$: how close we let $x$ get to $a=2$ on each side. Watch the two output values in the readout. A limit exists precisely when both sides converge on the same number as $\delta$ shrinks — whether or not the function is even defined at $a$.

The four cases, and what each teaches:

  • hole — $f$ is undefined at $a$, yet the limit exists. Proof that the limit ignores the point. This is the case that matters for derivatives.
  • jump — the two sides head to different numbers, so the limit does not exist, even though $f(a)$ is perfectly well defined.
  • blow-up — the values grow without bound. We write $\lim=+\infty$ as a description of the behaviour, but strictly the limit does not exist (no real number is approached).
  • continuous — the boring, best case: both sides head to $f(a)$ itself. That is exactly the definition of continuity in module 3.

Shrink δ towards 0 and read the two values. This is what "approaches" means operationally — and it is how you should think whenever a formal definition feels abstract.

DEF Notation, one-sided limits, and when a limit fails— ⊡ ✕

We write

$$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$$

to mean: $f(x)$ can be made as close to $L$ as we like, by taking $x$ close enough to $a$ (but not equal to $a$).

The formal version, for completeness — it says the same thing with the vagueness removed. "As close as we like" becomes "for every $\varepsilon>0$", and "close enough" becomes "there is a $\delta>0$":

$$\forall\varepsilon>0\;\exists\delta>0:\;0<|x-a|<\delta\;\Longrightarrow\;|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$$

Read it as a challenge–response game. Someone challenges you with a tolerance $\varepsilon$ ("get within 0.001 of $L$"); you must respond with a distance $\delta$ that guarantees it. If you can always answer, the limit is $L$. Note $0<|x-a|$ — that strict inequality is precisely the clause excluding $x=a$.

One-sided limits

Approaching from the left only ($x<a$) or the right only ($x>a$):

$$\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=L^-\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L^+$$

and the fundamental link between them:

The existence test
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ exists $\iff$ both one-sided limits exist and are equal to $L$.

This is the tool for every piecewise function: compute both sides separately, then compare.

Three ways a limit can fail

  1. The sides disagree (a jump). Classic: $f(x)=|x|/x$ at $0$ gives $-1$ from the left, $+1$ from the right.
  2. Unbounded growth. $1/x^2$ at $0$ heads to $+\infty$; no real number is approached.
  3. Wild oscillation. $\sin(1/x)$ at $0$ swings between $-1$ and $1$ infinitely often, never settling.

Check yourself: for $f(x)=\begin{cases}x+1&x<0\\ x^2&x\ge0\end{cases}$, does $\lim_{x\to0}f(x)$ exist? (Answer: no — left gives 1, right gives 0.)

IDEA 1 The algebra of limits — how you actually compute— ⊡ ✕

Nobody uses $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ to compute limits. You use these rules, which say that limits pass through arithmetic. Assume $\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ and $\lim_{x\to a}g=M$, both finite:

Sum$\lim(f\pm g)=L\pm M$
Scalar$\lim(cf)=cL$
Product$\lim(fg)=LM$
Quotient$\lim(f/g)=L/M$, provided $M\ne0$
Power$\lim f^n=L^n$; $\lim\sqrt[n]{f}=\sqrt[n]{L}$ (root legal)
Composite$\lim g(f(x))=g(L)$ if $g$ is continuous at $L$

The consequence that does 80% of the work

Apply those rules repeatedly to a polynomial and you get: for polynomials and rational functions, just substitute — provided the denominator does not vanish.

$$\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2+1}{x-1}=\frac{9+1}{3-1}=5$$

Substitution also works for $e^x$, $\ln x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $\sqrt{x}$ anywhere they are defined. So the honest description of limit-taking is: try substitution first; only when it produces something illegal do you need a technique.

Trap
$\tfrac00$ does not mean "the limit is 1", or 0, or undefined. It means the test was inconclusive. Compare $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x}=1$, $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x^2}{x}=0$, $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x^2}=\infty$ — all three are $\tfrac00$ on substitution, all three have different answers.

Check yourself: $\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x+1}$. (Answer: substitute — $\frac{0}{2}=0$. Denominator is fine, so no work needed. Don't reflexively factor.)

IDEA 2 Killing the $\tfrac00$: three techniques— ⊡ ✕

When substitution gives $\tfrac00$, the numerator and denominator share a common factor that vanishes at $a$. Your job is to expose and cancel it. There are three standard moves.

1 · Factor and cancel

The default for polynomials. If substituting $x=a$ gives zero on top, then $(x-a)$ is a factor — that is the factor theorem, and it guarantees the technique will work.

$$\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}=\lim_{x\to2}\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=\lim_{x\to2}(x+2)=4$$

The cancellation is legal precisely because $x\ne2$ throughout the limit process — we never divide by zero, we only ignore the single point where we would have.

2 · Multiply by the conjugate

The move whenever a square root causes the trouble. Multiply top and bottom by the same expression with the sign flipped, so that $(\sqrt{A}-B)(\sqrt{A}+B)=A-B^2$ clears the root.

$$\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{(\sqrt{x+4}-2)(\sqrt{x+4}+2)}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)}=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)}=\frac{1}{4}$$

3 · Simplify a compound fraction

Fractions stacked inside fractions: combine the inner ones over a common denominator first, then cancel.

$$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{1}{2+h}-\frac{1}{2}}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\frac{2-(2+h)}{2(2+h)}}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{-h}{2h(2+h)}=-\frac14$$

That last one is not a random exercise: it is exactly the derivative of $1/x$ at $x=2$, computed from the definition. You will meet it again in module 4.

Later shortcut
Module 6 introduces L'Hôpital's rule, which differentiates top and bottom separately and resolves most $\tfrac00$ forms in one line. It is faster — but you must be able to do it by hand too, because exams sometimes ask for the definition-based route, and because L'Hôpital requires the derivatives you haven't met yet.

Check yourself: $\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x^2-x-6}$. (Answer: $\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{(x-3)(x+2)}\to\frac{6}{5}$.)

IDEA 3 Limits at infinity and asymptotes— ⊡ ✕

A different question: not "where does $f$ head as $x$ approaches a point", but "where does $f$ head as $x$ runs off forever". Written $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)$.

The one technique: divide by the dominant power

For a rational function, divide every term top and bottom by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator, then use $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x^k}=0$.

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+5x}{2x^2-1}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3+\frac5x}{2-\frac1{x^2}}=\frac{3+0}{2-0}=\frac32$$

Doing this in general gives a rule you can quote instantly. For $\frac{\text{degree }n}{\text{degree }m}$:

$n<m$limit is $0$ (bottom wins)
$n=m$limit is the ratio of leading coefficients
$n>m$limit is $\pm\infty$ (top wins)

The growth hierarchy

Beyond polynomials, some functions simply outrun others. As $x\to\infty$, each of these beats everything to its left, eventually and decisively:

$$\ln x\;\ll\;x^{p}\;(p>0)\;\ll\;e^{x}\;\ll\;x!$$

Consequences worth memorising: $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x}=0$ and $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^{100}}{e^x}=0$. Exponential growth always wins against any power, however large the power.

Asymptotes — limits made visual

Vertical at $x=a$$\lim_{x\to a^\pm}f=\pm\infty$ — look where the denominator vanishes but the numerator doesn't
Horizontal at $y=L$$\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f=L$
Oblique $y=mx+b$when the numerator's degree is exactly one more than the denominator's; found by polynomial division
Trap
A hole is not a vertical asymptote. In $\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ the denominator vanishes at $x=2$, but so does the numerator — they cancel, leaving a removable hole. Always factor before declaring an asymptote.
Finance link
$\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{n}=e^{r}$ — compounding more and more often converges to continuous compounding rather than exploding. A limit at infinity, and the reason $e$ appears throughout finance.

Check yourself: $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{2x^3-x}{5x^3+7}$. (Answer: equal degrees, so $2/5$.)

TIP The squeeze theorem, and two limits to memorise— ⊡ ✕

Some functions are too erratic to attack directly. The squeeze (sandwich) theorem handles them by trapping the function between two others that you can evaluate:

Squeeze theorem
If $g(x)\le f(x)\le h(x)$ near $a$, and $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}g=\lim_{x\to a}h=L$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ too.

The intuition is exactly the name: if the bread closes in on a point, the filling has nowhere else to go.

The standard application. $\lim_{x\to0}x^2\sin(1/x)$. The factor $\sin(1/x)$ oscillates forever and has no limit — so the product rule is unusable. But $\sin$ is always between $-1$ and $1$, so

$$-x^2\;\le\;x^2\sin(1/x)\;\le\;x^2$$

Both outer functions tend to 0 as $x\to0$, hence the middle does too. The wild oscillation is irrelevant because its amplitude is being crushed.

Two limits you should simply know

These are used constantly and are the basis of the trigonometric derivatives in module 5:

$$\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1\qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x}=0$$

The first says that for small angles $\sin x\approx x$ (in radians — the identity is false in degrees). The second follows from it.

And the two definitions of $e$, both of which you will meet again:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^{n}=e\qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^{x}-1}{x}=1$$

The second is the statement that $e^x$ has slope exactly 1 at the origin — which is why $e$ is the natural base and why $\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x$.

Exam routine for any limit
1. Substitute. 2. If you get a number, stop. 3. If $\tfrac{c}{0}$ with $c\ne0$, it's $\pm\infty$ — check each side's sign. 4. If $\tfrac00$: factor, conjugate, or simplify the compound fraction (later: L'Hôpital). 5. If $x\to\infty$: divide by the dominant power, or quote the growth hierarchy. 6. If it oscillates: squeeze.
