W01●functionsNatural domain with three obstacles
Find the natural domain of $\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{\sqrt{x+2}}{\ln(5-x)}$.
Solution
  1. Square root: $x+2\ge0\Rightarrow x\ge-2$.
  2. Logarithm: $5-x>0\Rightarrow x<5$.
  3. Denominator non-zero: $\ln(5-x)\ne0\Rightarrow 5-x\ne1\Rightarrow x\ne4$.
  4. Intersect: $\;D=[-2,4)\cup(4,5)$.
Insight
Three separate checks, and the third is the one people miss: a logarithm in a denominator must be non-zero as well as defined, and $\ln u=0$ at $u=1$, not at $u=0$. Scan every formula for all three obstacles — division, even roots, logs — before writing an answer.
W02●functionsComposition and inverse
Let $f(x)=2x-1$ and $g(x)=x^2+3$. Find (a) $f(g(x))$, (b) $g(f(x))$, (c) $f^{-1}(x)$.
Solution
  1. (a) Substitute $g$ into $f$: $f(g(x))=2(x^2+3)-1=2x^2+5$.
  2. (b) Substitute $f$ into $g$: $g(f(x))=(2x-1)^2+3=4x^2-4x+4$.
  3. (c) Set $y=2x-1$, solve for $x$: $x=\dfrac{y+1}{2}$. Rename: $f^{-1}(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{2}$.
  4. Check: $f(f^{-1}(x))=2\cdot\frac{x+1}{2}-1=x$ ✓.
Insight
(a) and (b) are different functions — composition does not commute. The habit of verifying an inverse by composing it back costs five seconds and catches every algebra slip.
W03●●functionsA log equation with a rejected root
Solve $\ln x+\ln(x-3)=\ln 4$.
Solution
  1. Combine the left side with the product rule: $\ln\big(x(x-3)\big)=\ln4$.
  2. $\ln$ is one-to-one, so $x(x-3)=4$.
  3. $x^2-3x-4=0\Rightarrow(x-4)(x+1)=0\Rightarrow x=4$ or $x=-1$.
  4. Check the domain. The original equation needs $x>0$ and $x-3>0$, i.e. $x>3$. So $x=-1$ is rejected.
  5. $x=4$. Verify: $\ln4+\ln1=\ln4$ ✓.
Insight
Combining logs enlarges the domain — $\ln(x(x-3))$ is defined for $x<0$ too, where the original was not. Any manipulation that widens a domain can manufacture false roots, so always substitute back into the original equation.
W04●limitsFactor and cancel
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-5x+6}{x^2-9}$.
Solution
  1. Substitute: $\frac{9-15+6}{9-9}=\frac00$ — indeterminate, so work is needed.
  2. Factor both: numerator $(x-2)(x-3)$, denominator $(x-3)(x+3)$.
  3. Cancel the common $(x-3)$ — legal because $x\ne3$ throughout the limit process.
  4. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x-2}{x+3}=\frac{1}{6}$.
Insight
$\tfrac00$ guarantees a shared factor of $(x-a)$ in both, by the factor theorem — so factoring is certain to work on polynomials. The result also tells you the curve has a removable hole at $x=3$, not an asymptote; the genuine vertical asymptote is at $x=-3$, where only the denominator vanishes.
W05●●limitsConjugate
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}{x}$.
Solution
  1. Substitution gives $\tfrac00$. A root is causing it, so multiply by the conjugate.
  2. $\dfrac{(\sqrt{1+x}-1)(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}=\dfrac{(1+x)-1}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}=\dfrac{x}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}$.
  3. Cancel $x$: $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x}+1}$.
  4. Now substitute: $\dfrac{1}{1+1}=\dfrac12$.
Insight
This is the derivative of $\sqrt{1+x}$ at $x=0$ computed from the definition — and indeed $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{1+x}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}}$ equals $\tfrac12$ there. It also justifies the everyday approximation $\sqrt{1+x}\approx1+\tfrac x2$ for small $x$.
W06●●limitsConjugate at infinity
Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2+3x}-x\right)$.
Solution
  1. Form is $\infty-\infty$ — indeterminate, and "the $x^2$ dominates so it's $\infty$" is wrong reasoning.
  2. Multiply and divide by the conjugate: $\dfrac{(x^2+3x)-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+3x}+x}=\dfrac{3x}{\sqrt{x^2+3x}+x}$.
  3. Divide top and bottom by $x$ (positive, since $x\to+\infty$, so $\sqrt{x^2+3x}=x\sqrt{1+3/x}$):
  4. $\dfrac{3}{\sqrt{1+3/x}+1}\longrightarrow\dfrac{3}{1+1}=\dfrac32$.
Insight
The conjugate trick is not only for $\tfrac00$ — it converts $\infty-\infty$ into a quotient you can handle. Geometrically the answer says $\sqrt{x^2+3x}\approx x+\tfrac32$ far out: the curve approaches the oblique asymptote $y=x+\tfrac32$.
W07●●limitsOne-sided limits decide existence
For $f(x)=\dfrac{|x-2|}{x-2}$, find $\lim_{x\to2^-}f$, $\lim_{x\to2^+}f$, and state whether $\lim_{x\to2}f$ exists.
Solution
  1. For $x>2$: $|x-2|=x-2$, so $f(x)=1$. Hence $\lim_{x\to2^+}f=1$.
  2. For $x<2$: $|x-2|=-(x-2)$, so $f(x)=-1$. Hence $\lim_{x\to2^-}f=-1$.
  3. The one-sided limits differ, so $\lim_{x\to2}f(x)$ does not exist.
  4. $f$ has a jump discontinuity at $x=2$ (and is undefined there).
Insight
Whenever an absolute value, a piecewise definition, or a sign change appears, split into cases before taking limits. Note the function is perfectly well-behaved on each side — the failure is entirely about the two sides disagreeing.
W08●●continuityFind the constant
Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f$ is continuous everywhere: $f(x)=\begin{cases}x^2 & x\le1\\ ax+b & 1<x<3\\ 2x & x\ge3\end{cases}$
Solution
  1. Each piece is a polynomial, so only the seams $x=1$ and $x=3$ can break.
  2. At $x=1$: left limit and value $=1^2=1$; right limit $=a+b$. So $a+b=1$.
  3. At $x=3$: left limit $=3a+b$; right limit and value $=2(3)=6$. So $3a+b=6$.
  4. Subtract: $2a=5\Rightarrow a=\tfrac52$, then $b=1-\tfrac52=-\tfrac32$.
  5. Check: at $x=1$, $\tfrac52-\tfrac32=1$ ✓; at $x=3$, $\tfrac{15}{2}-\tfrac32=6$ ✓.
Insight
Two seams give two equations, so two unknowns are exactly determined. The method never changes: at each seam set left limit = right limit = value. Watch which piece owns the boundary point ($\le$ vs $<$) — here $x=1$ belongs to $x^2$ and $x=3$ to $2x$.
W09●●continuityIVT: prove a root exists
Show that $x^3-4x+1=0$ has a solution in the interval $(0,1)$, and narrow it to an interval of width $0.25$.
Solution
  1. $f(x)=x^3-4x+1$ is a polynomial, hence continuous on $[0,1]$ — the IVT hypothesis is satisfied.
  2. $f(0)=1>0$ and $f(1)=1-4+1=-2<0$. The values straddle zero.
  3. By the IVT there is $c\in(0,1)$ with $f(c)=0$.
  4. Bisection. $f(0.5)=0.125-2+1=-0.875<0$, so the root lies in $(0,0.5)$.
  5. $f(0.25)=0.0156-1+1=0.0156>0$, so the root lies in $(0.25,0.5)$ — width $0.25$ ✓.
Insight
The IVT is an existence theorem: it proves a root is there without finding it. Bisection converts that proof into an algorithm — each step halves the interval. Note you must state continuity explicitly; that is where the marks are.
W10●●derivativeFrom first principles
Use the definition of the derivative to find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=\sqrt{2x+1}$.
Solution
  1. $f'(x)=\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sqrt{2(x+h)+1}-\sqrt{2x+1}}{h}$.
  2. Multiply by the conjugate $\sqrt{2x+2h+1}+\sqrt{2x+1}$ over itself:
  3. Numerator becomes $(2x+2h+1)-(2x+1)=2h$.
  4. $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{2h}{h\left(\sqrt{2x+2h+1}+\sqrt{2x+1}\right)}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{2}{\sqrt{2x+2h+1}+\sqrt{2x+1}}$.
  5. Let $h\to0$: $\;f'(x)=\dfrac{2}{2\sqrt{2x+1}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2x+1}}$.
  6. Confirm with the chain rule: $\tfrac12(2x+1)^{-1/2}\cdot2=(2x+1)^{-1/2}$ ✓.
Insight
Every first-principles computation is: expand so the leading terms cancel, divide out the $h$ that must appear, then substitute $h=0$. If nothing cancels, there is an algebra error. Always sanity-check the answer against the rules afterwards.
W11●derivativeTangent line and linear approximation
For $f(x)=\ln x$: (a) find the tangent line at $x=1$; (b) use it to estimate $\ln(1.05)$; (c) is the estimate too high or too low, and why?
Solution
  1. (a) $f(1)=0$ and $f'(x)=1/x$ so $f'(1)=1$. Tangent: $y=0+1(x-1)=x-1$.
  2. (b) $\ln(1.05)\approx1.05-1=0.05$. (True value $0.048790$.)
  3. (c) $f''(x)=-1/x^2<0$, so $\ln$ is concave and lies below its tangents. The estimate is therefore an over-estimate ✓ ($0.05>0.0488$).
Insight
The sign of $f''$ tells you the direction of the approximation error for free: concave ⇒ tangent above ⇒ over-estimate; convex ⇒ under-estimate. This is also the source of $\ln(1+x)\approx x$, which finance uses constantly for small returns.
W12●●rulesThree derivatives, three decisions
Differentiate: (a) $\displaystyle y=\frac{x^3+2x}{\sqrt x}$, (b) $y=x^2\sin x$, (c) $\displaystyle y=\frac{2x+1}{x^2+3}$.
Solution
  1. (a) Simplify first — no quotient rule needed. $y=\dfrac{x^3}{x^{1/2}}+\dfrac{2x}{x^{1/2}}=x^{5/2}+2x^{1/2}$.
    $y'=\tfrac52x^{3/2}+x^{-1/2}=\tfrac52x^{3/2}+\dfrac{1}{\sqrt x}$.
  2. (b) Product rule. $f=x^2$, $g=\sin x$: $\;y'=2x\sin x+x^2\cos x$.
  3. (c) Quotient rule. $f=2x+1$, $g=x^2+3$:
    $y'=\dfrac{2(x^2+3)-(2x+1)(2x)}{(x^2+3)^2}=\dfrac{2x^2+6-4x^2-2x}{(x^2+3)^2}=\dfrac{-2x^2-2x+6}{(x^2+3)^2}$.
Insight
Part (a) is the lesson: a quotient is not automatically a quotient-rule problem. Splitting the fraction and rewriting roots as powers turned a messy derivative into two power-rule terms. Always ask "can I simplify first?" before choosing a rule.
W13●●rulesChain rule, nested twice
Differentiate: (a) $y=e^{\sin(3x)}$, (b) $y=\ln\sqrt{x^2+1}$, (c) $y=(1+\sqrt x)^4$.
Solution
  1. (a) Three layers: $e^\square$, $\sin\square$, $3x$.
    $y'=e^{\sin(3x)}\cdot\cos(3x)\cdot3=3\cos(3x)\,e^{\sin(3x)}$.
  2. (b) Simplify first: $\ln\sqrt{x^2+1}=\tfrac12\ln(x^2+1)$.
    $y'=\tfrac12\cdot\dfrac{2x}{x^2+1}=\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$.
  3. (c) Outer $\square^4$, inner $1+x^{1/2}$.
    $y'=4(1+\sqrt x)^3\cdot\tfrac12x^{-1/2}=\dfrac{2(1+\sqrt x)^3}{\sqrt x}$.
Insight
Peel layers from the outside in, multiplying as you go, and never differentiate the inside inside the outer derivative. Part (b) again shows a log identity beating brute force: two seconds of algebra removed an entire chain-rule layer.
W14●●rulesImplicit differentiation
The curve $x^2+xy+y^2=7$ passes through $(1,2)$. Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ there and the tangent line.
Solution
  1. Check the point: $1+2+4=7$ ✓.
  2. Differentiate both sides w.r.t. $x$, remembering $y=y(x)$ and using the product rule on $xy$:
    $2x+\big(y+xy'\big)+2yy'=0$.
  3. Collect the $y'$ terms: $y'(x+2y)=-(2x+y)$.
  4. $y'=-\dfrac{2x+y}{x+2y}$.
  5. At $(1,2)$: $y'=-\dfrac{2+2}{1+4}=-\dfrac45$.
  6. Tangent: $y-2=-\tfrac45(x-1)$, i.e. $y=-\tfrac45x+\tfrac{14}{5}$.
Insight
The term $xy$ needs the product rule and the chain rule at once — that is the step people drop. The answer depending on both $x$ and $y$ is normal for an implicit derivative; you evaluate it at a point, not simplify it away.
W15●●●rulesLogarithmic differentiation
Differentiate $y=x^{\ln x}$ for $x>0$.
Solution
  1. The variable is in both the base and the exponent, so neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies. Take logs.
  2. $\ln y=\ln x\cdot\ln x=(\ln x)^2$.
  3. Differentiate both sides. Left: $\dfrac{y'}{y}$. Right (chain rule): $2\ln x\cdot\dfrac1x$.
  4. $y'=y\cdot\dfrac{2\ln x}{x}=\dfrac{2\ln x}{x}\,x^{\ln x}$.
  5. Check by the other route: $x^{\ln x}=e^{(\ln x)^2}$, so $y'=e^{(\ln x)^2}\cdot\frac{2\ln x}{x}$ — the same ✓.
Insight
Two reliable signals for logarithmic differentiation: the variable appears in base and exponent, or the expression is a long product/quotient. The alternative — rewrite as $e^{(\text{something})}$ — always works too, and doing both is a free check.
W16●●●using f'Full study of a cubic
Study $f(x)=x^3-6x^2+9x$: intervals of increase and decrease, local extrema, concavity, inflection point. Then sketch.
Solution
  1. Domain $\mathbb{R}$. Intercepts: $f(x)=x(x^2-6x+9)=x(x-3)^2$, so roots at $x=0$ and a double root at $x=3$.
  2. First derivative: $f'=3x^2-12x+9=3(x-1)(x-3)$. Critical points $x=1,3$.
  3. Sign table for $f'$: on $(-\infty,1)$ both factors negative ⇒ $+$; on $(1,3)$ ⇒ $-$; on $(3,\infty)$ ⇒ $+$.
    So $f$ increases on $(-\infty,1)$, decreases on $(1,3)$, increases on $(3,\infty)$.
  4. Classify: $+\to-$ at $x=1$ ⇒ local max, $f(1)=1-6+9=4$. $-\to+$ at $x=3$ ⇒ local min, $f(3)=27-54+27=0$.
  5. Second derivative: $f''=6x-12=6(x-2)$. Negative for $x<2$ (concave), positive for $x>2$ (convex).
  6. Inflection at $x=2$, where the sign changes; $f(2)=8-24+18=2$. Point $(2,2)$.
  7. Ends: $f\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$, $f\to+\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$. Sketch: up to $(1,4)$, down to $(3,0)$, up again, bending changes at $(2,2)$.
Insight
The double root at $x=3$ is visible in the answer: a repeated root means the curve touches the axis without crossing, which is exactly why $x=3$ is also the local minimum. Reading structure out of the factored form before differentiating tells you what to expect.
W17●●using f'Global extrema on a closed interval
Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $f(x)=x^4-8x^2+3$ on $[-1,3]$.
Solution
  1. $f'=4x^3-16x=4x(x-2)(x+2)$. Critical points $x=0,\,2,\,-2$.
  2. Keep only those inside $[-1,3]$: $x=0$ and $x=2$. ($x=-2$ is outside — discard.)
  3. Evaluate at critical points and both endpoints:
    $f(-1)=1-8+3=-4$
    $f(0)=3$
    $f(2)=16-32+3=-13$
    $f(3)=81-72+3=12$
  4. Compare: absolute max $=12$ at $x=3$; absolute min $=-13$ at $x=2$.
Insight
No classification is needed — you simply compare numbers. Two standard errors are avoided here: discarding the out-of-range critical point, and remembering the endpoints (the maximum is an endpoint, and solving $f'=0$ would never have found it).
W18●●using f'Optimisation word problem
A rectangular plot is fenced on three sides, the fourth being a straight river. With 400 m of fencing, what dimensions maximise the enclosed area?
Solution
  1. Variables. Let $x$ = each of the two sides perpendicular to the river, $y$ = the side parallel to it.
  2. Constraint. Only three sides are fenced: $2x+y=400$, so $y=400-2x$.
  3. Objective in one variable. $A=xy=x(400-2x)=400x-2x^2$, on the physical domain $0\le x\le200$.
  4. Optimise. $A'=400-4x=0\Rightarrow x=100$. Then $y=400-200=200$.
  5. Confirm a maximum. $A''=-4<0$ ✓. (Or check endpoints: $A(0)=A(200)=0$.)
  6. Answer: $100\times200$ m, area $20{,}000$ m².
Insight
Every optimisation word problem has the same four steps: name the variables, write the constraint, express the objective in one variable, then differentiate. Note the optimum splits the fence half into the river-parallel side and half into the two perpendicular ones — a pattern that recurs in constrained problems.
W19●●using f'L'Hôpital, twice
Evaluate (a) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^{2x}-1-2x}{x^2}$ and (b) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x\ln x$.
Solution
  1. (a) Substituting gives $\tfrac00$ ✓ so L'Hôpital applies:
    $\displaystyle\lim\frac{2e^{2x}-2}{2x}$ — still $\tfrac00$, so apply again:
    $\displaystyle\lim\frac{4e^{2x}}{2}=\frac42=2$.
  2. (b) The form is $0\cdot(-\infty)$, which L'Hôpital does not accept. Rewrite as a quotient:
    $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x}$ — now $\tfrac{-\infty}{\infty}$ ✓.
  3. Differentiate top and bottom: $\displaystyle\lim\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}=\lim(-x)=0$.
Insight
Re-check the form before each application — (a) needed two passes, and stopping after one would give $\tfrac00$, not an answer. In (b), the choice of which factor to invert matters: putting $x$ downstairs works, putting $\ln x$ downstairs leads nowhere. Cross-check (a) with the Maclaurin series: $e^{2x}=1+2x+2x^2+\dots$, so the numerator is $2x^2+\dots$ and the ratio tends to 2 ✓.
W20●●using f'Taylor approximation
Use a second-order Taylor polynomial about $a=0$ to estimate $\ln(1.1)$, and comment on the error.
Solution
  1. $f(x)=\ln(1+x)$, so $x=0.1$. Derivatives at 0: $f(0)=0$, $f'(x)=\frac{1}{1+x}\Rightarrow f'(0)=1$, $f''(x)=-\frac{1}{(1+x)^2}\Rightarrow f''(0)=-1$.
  2. $P_2(x)=0+1\cdot x+\dfrac{-1}{2!}x^2=x-\dfrac{x^2}{2}$.
  3. $\ln(1.1)\approx0.1-\dfrac{0.01}{2}=0.1-0.005=\mathbf{0.095}$.
  4. True value $0.0953102$; error $\approx0.0003$. The first-order estimate ($0.1$) had error $0.005$ — 17 times worse.
  5. The next term is $+\frac{x^3}{3}=+0.000333$, which accounts for almost all the remaining error ✓.
Insight
Each extra Taylor term multiplies the accuracy by roughly $x$ — which is why these approximations are excellent for small $x$ and useless for large. The alternating signs also tell you the direction: $P_2$ under-estimates here, $P_1$ over-estimates.
W21●●economicsProfit maximisation and elasticity
Demand is $p=100-2q$ and total cost is $C(q)=20q+200$. Find the profit-maximising quantity, the price, the profit, and the price elasticity of demand at the optimum.
Solution
  1. Revenue: $R=pq=(100-2q)q=100q-2q^2$.
  2. Profit: $\pi=R-C=100q-2q^2-20q-200=80q-2q^2-200$.
  3. FOC: $\pi'=80-4q=0\Rightarrow q^*=20$. SOC: $\pi''=-4<0$ ✓ a maximum.
  4. Price: $p=100-40=60$. Profit: $\pi=1600-800-200=600$.
  5. Check MR = MC: $R'=100-4q=20$ at $q=20$, and $C'=20$ ✓.
  6. Elasticity. From $p=100-2q$ we get $q=\frac{100-p}{2}$, so $\frac{dq}{dp}=-\tfrac12$.
    $\varepsilon=\dfrac{dq}{dp}\cdot\dfrac pq=-\tfrac12\cdot\dfrac{60}{20}=-1.5$ — elastic.
Insight
"Marginal revenue equals marginal cost" is just $\pi'=0$ rewritten, and the SOC is module 6's second derivative test. The elasticity being below $-1$ is not a coincidence: a profit-maximising firm always operates on the elastic part of its demand curve, because in the inelastic region raising the price would raise revenue and cut costs simultaneously.
W22●integralsRewrite, then integrate
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\left(2x^3-\sqrt x+\frac3x-e^{2x}\right)dx$.
Solution
  1. Rewrite the root as a power: $\sqrt x=x^{1/2}$. Then integrate term by term (linearity).
  2. $\displaystyle\int2x^3dx=\frac{x^4}{2}$.
  3. $\displaystyle\int x^{1/2}dx=\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}=\frac23x^{3/2}$ — so this term contributes $-\tfrac23x^{3/2}$.
  4. $\displaystyle\int\frac3xdx=3\ln|x|$ — the $n=-1$ exception.
  5. $\displaystyle\int e^{2x}dx=\frac{e^{2x}}{2}$.
  6. Total: $\;\dfrac{x^4}{2}-\dfrac23x^{3/2}+3\ln|x|-\dfrac{e^{2x}}{2}+C$.
Insight
Three separate traps in one problem: the power rule fails at $n=-1$ (log instead), the absolute value in $\ln|x|$, and the $\tfrac12$ from the chain rule in $e^{2x}$. Differentiate the answer to confirm all three.
W23●●integralsNet area vs total area
For $f(x)=x^2-4$ on $[0,3]$: (a) evaluate $\int_0^3f(x)dx$; (b) find the total area between the curve and the $x$-axis.
Solution
  1. (a) $\displaystyle\int_0^3(x^2-4)dx=\left[\frac{x^3}{3}-4x\right]_0^3=(9-12)-0=-3$.
  2. (b) Find the crossing: $x^2-4=0\Rightarrow x=2$ (inside the interval). Split there.
  3. On $[0,2]$: $\displaystyle\int_0^2(x^2-4)dx=\frac83-8=-\frac{16}{3}$, so the area is $\frac{16}{3}$.
  4. On $[2,3]$: $\displaystyle\int_2^3(x^2-4)dx=(9-12)-\left(\frac83-8\right)=-3+\frac{16}{3}=\frac73$.
  5. Total area $=\dfrac{16}{3}+\dfrac73=\dfrac{23}{3}$.
  6. Consistency check: $-\frac{16}{3}+\frac73=-\frac93=-3$ ✓ matches (a).
Insight
"Evaluate the integral" and "find the area" are different questions with different answers ($-3$ vs $\tfrac{23}{3}$). Whenever the integrand changes sign inside the interval, find the roots and split. The net-vs-total check at the end is free and catches arithmetic slips.
W24●●integralsFTC Part 1 with a chain rule
Find $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^2}\ln t\,dt$ for $x>1$, and $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_x^{3}e^{-t^2}dt$.
Solution
  1. First. FTC 1 with a variable upper limit $g(x)=x^2$: the answer is $f(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$.
  2. $=\ln(x^2)\cdot2x=2\ln x\cdot2x=4x\ln x$.
  3. Second. The variable is the lower limit, so flip the integral first: $\displaystyle\int_x^3e^{-t^2}dt=-\int_3^xe^{-t^2}dt$.
  4. Differentiating: $-e^{-x^2}$.
Insight
Neither integrand needed to be integrated — and $e^{-t^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, so it could not have been. FTC Part 1 differentiates an accumulation directly. The minus sign for a variable lower limit is the standard lost mark.
W25●●techniquesSubstitution with changed limits
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^{e}\frac{(\ln x)^2}{x}\,dx$.
Solution
  1. The inner function $\ln x$ appears together with its derivative $1/x$ — substitution.
  2. Let $u=\ln x$, so $du=\dfrac{dx}{x}$.
  3. Change the limits: $x=1\Rightarrow u=\ln1=0$; $x=e\Rightarrow u=\ln e=1$.
  4. $\displaystyle\int_0^1u^2\,du=\left[\frac{u^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac13$.
Insight
Converting the limits avoids substituting back entirely — fewer steps and fewer errors. The tell-tale pattern here is $\frac{g'}{1}\cdot(\text{function of }g)$: whenever you see $\ln x$ next to $\frac1x$, or $x$ next to $x^2$, substitution is the move.
W26●●●techniquesBy parts: LIATE, and a circular case
Evaluate (a) $\displaystyle\int x^2\ln x\,dx$ and (b) $\displaystyle\int e^x\sin x\,dx$.
Solution
  1. (a) LIATE puts L before A, so $u=\ln x$ (not $x^2$), $dv=x^2dx$.
  2. $du=\dfrac{dx}{x}$, $v=\dfrac{x^3}{3}$. Then
    $\displaystyle\int x^2\ln x\,dx=\frac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\int\frac{x^3}{3}\cdot\frac1x dx=\frac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\frac13\int x^2dx$
  3. $=\dfrac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\dfrac{x^3}{9}+C=\dfrac{x^3}{9}\big(3\ln x-1\big)+C$.
  4. (b) The circular trick. Call the answer $I$. Take $u=\sin x$, $dv=e^xdx$:
    $I=e^x\sin x-\displaystyle\int e^x\cos x\,dx$.
  5. Apply by parts again to the new integral, keeping the same choice pattern ($u$ trigonometric):
    $\displaystyle\int e^x\cos x\,dx=e^x\cos x+\int e^x\sin x\,dx=e^x\cos x+I$.
  6. Substitute back: $I=e^x\sin x-e^x\cos x-I$, so $2I=e^x(\sin x-\cos x)$.
  7. $I=\dfrac{e^x(\sin x-\cos x)}{2}+C$.
Insight
In (a), choosing $u=x^2$ would have produced $\int\frac{x^3}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{x}$… no — it would have required $v=$ an antiderivative of $\ln x$, which is harder than the original. LIATE encodes that reasoning. In (b) the original integral reappears, so you solve for it algebraically instead of integrating further — but you must keep the same type in $u$ both times, or everything cancels to $0=0$.
W27●●techniquesPartial fractions
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int\frac{3x+11}{x^2-x-6}\,dx$.
Solution
  1. Degree of numerator (1) < degree of denominator (2) ⇒ proper, no division needed.
  2. Factor: $x^2-x-6=(x-3)(x+2)$.
  3. Template: $\dfrac{3x+11}{(x-3)(x+2)}=\dfrac{A}{x-3}+\dfrac{B}{x+2}$.
  4. Multiply through: $3x+11=A(x+2)+B(x-3)$.
  5. Set $x=3$: $20=5A\Rightarrow A=4$.   Set $x=-2$: $5=-5B\Rightarrow B=-1$.
  6. $\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{4}{x-3}-\frac{1}{x+2}\right)dx=4\ln|x-3|-\ln|x+2|+C$.
  7. Optionally combine: $\ln\left|\dfrac{(x-3)^4}{x+2}\right|+C$.
Insight
Substituting the roots kills one unknown at a time and is far faster than expanding and comparing coefficients. Distinct linear factors always give a sum of logs — if your answer has no logs, check the factorisation.
W28●●techniquesTwo improper integrals
Determine whether each converges, and evaluate if it does: (a) $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$, (b) $\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$.
Solution
  1. Both are improper with $p=\tfrac12$ — but at opposite ends, so the tests point opposite ways.
  2. (a) The integrand blows up at $x=0$. Write the limit:
    $\displaystyle\lim_{t\to0^+}\int_t^1x^{-1/2}dx=\lim_{t\to0^+}\big[2\sqrt x\big]_t^1=\lim_{t\to0^+}(2-2\sqrt t)=2$.
    Converges to 2 — consistent with the $p$-test ($p=\tfrac12<1$ ✓).
  3. (b) Infinite interval:
    $\displaystyle\lim_{b\to\infty}\big[2\sqrt x\big]_1^{b}=\lim_{b\to\infty}(2\sqrt b-2)=\infty$.
    Diverges — consistent with the $p$-test ($p=\tfrac12\not>1$ ✓).
Insight
The same integrand, convergent at one end and divergent at the other. Near zero you need a mild singularity ($p<1$); far out you need fast decay ($p>1$). Always write the limit explicitly — marks are awarded for the setup, and it is the only way to handle the blow-up honestly.
W29●●partialsGradient and directional derivative
For $f(x,y)=x^2y^3-3xy$ at the point $(1,2)$: find $\nabla f$, the maximum rate of increase, and the directional derivative in the direction of $\mathbf{v}=(3,4)$.
Solution
  1. Partials. $f_x=2xy^3-3y$ and $f_y=3x^2y^2-3x$.
  2. At $(1,2)$: $f_x=2(1)(8)-3(2)=16-6=10$; $f_y=3(1)(4)-3(1)=12-3=9$.
  3. $\nabla f(1,2)=(10,\,9)$.
  4. Maximum rate of increase $=\|\nabla f\|=\sqrt{100+81}=\sqrt{181}\approx13.45$, in the direction of $(10,9)$.
  5. Normalise $\mathbf v$: $\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{9+16}=5$, so $\mathbf u=\left(\tfrac35,\tfrac45\right)$.
  6. $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=10\cdot\tfrac35+9\cdot\tfrac45=6+7.2=13.2$.
  7. Sanity check: $13.2<13.45$ ✓ — no direction can beat the gradient's own.
Insight
Normalising $\mathbf v$ is not optional: using $(3,4)$ directly would give $66$, five times too large. The final comparison against $\|\nabla f\|$ is a free check that catches exactly that error.
W30●●●partialsCritical point, Hessian, and MRS
For $f(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2-3x$: (a) find the critical point and classify it using the Hessian; (b) find the slope $dy/dx$ along a level curve of $f$ at the point $(1,1)$.
Solution
  1. (a) First-order conditions. $f_x=2x+y-3=0$ and $f_y=x+2y=0$.
  2. From the second, $x=-2y$. Substitute: $2(-2y)+y=3\Rightarrow-3y=3\Rightarrow y=-1$, so $x=2$.
  3. Critical point $(2,-1)$. Check: $f_y=2+2(-1)=0$ ✓.
  4. Hessian. $f_{xx}=2$, $f_{yy}=2$, $f_{xy}=f_{yx}=1$, so $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.
  5. Definiteness (leading principal minors): $|H_1|=2>0$ and $\det H=4-1=3>0$ ⇒ positive definite ⇒ local (here global) minimum.
  6. (b) Level-curve slope. $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{f_x}{f_y}$. At $(1,1)$: $f_x=2+1-3=0$ and $f_y=1+2=3$.
  7. $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac03=0$ — the level curve through $(1,1)$ is horizontal there.
Insight
Part (a) is where calculus hands the problem to linear algebra: classifying a critical point is testing a quadratic form for definiteness, using the leading-principal-minor test from the linear algebra course. Part (b) shows the formula behaving sensibly — $f_x=0$ means moving in $x$ does not change $f$ to first order, so the contour runs flat.
W31●●●double integralsReverse the order of integration
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1\sin(y^2)\,dy\,dx$.
Solution
  1. The obstacle. $\int\sin(y^2)dy$ has no elementary antiderivative — the inner integral is impossible as written. So reverse the order.
  2. Read the region. $0\le x\le1$ and $x\le y\le1$: the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$ — everything in the unit square above the line $y=x$.
  3. Re-describe with horizontal slabs (Type II). $y$ runs from 0 to 1; for each $y$, $x$ runs from $0$ to $y$.
  4. $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{y}\sin(y^2)\,dx\,dy$.
  5. Inner integral is now trivial — $\sin(y^2)$ is constant in $x$: $\displaystyle\int_0^y\sin(y^2)dx=y\sin(y^2)$.
  6. Outer. Substitute $u=y^2$, $du=2y\,dy$, limits $0\to1$:
    $\displaystyle\int_0^1y\sin(y^2)dy=\tfrac12\int_0^1\sin u\,du=\tfrac12\big[-\cos u\big]_0^1=\dfrac{1-\cos1}{2}$.
  7. Numerically $\approx0.2298$.
Insight
Reversing the order turned an impossible integral into a one-line substitution, and the extra factor $y$ produced by the inner integration is precisely what substitution needed. Whenever you see $\sin(y^2)$, $e^{y^2}$ or $\frac{\sin y}{y}$ in an inner integral, reversal is the intended move — and you must sketch the region to do it, never just swap the limits.
W32●●●financePerpetuity, duration and convexity
(a) A project pays continuously at $\text{€}20{,}000$ per year, growing at 2%, discounted at 6%. Find its present value. (b) A zero-coupon bond pays 100 in $T=10$ years, priced $P(y)=100e^{-yT}$ with $y=5\%$. Find its duration and convexity, then estimate the price change for $\Delta y=+1\%$ using (i) duration only, (ii) duration and convexity, and compare with the exact change.
Solution
  1. (a) $\displaystyle PV=\int_0^{\infty}20000\,e^{0.02t}e^{-0.06t}dt=20000\int_0^{\infty}e^{-0.04t}dt=\frac{20000}{0.04}=\text{€}500{,}000$.
  2. The improper integral converges only because $r>g$; that condition is not an assumption but the requirement for convergence.
  3. (b) Derivatives. $P=100e^{-yT}$, so $P'=-T\cdot100e^{-yT}=-TP$ and $P''=T^2P$.
  4. Duration $D=-P'/P=T=10$. Convexity $C=P''/P=T^2=100$.
  5. Base price: $P(0.05)=100e^{-0.5}=60.653$.
  6. (i) Duration only: $\Delta P\approx-D\,P\,\Delta y=-10(60.653)(0.01)=-6.065$.
  7. (ii) With convexity: add $\tfrac12CP(\Delta y)^2=\tfrac12(100)(60.653)(0.0001)=+0.303$, giving $-5.762$.
  8. Exact: $P(0.06)=100e^{-0.6}=54.881$, so $\Delta P=-5.772$.
  9. Errors: duration alone is off by $0.293$; adding convexity cuts it to $0.010$ — about 30 times better.
Insight
Part (b) is a second-order Taylor expansion with finance vocabulary: duration is the first derivative, convexity the second. Because $P$ is convex, the tangent line lies below the curve, so duration alone always overstates the loss from a yield rise — a systematic bias, not random error. Part (a) shows an improper integral whose convergence condition is the economic condition $r>g$.
