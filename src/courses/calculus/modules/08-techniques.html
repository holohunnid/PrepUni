08

Integration techniques

substitution, by parts, partial fractions, and improper integrals
READ FIRST Why integration is harder than differentiation— ⊡ ✕

Differentiation is an algorithm. Any function built from the standard library can be differentiated by mechanically applying the rules of module 5; there is no cleverness involved, only care.

Integration is not like that. There is no procedure that always works, and worse, most functions have no elementary antiderivative at all. $\int e^{-x^2}dx$ cannot be written in terms of the functions you know — and it is not that nobody has found it, it is that no such expression exists. (That particular integral is the normal distribution's, which is exactly why probability tables exist.)

So integration is a craft: you learn a small set of techniques and how to recognise which one a problem wants. Each technique is a differentiation rule run backwards:

Chain rulereversed gives substitution
Product rulereversed gives integration by parts
Adding fractionsreversed gives partial fractions

Then a fourth topic, improper integrals, which is not a technique but an extension: what to do when the interval is infinite or the integrand blows up. It matters far beyond the exam — every probability density integrates over an infinite range.

Chapter map
Substitution (the one you use most) → definite integrals with substitution → by parts, and how to choose $u$ → partial fractions → improper integrals and the $p$-test.
IDEA 1 Substitution — the chain rule backwards— ⊡ ✕

The chain rule says $\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=F'(g(x))g'(x)$. Read backwards:

$$\int f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du\quad\text{with }u=g(x),\;du=g'(x)\,dx$$

In words: if the integrand contains an inner function together with (a multiple of) its derivative, substitute the inner function. The whole skill is spotting that pattern.

The procedure

  1. Choose $u=$ the inner function — typically what is inside a bracket, root, exponent, or denominator.
  2. Compute $du=g'(x)\,dx$.
  3. Rewrite the entire integral in terms of $u$. If any $x$ survives, the choice was wrong — back up.
  4. Integrate in $u$.
  5. Substitute back to $x$ (indefinite integrals only).
Three substitutions
  1. $\displaystyle\int 2x\,e^{x^2}dx$. Let $u=x^2$, $du=2x\,dx$ — the $2x$ is already present. Integral becomes $\int e^u du=e^u+C=e^{x^2}+C$.
  2. $\displaystyle\int \frac{x}{x^2+1}dx$. Let $u=x^2+1$, $du=2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx=\tfrac12du$. Integral $=\tfrac12\int\frac{du}{u}=\tfrac12\ln|u|+C=\tfrac12\ln(x^2+1)+C$.
  3. $\displaystyle\int (3x+1)^{7}dx$. Let $u=3x+1$, $du=3dx$, so $dx=\tfrac13du$. Integral $=\tfrac13\cdot\frac{u^8}{8}+C=\frac{(3x+1)^8}{24}+C$.
Constants are adjustable, variables are not
If $du$ is off by a constant factor, fix it by multiplying inside and dividing outside — as in examples 2 and 3. If it is off by a factor involving $x$, substitution has failed and you need another method. You may never move a variable across the integral sign.

The pattern worth memorising

$$\int\frac{g'(x)}{g(x)}\,dx=\ln|g(x)|+C$$

"Derivative over the function gives log of the function." It is just $u=g(x)$, but recognising it directly saves time constantly: $\int\tan x\,dx=\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}dx=-\ln|\cos x|+C$ (the minus because $\frac{d}{dx}\cos=-\sin$).

Definite integrals: change the limits

With a definite integral you have a choice, and one option is clearly better:

Change the limits, don't substitute back
$$\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du$$
$\displaystyle\int_0^2 x\,e^{x^2}dx$

$u=x^2$, $du=2x\,dx$. New limits: $x=0\Rightarrow u=0$; $x=2\Rightarrow u=4$.

$$\tfrac12\int_0^4 e^u du=\tfrac12\big[e^u\big]_0^4=\tfrac12(e^4-1)$$
Trap
If you switch to $u$ you must switch the limits too. Writing $\int_0^2 e^u du$ is wrong — those limits are $x$-values being applied to a $u$-integral. Either convert both, or convert back to $x$ before evaluating. Mixing the two is the classic lost mark.

Check yourself: $\int\frac{\ln x}{x}dx$. (Answer: $u=\ln x$, $du=dx/x$, giving $\frac{u^2}{2}+C=\frac{(\ln x)^2}{2}+C$.)

IDEA 2 Integration by parts — the product rule backwards— ⊡ ✕

Start from the product rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$, integrate both sides, and rearrange:

$$\boxed{\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du}$$

It does not evaluate the integral — it trades it for a different one. The technique works when the new integral $\int v\,du$ is easier than the one you started with. Choosing $u$ badly makes it harder, so the choice is the whole game.

Choosing $u$: LIATE

Pick $u$ as the first type that appears in this list; the rest becomes $dv$:

LLogarithmic — $\ln x$
IInverse trig — $\arctan x$
AAlgebraic — $x$, $x^2$
TTrigonometric — $\sin x$, $\cos x$
EExponential — $e^x$

The logic: things early in the list simplify when differentiated ($\ln x\to 1/x$, $x^2\to2x$), while things late in the list stay manageable when integrated ($e^x\to e^x$). So put the first kind in $u$ and the second in $dv$.

1 · $\displaystyle\int x e^x dx$ — algebraic before exponential

$u=x\Rightarrow du=dx$; $\;dv=e^xdx\Rightarrow v=e^x$.

$$\int xe^xdx=xe^x-\int e^xdx=xe^x-e^x+C=e^x(x-1)+C$$

The trade worked: $\int e^xdx$ is trivial where $\int xe^xdx$ was not. Had you chosen $u=e^x$ you would have got $\int \frac{x^2}{2}e^xdx$ — worse.

2 · $\displaystyle\int \ln x\,dx$ — the trick case

There is only one factor, so take $dv=dx$:

$u=\ln x\Rightarrow du=\frac{dx}{x}$; $\;dv=dx\Rightarrow v=x$.

$$\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-\int x\cdot\frac1x dx=x\ln x-x+C$$

Worth memorising outright — $\int\ln x\,dx=x(\ln x-1)+C$ appears constantly.

3 · $\displaystyle\int x^2 e^x dx$ — apply it twice

First pass: $u=x^2$, $dv=e^xdx$ gives $x^2e^x-2\int xe^xdx$.

The remaining integral is example 1: $\int xe^xdx=e^x(x-1)$.

$$\int x^2e^xdx=x^2e^x-2e^x(x-1)+C=e^x(x^2-2x+2)+C$$

Each pass drops the power of $x$ by one. A power $x^n$ against $e^x$ needs $n$ passes.

Definite integrals by parts
$$\int_a^b u\,dv=\Big[uv\Big]_a^b-\int_a^b v\,du$$ Evaluate the bracket at both limits — a very common slip is to apply the limits only to the surviving integral.
IDEA 3 Partial fractions— ⊡ ✕

For a rational function $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$, split it into a sum of simpler fractions that you already know how to integrate. It is the reverse of putting fractions over a common denominator.

The procedure

  1. Check degrees. If $\deg P\ge\deg Q$, do polynomial long division first. Partial fractions only apply to a proper fraction.
  2. Factor $Q$ completely.
  3. Write the template, one term per factor (see the table).
  4. Solve for the constants — multiply through by $Q$ and either substitute convenient roots or compare coefficients.
  5. Integrate each simple piece.
Factor in $Q$Contributes
distinct linear $(x-a)$$\dfrac{A}{x-a}$
repeated $(x-a)^2$$\dfrac{A}{x-a}+\dfrac{B}{(x-a)^2}$
irreducible quadratic$\dfrac{Ax+B}{x^2+px+q}$
$\displaystyle\int\frac{5x-4}{x^2-x-2}\,dx$
  1. Proper fraction ✓. Factor: $x^2-x-2=(x-2)(x+1)$.
  2. Template: $\dfrac{5x-4}{(x-2)(x+1)}=\dfrac{A}{x-2}+\dfrac{B}{x+1}$.
  3. Multiply through: $5x-4=A(x+1)+B(x-2)$.
  4. Set $x=2$: $6=3A\Rightarrow A=2$. Set $x=-1$: $-9=-3B\Rightarrow B=3$. (Choosing the roots makes one term vanish each time — much faster than comparing coefficients.)
  5. $\displaystyle\int\left(\frac{2}{x-2}+\frac{3}{x+1}\right)dx=2\ln|x-2|+3\ln|x+1|+C$.

Every distinct linear factor produces a logarithm — that is the shape of the answer you should expect before starting.

Where it shows up
Partial fractions are how you invert Laplace transforms and how you decompose the pricing of a stream of cash flows into components with different decay rates. In this course, the immediate use is any integral with a factorable quadratic downstairs.

Check yourself: decompose $\frac{1}{x(x+1)}$. (Answer: $\frac1x-\frac{1}{x+1}$ — so the integral is $\ln\left|\frac{x}{x+1}\right|+C$.)

IDEA 4 Improper integrals— ⊡ ✕

The definition of $\int_a^b f$ assumed a finite interval and a bounded $f$. Two situations break that, and both are handled the same way: replace the offending endpoint with a variable, integrate normally, then take a limit.

Type 1 — infinite interval

$$\int_a^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^{b}f(x)\,dx$$

If the limit is a finite number the integral converges; otherwise it diverges. For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$, split at any convenient point and require both halves to converge separately.

Type 2 — unbounded integrand

If $f$ blows up at an endpoint $c$, approach it: $\int_a^c f=\lim_{t\to c^-}\int_a^t f$. If the blow-up is inside the interval, split there first — this is what makes the $\int_{-1}^1 dx/x^2$ trap of module 7 come out correctly (as divergent).

Two integrals, one convergent, one not

$\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^2}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left[-\frac1x\right]_1^{b}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(1-\frac1b\right)=1$ — converges.

$\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\big[\ln x\big]_1^{b}=\lim_{b\to\infty}\ln b=\infty$ — diverges.

Both integrands shrink to zero, yet only one encloses finite area. "The function tends to 0" is not enough; it must tend to zero fast enough.

The $p$-test — memorise both halves
$$\int_1^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^p}\ \text{converges}\iff p>1\qquad\qquad \int_0^{1}\frac{dx}{x^p}\ \text{converges}\iff p<1$$

The two halves are mirror images: far out, you need a fast decay ($p>1$); near zero, you need a mild singularity ($p<1$). The borderline case $p=1$ diverges at both ends — $1/x$ is the exact dividing line in both directions.

Finance link
The present value of a perpetuity paying continuously is $\int_0^{\infty}Ce^{-rt}dt=\frac{C}{r}$ — an infinite stream of payments with a finite value, because the discount factor decays exponentially (far faster than any $1/x^p$). If $r\le0$ the integral diverges and the perpetuity has no finite price. Module 11 develops this.

Check yourself: does $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}dx$ converge? (Answer: yes, $p=1.5>1$; the value is 2.)

FIGURE Convergence, watched— ⊡ ✕

The integrand is $x^{-p}$ on $[1,b]$. Push $b$ out and watch the running total: for $p>1$ it flattens towards a finite ceiling; for $p\le1$ it keeps climbing.

Set $p=1$ and push $b$ to 40: the total is only about 3.7 and still creeping upward. It reaches infinity, but logarithmically slowly — which is why divergence here is impossible to detect by eye and must be settled algebraically.

Compare $p=2$, where the total is visibly pinned below 1, and $p=0.5$, where it climbs like $2\sqrt b$ without hesitation.

Exam routine
Spot that the integral is improper (infinite limit, or integrand undefined somewhere in range) — say so explicitly and write the limit. Compute the antiderivative as usual, evaluate at the variable endpoint, then take the limit. Marks are given for the setup, not just the number.
TIP Which technique? A decision procedure— ⊡ ✕
  1. Simplify first. Expand brackets, split fractions term by term, use log and trig identities. A surprising number of "hard" integrals are easy after two lines of algebra.
  2. Is it in the table? Check module 7's list, including the near-misses like $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$.
  3. Do you see an inner function and its derivative? → substitution. Look especially for $\frac{g'}{g}$, and for anything inside a bracket, root, or exponent.
  4. Is it a product of two unlike types (polynomial × exponential, polynomial × log, polynomial × trig)? → by parts, choosing $u$ by LIATE.
  5. Is it a rational function? → divide if improper, then partial fractions.
  6. Any infinite limit or blow-up? → set it up as a limit, then use one of the above.
Three false rules
There is no product rule for integrals: $\int fg\ne\int f\int g$. There is no quotient rule. And $\int\frac{1}{f(x)}dx\ne\ln|f(x)|$ unless the numerator happens to be exactly $f'$ — $\int\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ is $\arctan x$, not a log.

Integrals worth knowing on sight

$\displaystyle\int\frac{g'}{g}dx$$\ln|g|+C$
$\displaystyle\int e^{ax}dx$$\dfrac{e^{ax}}{a}+C$
$\displaystyle\int (ax+b)^n dx$$\dfrac{(ax+b)^{n+1}}{a(n+1)}+C$
$\displaystyle\int \ln x\,dx$$x\ln x-x+C$
$\displaystyle\int xe^{x}dx$$e^x(x-1)+C$
$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda x}dx$$\dfrac1\lambda$ (for $\lambda>0$)
$\displaystyle\int_0^\infty xe^{-\lambda x}dx$$\dfrac{1}{\lambda^2}$
$\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx$$\sqrt{\pi}$ — no elementary antiderivative, yet a clean value

The last three are the ones the probability course leans on: the exponential distribution's normalisation and mean, and the Gaussian integral behind the normal density's $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$.

And always
Differentiate your answer. Integration is the one topic where checking is as fast as the original computation and catches nearly everything.
