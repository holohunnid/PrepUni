Multiple integrals

accumulating over a region instead of an interval
READ FIRST The same idea, one dimension up— ⊡ ✕

Module 7 built the integral by chopping an interval into strips, multiplying width by height, and adding. The double integral does exactly the same thing over a two-dimensional region: chop it into small rectangles of area $\Delta A=\Delta x\,\Delta y$, multiply each by the value of $f$ there, and add.

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\lim_{\text{mesh}\to0}\sum_i f(x_i^*,y_i^*)\,\Delta A_i$$

If $f\ge0$ this is the volume under the surface $z=f(x,y)$ over the region $R$ — the natural next step after "area under a curve". If $f=1$ it is simply the area of $R$.

And as before, the definition is not how you compute. The computational fact is Fubini's theorem: a double integral equals an iterated integral — do one variable at a time, holding the other fixed, exactly as with partial derivatives. So a double integral is two ordinary integrations in sequence, and everything from modules 7 and 8 applies unchanged.

The genuinely new skill is not integration at all. It is setting up the limits: describing a two-dimensional region correctly so that the inner and outer integrals cover it exactly once. That is where the marks are, and it is what this module drills.

IDEA 1 Over a rectangle: Fubini's theorem— ⊡ ✕

The easiest case is a rectangle $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$, meaning $a\le x\le b$ and $c\le y\le d$ independently.

Fubini's theorem (rectangles)
If $f$ is continuous on $R$, $$\iint_R f\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$ — both orders give the same number.

How to read an iterated integral. Work from the inside out, and match each $d$ with its own limits: in $\int_a^b\int_c^d f\,dy\,dx$ the inner $dy$ belongs to $c,d$ and the outer $dx$ to $a,b$. When integrating the inner one, treat $x$ as a constant — the exact mirror of a partial derivative.

$\displaystyle\int_0^2\!\!\int_1^3 xy^2\,dy\,dx$
  1. Inner ($y$ from 1 to 3, $x$ frozen): $\displaystyle\int_1^3 xy^2dy=x\left[\frac{y^3}{3}\right]_1^3=x\left(9-\tfrac13\right)=\tfrac{26}{3}x$.
  2. Outer ($x$ from 0 to 2): $\displaystyle\int_0^2\tfrac{26}{3}x\,dx=\tfrac{26}{3}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^2=\tfrac{26}{3}\cdot2=\tfrac{52}{3}$.

Check the other order: $\int_1^3\int_0^2 xy^2dxdy=\int_1^3 2y^2dy=\tfrac{2}{3}(27-1)=\tfrac{52}{3}$ ✓.

The separable shortcut
If the integrand factors as $f(x,y)=g(x)h(y)$ and the region is a rectangle with constant limits, the double integral splits into a product: $$\iint_R g(x)h(y)\,dA=\left(\int_a^b g\,dx\right)\left(\int_c^d h\,dy\right)$$ The example above is $\left(\int_0^2x\,dx\right)\left(\int_1^3y^2dy\right)=2\cdot\tfrac{26}{3}$ — one line. Both conditions are needed; the shortcut fails the moment a limit involves the other variable.

Check yourself: $\int_0^1\int_0^1 e^{x+y}dydx$. (Answer: separable as $e^xe^y$, giving $(e-1)^2$.)

IDEA 2 General regions: Type I and Type II— ⊡ ✕

Most regions are not rectangles, and then the inner limits become functions of the outer variable. There are two ways to describe a region, and choosing well can be the difference between a two-line integral and an impossible one.

Type I — vertical slabs

Describe the region as: $x$ runs between constants, and for each $x$, $y$ runs between two curves.

$$R=\{(x,y): a\le x\le b,\;g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\}\;\Longrightarrow\;\iint_R f\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f\,dy\,dx$$

Type II — horizontal slabs

Or: $y$ runs between constants, and for each $y$, $x$ runs between two curves.

$$R=\{(x,y): c\le y\le d,\;h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)\}\;\Longrightarrow\;\iint_R f\,dA=\int_c^d\!\!\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)}f\,dx\,dy$$
The rule that prevents nonsense
The outer limits are always constants. The inner limits may involve the outer variable, never the other way round. If your inner limits are constants and your outer ones are not, you have written the integral upside down.

The setup procedure

  1. Sketch the region. Non-negotiable — the limits are read off the picture, and nobody gets them right from the algebra alone.
  2. Find the intersection points of the bounding curves; these give the constant limits.
  3. Choose a direction. Draw an arrow through the region: vertical for Type I, horizontal for Type II.
  4. Read off where the arrow enters and leaves — those are the inner limits, as functions of the other variable.
  5. If the arrow enters or leaves through different curves in different parts of the region, split the integral, or use the other type.

Check yourself: for the triangle with vertices $(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)$, give the Type I description. (Answer: $0\le x\le1$, $0\le y\le x$.)

FIGURE One region, two descriptions— ⊡ ✕

The region is bounded by $y=x^2$ and $y=2x$, meeting at $(0,0)$ and $(2,4)$. Toggle the slab direction and read the limits the picture dictates. The area is the same either way — but for a given integrand, one order can be easy and the other impossible.

Both give the area $\tfrac43$:

Type I — a vertical arrow enters at $y=x^2$ and leaves at $y=2x$, for $x$ from 0 to 2:

$$\int_0^2\!\!\int_{x^2}^{2x}dy\,dx=\int_0^2(2x-x^2)dx=\left[x^2-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\tfrac43$$

Type II — a horizontal arrow enters at $x=y/2$ and leaves at $x=\sqrt y$, for $y$ from 0 to 4:

$$\int_0^4\!\!\int_{y/2}^{\sqrt y}dx\,dy=\int_0^4\left(\sqrt y-\tfrac y2\right)dy=\left[\tfrac23y^{3/2}-\tfrac{y^2}{4}\right]_0^4=\tfrac{16}{3}-4=\tfrac43$$

Notice how the boundary curves had to be re-solved for the other variable: $y=x^2$ becomes $x=\sqrt y$, and $y=2x$ becomes $x=y/2$. That inversion is the mechanical part of switching type.

IDEA 3 Reversing the order of integration— ⊡ ✕

Sometimes an integral in the given order is impossible, and in the other order it is easy. Since Fubini says both compute the same number, you are free to swap — but you must go through the region to do it. You cannot simply exchange the limits.

Procedure
1. Read the given limits and sketch the region they describe. 2. Re-describe that same region with the other slab direction. 3. Write the new integral. 4. Compute.
$\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx$

The obstacle. $\int e^{y^2}dy$ has no elementary antiderivative — the inner integral cannot be done as written. So reverse.

  1. Read the region: $0\le x\le1$ and $x\le y\le1$. That is the triangle above the line $y=x$, inside the unit square — vertices $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$.
  2. Re-describe with horizontal slabs: $y$ runs 0 to 1, and for each $y$, $x$ runs from $0$ to $y$.
  3. Rewrite: $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^y e^{y^2}dx\,dy$.
  4. Inner integral is now trivial ($e^{y^2}$ is constant in $x$): $\displaystyle\int_0^y e^{y^2}dx=y\,e^{y^2}$.
  5. Outer: substitute $u=y^2$, $du=2y\,dy$: $\displaystyle\int_0^1 ye^{y^2}dy=\tfrac12\big[e^{y^2}\big]_0^1=\tfrac{e-1}{2}$.

The reversal converted an impossible integral into a one-line substitution. Whenever you meet $e^{y^2}$, $\sin(y^2)$, $\frac{\sin y}{y}$ or similar in an inner integral, reversing the order is almost certainly the intended move.

Trap
Swapping to $\int_x^1\int_0^1 dx\,dy$ is meaningless: the outer limits now contain $x$, the very variable being integrated away. Outer limits must be constants. If yours are not, you skipped the sketch.
IDEA 4 Area, average value, and polar coordinates— ⊡ ✕

Area and average value

Area of $R$$\displaystyle\iint_R 1\,dA$
Volume under $z=f$$\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA$ (for $f\ge0$)
Average value$\displaystyle\bar f=\frac{1}{\text{area}(R)}\iint_R f\,dA$
Between two surfaces$\displaystyle\iint_R (f_{\text{top}}-f_{\text{bot}})\,dA$

Polar coordinates

When the region is a disc, an annulus or a sector — or when the integrand contains $x^2+y^2$ — Cartesian limits are horrible and polar ones are trivial. Substitute

$$x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad x^2+y^2=r^2$$
The area element
$$dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$$ — the factor $r$ is not optional. A polar "rectangle" of sides $dr$ and $d\theta$ has area $r\,dr\,d\theta$, because an angular step $d\theta$ sweeps a longer arc the further out you are.

Forgetting that $r$ is the single most common error in polar integration. If your answer has the wrong power of the radius, that is why.

$\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2\le4}(x^2+y^2)\,dA$

The disc of radius 2 becomes $0\le r\le2$, $0\le\theta\le2\pi$ — constant limits.

$$\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2 r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2 d\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}4\,d\theta=8\pi$$

In Cartesian coordinates the same integral needs limits $\pm\sqrt{4-x^2}$ and a good deal of work.

The famous one
Polar coordinates are how $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx=\sqrt\pi$ is proved: square it to make a double integral over the whole plane, switch to polar, and the stubborn $r$ from $dA$ is exactly the factor that makes $\int re^{-r^2}dr$ a routine substitution. That result is what normalises the normal distribution — the trick that makes probability's most important density integrate to 1.

Check yourself: what is $\iint_R dA$ over the unit disc in polar form? (Answer: $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\tfrac12=\pi$ ✓.)

TIP Triple integrals, and why any of this matters for probability— ⊡ ✕

Triple integrals

Nothing conceptually new: one more nesting level.

$$\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV=\int_a^b\!\!\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}\!\!\int_{h_1(x,y)}^{h_2(x,y)}f\,dz\,dy\,dx$$

The same rule governs the limits: innermost may depend on everything outside it; outermost must be constants. With $f=1$ you get the volume of $E$. There are six possible orders, and as in two dimensions, choosing the right one is the whole difficulty.

The syllabus asks for "multiple integrals" rather than a deep treatment of three dimensions, so the double-integral skills above are the priority. If a triple integral appears, set it up carefully and expect the integrand to be simple.

Sanity checks worth doing

  • Sign. If $f\ge0$ on $R$, the answer must be $\ge0$. A negative result means a limits error.
  • Magnitude. The answer lies between $(\min f)\cdot\text{area}$ and $(\max f)\cdot\text{area}$.
  • Both orders. On a rectangle, computing the other order is a complete check.
  • Units of the limits. After the inner integration, the inner variable must be gone. If a $y$ survives into the outer $x$-integral, you have made a slip.

Where this lands in probability

This module is the machinery behind the joint-distribution material of the probability course. Every formula there is a double integral, and now you can actually compute them:

Normalisation$\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} f(x,y)\,dA=1$
Probability of a region$\displaystyle P\big((X,Y)\in R\big)=\iint_R f\,dA$
Marginal density$\displaystyle f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy$
Expectation$\displaystyle E[g(X,Y)]=\iint g(x,y)f(x,y)\,dA$
Independence$f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$ — the separable case of Idea 1

Two things transfer directly. A marginal density is "integrate out the other variable", which is exactly an inner integral. And independence is exactly the separability condition that let a double integral split into a product — which is why independent random variables are so much easier to work with, and why the shortcut fails the moment the region of support couples $x$ and $y$.

The classic joint-density trap
If the support is a region like $0<x<y<1$, then $X$ and $Y$ are not independent even if the formula for $f(x,y)$ looks separable — because the limits couple them. Knowing $Y=0.3$ restricts $X$ to $(0,0.3)$. In the language of this module: it is not a rectangle, so the separable shortcut does not apply. Sketch the support region before doing anything.
Finance link
The probability that two correlated assets both fall below their thresholds is a double integral of the bivariate normal density over a quadrant. It has no closed form, which is why such joint tail probabilities are computed numerically — but the setup is precisely the Type I / Type II reasoning of this module.
