One habit is worth adopting before the details. In finance, a derivative is almost never asked for as "the derivative": it is asked for as "how much does this move if that moves a little?" Every risk measure in the industry — duration, delta, gamma, vega, beta — is a partial derivative wearing a different name. Once you see that, the vocabulary stops being a list to memorise.

IDEA 1 Compounding, $e$, and log returns— ⊡ ✕

Invest $P$ at annual rate $r$, compounded $n$ times a year, for $t$ years:

$$A=P\left(1+\frac rn\right)^{nt}$$

Let $n\to\infty$ — compound continuously — and the limit from module 2 gives

$$\boxed{A=Pe^{rt}}$$

The derivation is worth following once, because it is a clean use of the tools. Take logs: $\ln A=\ln P+nt\ln(1+\tfrac rn)$. As $n\to\infty$ this is $\infty\cdot0$; rewrite it as a fraction and apply L'Hôpital (module 6) to get $rt$. Exponentiate. Done.

The intuition matters as much as the formula: more frequent compounding does not grow without bound. It converges. Daily compounding at 10% gives a factor of 1.10516; continuous gives $e^{0.1}=1.10517$. The difference between "daily" and "instantaneous" is a rounding error, which is why continuous compounding is the standard modelling convention — it is both the limit and the simplest algebra.

Why continuous rates are the convenient ones

Continuous compounding satisfies $\frac{dA}{dt}=rA$: growth proportional to size. That differential equation is the definition of exponential growth, and $r$ is recoverable as

$$r=\frac{A'(t)}{A(t)}=\frac{d}{dt}\ln A(t)$$

i.e. the derivative of the log is the growth rate. This is why finance works in logs: a log difference is a return, and log returns simply add across periods where simple returns must be multiplied.

Simple return$R=\dfrac{P_1-P_0}{P_0}$
Log return$r=\ln\dfrac{P_1}{P_0}=\ln(1+R)$
Over $T$ periods$r_{\text{total}}=r_1+r_2+\dots+r_T$ — additive

And from module 6's Taylor series, $\ln(1+R)\approx R-\frac{R^2}{2}$: for small returns the two measures nearly coincide (a 2% return is a 1.980% log return), while for large ones they diverge — a $+50\%$ return is only a $+40.5\%$ log return.

Trap
Log returns are additive over time but not across assets in a portfolio. A portfolio's simple return is the weighted average of the assets' simple returns; that is false for log returns, by Jensen's inequality (module 6's concavity). Mixing the two is a classic error.

Check yourself: what continuously-compounded rate matches 6% compounded annually? (Answer: $r=\ln 1.06=5.83\%$.)

IDEA 2 Present value, annuities and perpetuities— ⊡ ✕

A payment $C$ at time $t$ is worth $Ce^{-rt}$ today. Discounting is the inverse of compounding, and $e^{-rt}$ is the discount factor.

For discrete cash flows, present value is a sum:

$$PV=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{C_t}{(1+r)^t}\qquad\text{or}\qquad PV=\sum_t C_te^{-rt}$$

For a continuous stream paying at rate $C(t)$ per unit time, the sum becomes an integral — exactly the module 7 construction, with $C(t)e^{-rt}dt$ as the contribution of the sliver $[t,t+dt]$:

$$PV=\int_0^T C(t)\,e^{-rt}\,dt$$
Constant stream over $T$ years
$$PV=\int_0^T Ce^{-rt}dt=C\left[\frac{e^{-rt}}{-r}\right]_0^T=\frac{C}{r}\left(1-e^{-rT}\right)$$

The continuous-time annuity formula, in two lines of module 7.

Perpetuity — an improper integral

Let $T\to\infty$ (module 8's Type 1 improper integral):

$$PV=\int_0^{\infty}Ce^{-rt}dt=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac Cr\left(1-e^{-rT}\right)=\frac Cr$$

An infinite stream of payments with a finite value — because $e^{-rt}$ decays fast enough for the integral to converge. If $r\le0$ the integral diverges and the perpetuity has no finite price, which is the mathematical statement of "you cannot discount at a non-positive rate forever".

Growing perpetuity (Gordon growth)

Payments growing at rate $g$: $C(t)=Ce^{gt}$.

$$PV=\int_0^{\infty}Ce^{gt}e^{-rt}dt=C\int_0^{\infty}e^{-(r-g)t}dt=\frac{C}{r-g}\quad\text{for }r>g$$

The famous dividend-discount formula, and the convergence condition $r>g$ is now visible as exactly the condition for the improper integral to converge — not an arbitrary assumption but a mathematical necessity.

NPV and the IRR

$NPV(r)=-C_0+\sum_t C_te^{-rt}$. The internal rate of return is the $r$ solving $NPV(r)=0$. Since $NPV$ is continuous and (for a conventional project) strictly decreasing in $r$, the Intermediate Value Theorem of module 3 guarantees a unique root once you find one rate giving a positive NPV and another giving a negative one — and bisection then finds it numerically.

Check yourself: a perpetuity pays €50k/year continuously, $r=4\%$. Value? (Answer: $50{,}000/0.04=$ €1.25m.)

FIGURE Duration and convexity: a Taylor expansion you can trade on— ⊡ ✕

A bond's price is a decreasing, convex function of its yield. Traders do not re-price the bond for every yield move — they use the Taylor expansion of module 6, truncated after one or two terms. Move the yield shock and watch how far each approximation can be trusted.

The mathematics is one Taylor expansion:

$$\Delta P\approx \underbrace{P'(y)\Delta y}_{\text{duration}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12P''(y)(\Delta y)^2}_{\text{convexity}}$$

Divide by $P$ and give the two coefficients their industry names:

Modified duration$D=-\dfrac{P'(y)}{P(y)}$ — % price fall per 1 unit rise in yield
Convexity$C=\dfrac{P''(y)}{P(y)}$
Combined$\dfrac{\Delta P}{P}\approx-D\,\Delta y+\tfrac12C(\Delta y)^2$
  • Small shock: the tangent line is excellent — the red and blue curves are indistinguishable.
  • Big shock: the tangent under-estimates the price (it lies below the convex curve), so duration alone overstates losses and understates gains. Adding the convexity term nearly closes the gap.
  • Long bond: both duration and convexity grow with maturity — long bonds are far more rate-sensitive, and the linear approximation degrades sooner.
Why convexity is desirable
Because $P$ is convex ($P''>0$, module 6), a bond gains more from a yield fall than it loses from an equal-sized rise. That asymmetry is a genuine benefit, and it is priced. It is also the second-order term of a Taylor series — nothing more.
IDEA 3 Marginal analysis and elasticity— ⊡ ✕

Marginal quantities are derivatives

In economics, "marginal X" means "the derivative of total X". The definition of the derivative is precisely "the effect of one more unit, in the limit".

Marginal cost$MC=C'(q)$
Marginal revenue$MR=R'(q)$
Marginal utility$MU_x=\partial U/\partial x$
Marginal product of labour$MP_L=\partial F/\partial L$
Profit maximisation

$\pi(q)=R(q)-C(q)$. The first-order condition $\pi'=0$ gives

$$R'(q)=C'(q)\qquad\text{— marginal revenue equals marginal cost}$$

and the second-order condition $\pi''<0$, i.e. $R''<C''$, confirms a maximum rather than a minimum. The central result of microeconomics is module 6's second derivative test.

Concretely: $R(q)=100q-q^2$, $C(q)=20q+100$. Then $\pi'=100-2q-20=0\Rightarrow q^*=40$, and $\pi''=-2<0$ ✓ a maximum. Profit $=\pi(40)=2400-900-100$… evaluate: $R(40)=4000-1600=2400$, $C(40)=800+100=900$, so $\pi=1500$.

Elasticity: the unit-free derivative

A derivative has units, which makes cross-country or cross-product comparison meaningless. Elasticity fixes that by measuring percentage against percentage:

$$\varepsilon=\frac{\%\Delta f}{\%\Delta x}=\frac{df/f}{dx/x}=f'(x)\cdot\frac{x}{f(x)}=\frac{d\ln f}{d\ln x}$$

The last form is the most useful: elasticity is the derivative of log with respect to log. It also explains why economists run regressions on logged variables — the coefficients are read directly as elasticities.

Constant-elasticity demand

$Q=Ap^{-2}$. Then $\ln Q=\ln A-2\ln p$, so $\varepsilon=\frac{d\ln Q}{d\ln p}=-2$ at every price.

Any power function has constant elasticity equal to its exponent — which is why Cobb–Douglas functions $F=AK^\alpha L^\beta$ are ubiquitous: $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the elasticities of output with respect to capital and labour.

Check yourself: demand $Q=100-2p$ at $p=10$. Elasticity? (Answer: $Q=80$, $Q'=-2$, $\varepsilon=-2\cdot\frac{10}{80}=-0.25$ — inelastic.)

IDEA 4 Multivariable sensitivities, and integrals in probability— ⊡ ✕

The Greeks are partial derivatives

An option price $V(S,\sigma,r,t)$ depends on several inputs, so its sensitivities are the partials of module 9:

Delta$\Delta=\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial S}$ — sensitivity to the underlying
Gamma$\Gamma=\dfrac{\partial^2V}{\partial S^2}$ — how delta itself moves
Vega$\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial\sigma}$ — sensitivity to volatility
Theta$\dfrac{\partial V}{\partial t}$ — time decay

And the total differential of module 9 is exactly how a desk decomposes a day's P&L:

$$dV\approx \Delta\,dS+\tfrac12\Gamma\,(dS)^2+\text{vega}\,d\sigma+\Theta\,dt$$

Delta–gamma is the same duration–convexity idea in a different market: a first-order term plus a curvature correction. Note that the $(dS)^2$ term is retained even though it is second-order — for a volatile underlying it is not negligible, and taking it seriously is the step that leads to Itô calculus.

Integrals as expectations

Every expectation in the probability course is an integral from modules 7–8:

Total probability$\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)dx=1$
Mean$\displaystyle\mu=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xf(x)dx$
Variance$\displaystyle\sigma^2=\int (x-\mu)^2f(x)dx$
Tail / VaR$\displaystyle P(X\le q)=\int_{-\infty}^{q}f(x)dx=\alpha$
Expected shortfall$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{q}xf(x)dx$

All are improper integrals, and all converge because densities decay fast enough — the $p$-test intuition of module 8. And the FTC (module 7) supplies the link every probability course uses without comment: $F'(x)=f(x)$, the density is the derivative of the CDF, because $F$ is precisely an accumulation function.

Exponential lifetime

$f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ on $[0,\infty)$. Check it integrates to 1: $\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\big[-e^{-\lambda t}\big]_0^\infty=1$ ✓.

Mean: $\int_0^\infty t\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac1\lambda$ — by parts (module 8), with $u=t$, $dv=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}dt$.

Exactly the "integrals worth knowing on sight" from module 8, now with an interpretation: the mean waiting time is the reciprocal of the arrival rate.

One picture to carry away
Discounting and probability densities are the same integral with different weights. A present value is $\int C(t)e^{-rt}dt$ — cash flows weighted by a discount factor. An expectation is $\int x f(x)dx$ — outcomes weighted by a density. Pricing under a risk-neutral measure combines both: $\int$ payoff $\times$ density $\times$ discount factor. All the calculus you need for it is in modules 7, 8 and 10.
