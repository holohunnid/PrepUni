Continuity

functions you can draw without lifting the pen — and the theorems that buys you
READ FIRST Why continuity is worth naming— ⊡ ✕

Module 2 built a tool for asking "where is $f$ heading near $a$?", carefully ignoring $f(a)$. Continuity is the name for the pleasant situation where the answer is the obvious one: $f$ heads exactly to where it actually is.

Informally: a function is continuous if you can draw its graph without lifting your pen. No holes, no jumps, no vertical explosions. That informal picture is reliable — but the formal definition is what you use in proofs and in exam questions of the form "find $k$ such that $f$ is continuous".

Two reasons this matters beyond the definition itself:

  • Continuity licenses substitution. Every time you compute a limit by plugging in, you are silently using continuity. Naming the property tells you exactly when that shortcut is legal.
  • Continuity unlocks two big theorems. The Intermediate Value Theorem (a continuous function hits every value in between) and the Extreme Value Theorem (a continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains a maximum and a minimum). The second is the foundation of every optimisation problem you will ever solve — it is what guarantees the maximum you're hunting for actually exists.

DEF The three-part definition— ⊡ ✕

$f$ is continuous at $a$ if

$$\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$$

Compact, but it quietly asserts three separate things, and an exam question can break any one of them. Check them in order:

  1. $f(a)$ exists — $a$ is in the domain. (Fails for $1/x$ at $0$.)
  2. $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists — both one-sided limits exist and agree. (Fails at a jump.)
  3. They are equal — the destination matches the arrival. (Fails when a point has been redefined out of place.)

Equivalently, in one line: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)$. That three-way equation is the form to write down when solving for an unknown constant.

On an interval

$f$ is continuous on an open interval if it is continuous at every point of it. On a closed interval $[a,b]$, the endpoints only get a one-sided requirement — at $a$ we ask $\lim_{x\to a^+}f=f(a)$, since there is no left side to approach from.

Where discontinuity is real
Not every quantity in finance is continuous, and that is economically meaningful. A bond's price jumps on the coupon date; a stock's price gaps at an earnings announcement. Modelling those with continuous functions is exactly the wrong choice — the discontinuity is the event.

Check yourself: $f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ with $f(1)=5$. Continuous at 1? (Answer: no. The limit is 2, but $f(1)=5$ — part 3 fails. Redefining $f(1)=2$ would fix it.)

IDEA 1 The three kinds of discontinuity— ⊡ ✕

Classifying a break tells you whether it can be repaired, which is exactly what "removable" means.

TypeWhat happensExample
Removablethe limit exists, but $f(a)$ is missing or wrong$\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ at $2$
Jumpboth one-sided limits exist but differ$\frac{|x|}{x}$ at $0$
Infiniteat least one side runs to $\pm\infty$$\frac1x$ at $0$

Removable earns its name honestly: define (or redefine) $f(a)$ to equal the limit and the function becomes continuous. Nothing is really broken — a single point was mislabelled. This is why $\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ and $x+2$ behave identically in every limit computation.

Jump and infinite discontinuities cannot be repaired by changing one value. No choice of $f(0)$ makes $|x|/x$ continuous: whatever you pick, one side disagrees.

Check yourself: classify $f(x)=\frac{\sin x}{x}$ at $x=0$. (Answer: removable — the limit is 1, the function is undefined there. Setting $f(0)=1$ makes it continuous everywhere.)

FIGURE Solve for the constant that makes a piecewise function continuous— ⊡ ✕

This is the most common exam form of the definition. Given

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} x^2+1, & x\le 1\\[2pt] kx+3, & x>1\end{cases}$$

find $k$ so that $f$ is continuous at $x=1$. Slide $k$ until the gap closes, then read off the algebra that predicts it.

The method, which never changes:

  1. Left limit. $\lim_{x\to1^-}(x^2+1)=2$.
  2. Value at the point. $f(1)=1^2+1=2$ (the $\le$ puts $x=1$ in the first piece).
  3. Right limit. $\lim_{x\to1^+}(kx+3)=k+3$.
  4. Set them equal. $k+3=2\Rightarrow \boxed{k=-1}$.
Trap
Check which piece owns the boundary point. Here $x\le1$ means $f(1)$ comes from $x^2+1$. If the definition had said $x<1$ and $x\ge1$, then $f(1)=k+3$ instead — the equation is the same, but sloppy reading loses the mark on a different part.

Away from $x=1$ there is nothing to check: both pieces are polynomials, continuous everywhere. Only the seam can break.

TIP Which functions are continuous? (almost all of them)— ⊡ ✕

Rather than checking the definition each time, learn the catalogue. The following are continuous everywhere on their natural domains:

Polynomialscontinuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$
Rational $p/q$continuous wherever $q(x)\ne0$
$e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$
$\ln x$continuous on $(0,\infty)$
$\sqrt[n]{x}$continuous on its domain
$|x|$continuous everywhere (though not differentiable at 0 — see module 4)

Continuity survives combination

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$, then so are

$$f\pm g,\qquad fg,\qquad \frac fg\ (\text{if }g(a)\ne0),\qquad f\circ g\ (\text{composite})$$

The composite rule is the useful one: $e^{\sin(x^2)}$ is continuous everywhere because it is built by composing continuous pieces. You never need to touch $\varepsilon$–$\delta$.

Practical consequence
Anything written with one formula out of the standard library is continuous on its domain. In practice, only two things create discontinuities in exam problems: a denominator hitting zero, and a piecewise seam. Check those two, and you are done.

Check yourself: where is $f(x)=\frac{e^x}{x^2-9}$ discontinuous? (Answer: at $x=\pm3$ only — infinite discontinuities where the denominator vanishes.)

IDEA 2 The two theorems continuity buys you— ⊡ ✕

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

IVT
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $N$ is any value strictly between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then there exists at least one $c\in(a,b)$ with $f(c)=N$.

In words: an unbroken curve that starts below a level and ends above it must cross that level. Obvious from the picture — and false without continuity, since a jump can leap right over $N$.

What it is used for: proving a root exists without finding it. To show $x^3+x-1=0$ has a solution in $[0,1]$: $f$ is a polynomial (continuous), $f(0)=-1<0$ and $f(1)=1>0$, so by IVT some $c\in(0,1)$ has $f(c)=0$. Note the theorem says a root exists; it does not tell you where. Repeatedly halving the interval (bisection) turns this into an actual numerical method.

Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)

EVT
If $f$ is continuous on a closed, bounded interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[a,b]$.

Both hypotheses are essential, and each fails instructively:

  • Drop closed: $f(x)=x$ on $(0,1)$ has no maximum — values approach 1 but never reach it.
  • Drop bounded: $f(x)=x$ on $[0,\infty)$ has no maximum.
  • Drop continuity: $f(x)=1/x$ on $(0,1]$ is unbounded above.
Why you should care
EVT is the licence for the whole of optimisation. When you solve $f'(x)=0$ and compare candidate values, you are assuming a maximum exists to be found. On a closed bounded interval with a continuous $f$, EVT guarantees it. Module 6 turns this into the closed-interval method; the Optimization course extends it to several variables.

Check yourself: does $f(x)=\tan x$ attain a maximum on $[0,\pi]$? (Answer: no — it is not continuous there; it blows up at $\pi/2$, so EVT does not apply.)
