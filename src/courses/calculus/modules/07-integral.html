07

The integral & the Fundamental Theorem

accumulation, area, and why it is the reverse of differentiation
READ FIRST Two problems that turn out to be one— ⊡ ✕

Integration is introduced in two completely different ways, and the relationship between them is the single deepest fact in elementary calculus. Keep them separate in your head at first:

  • The area problem (definite integral). Given a curve, what is the area under it between $a$ and $b$? Defined as a limit of sums of thin rectangles. Answer: a number.
  • The anti-derivative problem (indefinite integral). Given $f$, which function has $f$ as its derivative? Answer: a family of functions, differing by a constant.

These have nothing obviously to do with each other. One is about geometry and limits of sums; the other is about undoing differentiation. Yet the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says they are two faces of the same thing: to compute an area, find an antiderivative and subtract its values at the endpoints. No limits of sums required.

That theorem is why integration is practical at all. Computing $\int_0^1 x^2dx$ from the definition means summing $n$ rectangles and taking $n\to\infty$ — painful. Via the FTC it is $\left[\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\tfrac13$, a five-second computation.

The broader reading, worth carrying into probability and finance: the derivative takes a total and gives you a rate; the integral takes a rate and gives you a total. Speed integrates to distance; a marginal cost integrates to total cost; a probability density integrates to a probability.

DEF Antiderivatives and the indefinite integral— ⊡ ✕

$F$ is an antiderivative (or primitive) of $f$ if $F'(x)=f(x)$.

Antiderivatives are never unique. If $F$ works, so does $F+7$, since the derivative of a constant is zero. And that is the only freedom there is: if $F'=G'$ on an interval, then $(F-G)'=0$, and a function with zero derivative on an interval is constant (a consequence of the Mean Value Theorem). So the complete family is $F+C$.

We write the whole family as the indefinite integral:

$$\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C\qquad\text{where }F'=f$$

The $dx$ says which variable you are integrating with respect to; $C$ is the constant of integration.

Trap
Omitting $+C$ is the most-penalised routine error in integration. It is not decoration: without it you have named one antiderivative instead of the family, and in differential-equation questions the constant is what carries the initial condition.

The basic table — read the derivative table backwards

$\int f\,dx$resultnote
$\int x^n dx$$\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C$$n\ne-1$
$\int \dfrac1x dx$$\ln|x|+C$the missing case $n=-1$
$\int e^x dx$$e^x+C$
$\int a^x dx$$\dfrac{a^x}{\ln a}+C$
$\int \cos x\,dx$$\sin x+C$
$\int \sin x\,dx$$-\cos x+C$sign flips this way
$\int \dfrac{dx}{1+x^2}$$\arctan x+C$

Two remarks on the first two rows. The power rule fails at $n=-1$ because it would divide by zero — and the gap is filled by the logarithm, which is the one genuinely new fact in the table. The absolute value in $\ln|x|$ matters: it makes the formula valid on $x<0$ as well, where $\frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x)=\frac{-1}{-x}=\frac1x$ ✓.

Linearity holds, exactly as for derivatives:

$$\int\big(af+bg\big)dx=a\int f\,dx+b\int g\,dx$$
Always check by differentiating
Integration has a free verification step that differentiation does not: differentiate your answer and see whether you get the integrand back. It costs ten seconds and catches nearly every error. Use it on every integral you compute.

Check yourself: $\int\left(3x^2-\frac{2}{x}\right)dx$. (Answer: $x^3-2\ln|x|+C$. Differentiate to confirm.)

IDEA 1 The definite integral as a limit of sums— ⊡ ✕

Now the other problem. To find the area under $f$ from $a$ to $b$, approximate it with rectangles:

  1. Cut $[a,b]$ into $n$ strips, each of width $\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{n}$.
  2. On each strip pick a sample point $x_i^*$ and build a rectangle of height $f(x_i^*)$.
  3. Add the areas: this is a Riemann sum $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\Delta x$.
  4. Let $n\to\infty$, so the strips become infinitely thin.
Definition
$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x$$ If this limit exists (and is independent of how the sample points were chosen), $f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$.

The notation is a picture of the definition: $\int$ is an elongated S for "sum", $f(x)$ is the height, and $dx$ is the infinitesimal width. You are adding up infinitely many infinitely thin rectangles.

Which functions are integrable? In practice all the ones you meet: every function continuous on $[a,b]$ is integrable, and so is every bounded function with only finitely many discontinuities. A jump does not prevent integration — you can still measure the area under a function that steps.

Note the contrast with differentiability, which is fragile (a single corner destroys it). Integration is smoothing: it tolerates kinks and jumps. That asymmetry is worth remembering.

The definite integral is a number

$\int_a^b f(x)dx$ is a number; $\int f(x)dx$ is a family of functions. Different objects despite the shared symbol. And the variable inside is a dummy: $\int_a^b f(x)dx=\int_a^b f(t)dt$ — the name has no effect on the value.

Why this construction matters later
The probability course defines $P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f(x)dx$ for a density $f$. That definition is this limit of rectangles: chop the range into tiny intervals, multiply each width by the density there, add up. The integral is the only way to give "probability of a range" a meaning when individual points have probability zero.
FIGURE Riemann sums converging— ⊡ ✕

The curve is $f(x)=0.35x^2+0.4$ on $[0,3]$, whose exact integral is $\left[\tfrac{0.35x^3}{3}+0.4x\right]_0^3=3.15+1.2=4.35$. Increase $n$ and watch the sum close in on it from whichever side the sampling rule dictates.

What the four sampling rules show:

  • Left and right endpoints bracket the answer for a monotone function — left under-estimates and right over-estimates an increasing $f$. Their errors shrink like $1/n$.
  • Midpoint is dramatically better: the parts of each rectangle that stick out above the curve roughly cancel the parts that fall short. Error shrinks like $1/n^2$.
  • Trapezoid joins the sample heights with straight lines instead of flat tops — also $1/n^2$, and the standard basis for numerical integration.

Push $n$ to 60 and every rule agrees to two decimals. That convergence — independent of the sampling rule — is exactly what "integrable" means.

IDEA 2 Properties of the definite integral— ⊡ ✕

All of these follow from the rectangle definition, and all are used to simplify before computing.

Linearity$\displaystyle\int_a^b(cf+dg)=c\!\int_a^b\! f+d\!\int_a^b\! g$
Zero width$\displaystyle\int_a^a f=0$
Reversal$\displaystyle\int_b^a f=-\int_a^b f$
Additivity$\displaystyle\int_a^c f=\int_a^b f+\int_b^c f$
Constant$\displaystyle\int_a^b c\,dx=c(b-a)$
Comparisonif $f\le g$ then $\displaystyle\int_a^b f\le\int_a^b g$
Boundsif $m\le f\le M$ then $m(b-a)\le\displaystyle\int_a^b f\le M(b-a)$

Additivity holds even when $b$ lies outside $[a,c]$, thanks to the reversal convention — a small piece of bookkeeping that makes the algebra uniform.

Symmetry — free marks when it applies

On an interval symmetric about the origin:

$f$ even$\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}f=2\int_0^a f$
$f$ odd$\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a}f=0$

So $\int_{-2}^{2}x^3dx=0$ with no computation at all: the area below the axis on the left exactly cancels the area above it on the right. Always check symmetry before starting work.

Mean value of a function

The average value of $f$ over $[a,b]$ is

$$\bar f=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

i.e. the height of the rectangle with the same area. The Mean Value Theorem for integrals says a continuous $f$ actually attains this average somewhere: $f(c)=\bar f$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.

This is the definition behind $E[X]=\int xf(x)dx$ in the probability course — an average weighted by density.

TRAP Net area vs total area— ⊡ ✕

The definite integral counts area below the $x$-axis as negative. That is a feature, not a bug — for a velocity function it gives displacement rather than distance travelled — but it means "the integral" and "the area" are different questions.

Net (signed) area$\displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$
Total (geometric) area$\displaystyle\int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx$

To compute a total area: find where $f$ crosses zero, split the integral at those points, and take absolute values of the pieces before adding.

$f(x)=x$ on $[-2,3]$ — both questions

Net: $\displaystyle\int_{-2}^{3}x\,dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{-2}^{3}=\frac92-2=\frac52$.

Total: split at the crossing $x=0$. On $[-2,0]$ the integral is $-2$, so the area is $2$; on $[0,3]$ the integral is $\tfrac92$. Total $=2+\tfrac92=\tfrac{13}{2}$.

Very different answers. Read the question: "evaluate the integral" wants the first, "find the area enclosed" wants the second.

A second trap
Never integrate blindly across a vertical asymptote. $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2}$ looks like $\left[-\frac1x\right]_{-1}^{1}=-2$ — a negative answer for a strictly positive integrand, which is impossible. The FTC does not apply because the integrand is unbounded at $0$. That is an improper integral, handled properly in module 8, and it diverges.
IDEA 3 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus— ⊡ ✕

The theorem comes in two parts. Part 2 is the computational one you will use hundreds of times; Part 1 is the conceptual one that explains why Part 2 is true.

FTC Part 1 — differentiation undoes integration
If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, define the accumulation function $$A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt.$$ Then $A$ is differentiable and $$A'(x)=f(x).$$

Why. Increase $x$ by a tiny $h$. The extra area is a sliver of width $h$ and height about $f(x)$, so $A(x+h)-A(x)\approx f(x)h$. Divide by $h$ and let $h\to0$: the rate at which accumulated area grows is exactly the current height. Continuity is what makes "height about $f(x)$" legitimate across the sliver.

This is the sentence to remember: every continuous function has an antiderivative, namely its own accumulation function. That is what links the two problems.

FTC Part 2 — the evaluation theorem
If $F$ is any antiderivative of a continuous $f$, then $$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\;\equiv\;\Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b$$

Why it follows. $A$ from Part 1 is an antiderivative, and any other $F$ differs from it by a constant. So $F(b)-F(a)=A(b)-A(a)=A(b)-0=\int_a^b f$ — the constant cancels in the subtraction. That cancellation is also why you can safely ignore $+C$ in definite integrals.

Three evaluations
  1. $\displaystyle\int_0^2 x^2dx=\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\frac83-0=\frac83$.
  2. $\displaystyle\int_1^e\frac{dx}{x}=\big[\ln|x|\big]_1^e=1-0=1$.
  3. $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx=\big[-\cos x\big]_0^{\pi}=1-(-1)=2$.
Trap
Watch signs when substituting a negative lower limit. $\int_{-1}^{2}x^2dx=\frac83-\frac{(-1)^3}{3}=\frac83+\frac13=3$ — the minus of the FTC times the minus of $(-1)^3$ gives a plus. Write the subtraction out in full rather than doing it in your head.

Part 1 with a chain rule

When the upper limit is a function of $x$, differentiate the accumulation and multiply by the derivative of the limit:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)}f(t)\,dt=f\big(g(x)\big)\cdot g'(x)$$

Example: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2}e^{-t^2}dt=e^{-x^4}\cdot 2x$. Note you never had to integrate $e^{-t^2}$ — which is just as well, since it has no elementary antiderivative.

If the lower limit is the variable one, flip the integral first and pick up a minus sign.

See it: the accumulation function

Drag $x$ and watch the shaded area on the left become the height on the right. Where $f$ is above the axis $A$ climbs; where $f$ dips below, $A$ falls; where $f$ crosses zero, $A$ has a turning point — because $A'=f$.

With $f=\sin$: $A$ peaks exactly where $\sin$ crosses down through zero at $\pi$. With $f=x-2$: $A$ falls until $x=2$ then rises — the minimum of the accumulation is where the integrand changes sign.
