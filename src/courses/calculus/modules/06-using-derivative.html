06

Using the derivative

shape, extrema, L'Hôpital, and Taylor approximation
READ FIRST One number, four jobs— ⊡ ✕

You can now compute derivatives. This module is about what they are for. Four jobs, all following from a single observation — the sign of $f'$ tells you whether $f$ is going up or down, and the sign of $f''$ tells you which way it bends:

  • Shape. Where is $f$ increasing, decreasing, convex, concave? This is curve sketching, and it lets you draw a function you have never seen without plotting points.
  • Extrema. Where is $f$ largest or smallest? The single most important application, and the reason economics is written in calculus. This module does one variable; the Optimization course does several and adds constraints.
  • Indeterminate limits. L'Hôpital's rule turns the $\tfrac00$ problems of module 2 into one-line computations.
  • Approximation. Taylor polynomials extend the tangent line of module 4 to any accuracy you like.

Read the first two as a single skill. An exam question saying "sketch the curve" and one saying "find the maximum" are answered by the same table of signs; only the final sentence differs.

Chapter map
Monotonicity from the sign of $f'$ → critical points and the two tests → concavity and inflection from $f''$ → the closed-interval method for global extrema → L'Hôpital → Taylor polynomials → the curve-sketching checklist.
IDEA 1 Monotonicity and critical points— ⊡ ✕
Increasing / decreasing test
On an interval where $f'(x)>0$, $f$ is increasing. Where $f'(x)<0$, $f$ is decreasing.

This is intuitively obvious (positive slope means going up) but it is genuinely a theorem, and it follows from the Mean Value Theorem: for $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, there is some $c$ in between with

$$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$$

i.e. at some instant the actual speed equals the average speed. If $f'>0$ everywhere then that fraction is positive, so $f(b)>f(a)$ whenever $b>a$ — which is what "increasing" means. The MVT is the bridge from local information ($f'$ at points) to global behaviour (what $f$ does across an interval), and almost every theorem in this module rests on it.

Critical points

$c$ is a critical point of $f$ if $c$ is in the domain and either

  • $f'(c)=0$ — the tangent is horizontal, or
  • $f'(c)$ does not exist — a corner or a vertical tangent.
Fermat's theorem
If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $c$, and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$.

This is the first-order necessary condition, and it is the workhorse: it says every interior extremum hides among the critical points, so you only ever have to check a finite list.

Trap
The converse is false. $f'(c)=0$ does not imply an extremum: $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$ yet 0 is neither a max nor a min — the curve merely pauses. Critical points are candidates, not answers. You must always classify them.

And do not forget the second bullet: $f(x)=|x|$ has a genuine minimum at $0$, where $f'$ does not exist. Solving $f'=0$ alone would miss it entirely.

Check yourself: find the critical points of $f(x)=x^3-3x$. (Answer: $f'=3x^2-3=0$ at $x=\pm1$.)

IDEA 2 Classifying: the two tests— ⊡ ✕

First derivative test — always works

Look at the sign change of $f'$ as $x$ passes through the critical point $c$:

$f'$ goesConclusion
$+\;\to\;-$local maximum at $c$ (rising then falling)
$-\;\to\;+$local minimum at $c$
no changeneither — an inflection with a horizontal tangent

In practice: mark the critical points on a number line, pick one test value in each resulting interval, evaluate the sign of $f'$ there, and read off the pattern. This sign table is the single most useful device in the module, and it never fails — it needs no second derivative and works at corners too.

Second derivative test — faster, sometimes silent

If $f'(c)=0$ and $f''(c)$ exists:

$f''(c)>0$curve bends up ⇒ local minimum
$f''(c)<0$curve bends down ⇒ local maximum
$f''(c)=0$inconclusive — fall back on the first derivative test

Remember it by the shape: $f''>0$ is a cup (∪) which holds water, so the flat point is a bottom. $f''<0$ is a cap (∩), so the flat point is a top.

Trap
$f''(c)=0$ tells you nothing. All of $x^4$ (min), $-x^4$ (max) and $x^3$ (neither) have $f'(0)=f''(0)=0$. When the second derivative test goes silent, the first derivative test still answers.
Classify the critical points of $f(x)=x^3-3x$

$f'=3x^2-3=3(x-1)(x+1)$, zero at $x=\pm1$. $f''=6x$.

  • At $x=-1$: $f''(-1)=-6<0$ ⇒ local max, value $f(-1)=2$.
  • At $x=1$: $f''(1)=6>0$ ⇒ local min, value $f(1)=-2$.

Sign table check: $f'>0$ on $(-\infty,-1)$, $f'<0$ on $(-1,1)$, $f'>0$ on $(1,\infty)$ — up, down, up. Both tests agree.

Check yourself: $f(x)=x^4-4x$. Classify. (Answer: $f'=4x^3-4=0$ at $x=1$; $f''=12x^2$, $f''(1)=12>0$ ⇒ local min.)

FIGURE Curve sketching: $f$, $f'$ and $f''$ at once— ⊡ ✕

The family is $f(x)=x^3+bx^2+cx$. Change the two coefficients and watch the critical points appear, merge and vanish. The sign bars under the graph are the sign tables you would write by hand.

Things worth producing deliberately with the sliders:

  • Two extrema ($c<0$): the discriminant of $f'=3x^2+2bx+c$ is positive, so $f'$ has two roots — a max then a min.
  • Saddle ($b=c=0$): the two critical points have merged into one at the origin. $f'=3x^2\ge0$ never changes sign, so this is an inflection with a horizontal tangent, not an extremum. This is the counterexample to "$f'=0$ means extremum".
  • None ($c>0$ with small $b$): $f'>0$ everywhere, so $f$ is strictly increasing. A cubic need not have any turning points at all.

The inflection is always at $x=-b/3$ for this family, and every cubic is symmetric about its inflection point.

IDEA 3 Concavity, inflection, and global extrema— ⊡ ✕

Concavity

The second derivative measures how the slope itself is changing — that is, curvature.

$f''>0$convex (concave up, ∪) — slope increasing; the curve lies above its tangents
$f''<0$concave (concave down, ∩) — slope decreasing; the curve lies below its tangents

An inflection point is where concavity changes. Necessarily $f''=0$ there (or $f''$ fails to exist), but as always the condition is necessary, not sufficient: $f(x)=x^4$ has $f''(0)=0$ yet stays convex throughout. You must check that the sign actually flips.

Why economists care
Concavity is the mathematics of diminishing returns: each extra unit of input adds less than the one before. A concave utility function is exactly what "risk aversion" means, and Jensen's inequality — $E[u(X)]\le u(E[X])$ for concave $u$ — is why a risk-averse investor prefers a certain payoff to a gamble with the same mean. For a convex $f$ the inequality reverses. This is the bridge between this module and the probability course.

Local vs global

A local extremum beats its immediate neighbours. A global (absolute) extremum beats everything in the domain. A local max need not be global, and on an unbounded domain a global extremum need not exist at all.

Closed-interval method (for a continuous $f$ on $[a,b]$)
1. Find all critical points inside $(a,b)$. 2. Evaluate $f$ at each of them and at both endpoints $a$, $b$. 3. The largest value is the global max, the smallest the global min.

No classification is needed — you are just comparing numbers. EVT (module 3) is what guarantees this procedure terminates with an answer.

Trap
Forgetting the endpoints. The global maximum of $f(x)=x^2$ on $[-1,3]$ is $9$, at the endpoint $x=3$ — the only critical point, $x=0$, is the minimum. Endpoints are not critical points and will never appear from solving $f'=0$.
Global extrema of $f(x)=x^3-3x$ on $[0,3]$

Critical points: $x=\pm1$, but only $x=1$ lies in $[0,3]$.

Evaluate: $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=-2$, $f(3)=27-9=18$.

Global max $18$ at $x=3$ (endpoint); global min $-2$ at $x=1$ (critical point).

IDEA 4 L'Hôpital's rule— ⊡ ✕
L'Hôpital's rule
If $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ has the indeterminate form $\dfrac00$ or $\dfrac{\pm\infty}{\pm\infty}$, and $g'\ne0$ near $a$, then $$\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$$ provided the right-hand limit exists.

Why it works, informally. Near $a$ both functions are approximately their tangent lines: $f(x)\approx f'(a)(x-a)$ and $g(x)\approx g'(a)(x-a)$ (the constant terms are zero, since both vanish at $a$). The factors $(x-a)$ cancel, leaving $f'(a)/g'(a)$. The rule is the linear approximation of module 4, applied twice.

Trap
Differentiate the numerator and denominator separately. This is not the quotient rule. And check the form first: applying L'Hôpital to something that is not indeterminate gives a wrong answer. $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x+1}{x+2}=\tfrac12$ by substitution; blindly differentiating gives $\tfrac11=1$, which is simply false.
Four applications
  1. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}$ — form $\tfrac00$ ⇒ $\displaystyle\lim\frac{\cos x}{1}=1$. (The limit we assumed in module 2.)
  2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}$ — form $\tfrac00$ ⇒ $\displaystyle\lim\frac{e^x-1}{2x}$, still $\tfrac00$ ⇒ $\displaystyle\lim\frac{e^x}{2}=\tfrac12$. Repeated application is allowed, as long as you re-check the form each time.
  3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x}$ — form $\tfrac\infty\infty$ ⇒ $\displaystyle\lim\frac{1/x}{1}=0$. This proves the growth hierarchy of module 2.
  4. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x\ln x$ — form $0\cdot(-\infty)$, so rewrite as a fraction first: $\displaystyle\lim\frac{\ln x}{1/x}=\lim\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}=\lim(-x)=0$.

The other indeterminate forms

L'Hôpital only accepts $\tfrac00$ and $\tfrac\infty\infty$. Everything else must be converted:

$0\cdot\infty$write one factor as a reciprocal to make a fraction
$\infty-\infty$combine over a common denominator
$1^\infty,\;0^0,\;\infty^0$set $y=$ the expression, take $\ln$, find $\lim\ln y$, then exponentiate

The last row is how you prove $\lim_{n\to\infty}(1+\tfrac rn)^n=e^r$: take logs to get $n\ln(1+\tfrac rn)$, which is $\infty\cdot 0$, rewrite as a fraction, apply L'Hôpital to get $r$, then exponentiate.

Check yourself: $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}$. (Answer: $\tfrac00\Rightarrow\frac{\sin x}{2x}\Rightarrow\frac{\cos x}{2}=\tfrac12$.)

TIP Taylor polynomials, and the curve-sketching checklist— ⊡ ✕

Taylor: better than a tangent line

The tangent line matches $f$ in value and slope at $a$. Why stop there? Match the second derivative too, and the third, and you get a polynomial that hugs the curve ever more closely:

$$f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2+\dots+\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$$

Each term is built so that the polynomial's $k$-th derivative at $a$ equals $f^{(k)}(a)$ — the $k!$ in the denominator is exactly what cancels the factorial produced by differentiating $(x-a)^k$ $k$ times.

With $a=0$ it is called a Maclaurin series, and three of them are worth memorising:

$e^x$$1+x+\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^3}{6}+\dots$
$\ln(1+x)$$x-\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^3}{3}-\dots$
$\sin x$$x-\dfrac{x^3}{6}+\dfrac{x^5}{120}-\dots$

Truncating at the first term gives the approximations you use constantly: $e^x\approx1+x$, $\ln(1+x)\approx x$, $\sin x\approx x$ for small $x$.

Finance link
$\ln(1+r)\approx r$ is why log returns and simple returns are nearly identical for small $r$ — a 2% return is a 1.98% log return. The second-order term $-r^2/2$ is exactly the gap, and it is why the approximation degrades for large moves. Similarly, the bond price change $\Delta P\approx -D\Delta y+\tfrac12 C(\Delta y)^2$ is a second-order Taylor expansion: duration is the first-order term, convexity the second.

The curve-sketching checklist

Given an unfamiliar $f$, work through this in order and the graph draws itself. Exam questions asking you to "study the function" want exactly these steps.

  1. Domain. Where is $f$ defined? (Module 1's three checks.)
  2. Intercepts. $f(0)$ for the $y$-intercept; solve $f(x)=0$ for the $x$-intercepts if it is easy.
  3. Symmetry. Even, odd, or neither — halves the work when it applies.
  4. Asymptotes. Vertical (denominator zero), horizontal ($x\to\pm\infty$), oblique if the degree gap is one.
  5. $f'$: sign table. Critical points, intervals of increase and decrease.
  6. Classify. Local maxima and minima, with their $y$-values.
  7. $f''$: sign table. Convex and concave intervals, inflection points.
  8. Draw. Plot the special points, respect the asymptotes, join with the shapes the sign tables dictate.
Self-check
Your sketch must be consistent with both tables at once. A stretch that is simultaneously "increasing" and "concave" must rise while flattening; if your drawing does something else, one of your sign tables is wrong. Also: between two consecutive critical points the sign of $f'$ cannot change, so the curve is monotone there — no wiggles.
Marginal thinking
In economics $f'$ is called the marginal quantity: marginal cost $C'(q)$, marginal revenue $R'(q)$. Profit $\pi=R-C$ is maximised where $\pi'=R'-C'=0$, i.e. marginal revenue equals marginal cost — the most quoted result in microeconomics is just Fermat's theorem. The related unit-free measure is elasticity, $\varepsilon=\dfrac{f'(x)\,x}{f(x)}=\dfrac{d\ln f}{d\ln x}$, the percentage change in output per percentage change in input.
