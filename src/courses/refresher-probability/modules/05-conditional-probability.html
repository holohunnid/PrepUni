05

Conditional probability

lecture 3, slides 2–8 · what happens to a probability when you learn something
READ FIRST The question this module answers— ⊡ ✕

What is the probability of an event $A$, given an event $B$? The lecture's example makes the point in one line:

$$P(\text{getting wet}\mid\text{it rains today})\approx1,\qquad P(\text{getting wet}\mid\text{it does not rain today})\approx0$$

where "$\mid$" reads "given that" and is followed by the conditioning event. Same event of interest, wildly different probabilities. So: conditioning on an event can change the probability of another event, and quantifying that change is the whole subject.

Two points of interpretation that trip people up, and the lecture flags both:

  • The conditioning event may or may not have already occurred — we do not care. We take it as given. $P(\text{I will get wet}\mid\text{it will rain tomorrow})$ is perfectly meaningful even though tomorrow has not happened.
  • The probability of the conditioning event is irrelevant to the question being asked. The question is: given $B$ (regardless of how likely $B$ was, or whether it occurred), what is the probability of $A$?
The mental picture
Conditioning on $B$ means throwing away the rest of the sample space and renormalising. The world shrinks from $\Omega$ to $B$; probabilities are then measured as fractions of $B$ instead of fractions of $\Omega$. Everything in this module follows from that one image.
DEF Conditional probability— ⊡ ✕

Given an event $B$ with $P(B)>0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$\boxed{\;P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad\text{when }P(B)>0\;}\tag{1}$$

where $P(A\cap B)$ is the joint probability that both $A$ and $B$ occur.

Reading the formula

The denominator $P(B)$ is the renormalisation: it rescales so that the new, shrunken world $B$ has total probability 1. The numerator asks: of the outcomes in $B$, which ones are also in $A$? Only $A\cap B$ counts — the part of $A$ outside $B$ has been discarded along with everything else outside $B$.

$P(B)>0$ is required
Dividing by zero is undefined, so conditioning on an impossible event is meaningless. (For continuous random variables in module 16 every single value has $P(X=x)=0$, and the definition has to be rebuilt with densities — which is exactly what happens there.)
RULES Three properties, and what each one says— ⊡ ✕

All three follow immediately from formula (1), and all three are worth reading as sanity checks on the "shrink the world" picture.

  1. $P(A\mid B)=0$ if $A\subseteq B^c$.
    If $A$ lies entirely outside $B$, then $A\cap B=\emptyset$, so the numerator is $P(\emptyset)=0$. In words: $A$ cannot occur, since $A$ and $B$ have no common elements and we are told $B$ occurred.
  2. If $A\subseteq B$ then $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A)}{P(B)}$.
    Here $A\cap B=A$, so the numerator is just $P(A)$. Every outcome of $A$ survives the shrinking, and all that happens is the rescaling by $1/P(B)$ — which is $\ge1$. In words: conditioning should not alter the relative frequency of the various events contained in $B$; it multiplies them all by the same factor.
  3. $P(B\mid B)=1$.
    Take $A=B$ in (1): $P(B\cap B)/P(B)=P(B)/P(B)=1$. In words: $B$ will be observed with certainty, since we know $B$ has occurred. This also confirms that $P(\cdot\mid B)$ is itself a legitimate probability measure — it satisfies Axiom 3 on the reduced sample space $B$.
Conditional probability is a probability
Fix $B$ and let $A$ vary: the function $A\mapsto P(A\mid B)$ satisfies all three Kolmogorov axioms (with $B$ playing the role of $\Omega$). So every rule in module 04 has a conditional twin — for instance $P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B)$. This is used without comment in later modules.
RULE The multiplicative rule— ⊡ ✕

An immediate consequence of the definition: multiply both sides of (1) by $P(B)$.

$$\boxed{\;P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)\;}\tag{2}$$

Small algebraic step, large practical consequence. Formula (1) computes a conditional probability from a joint one; formula (2) runs the machine backwards, computing a joint probability from a conditional one — and conditional probabilities are very often what a problem actually hands you.

"Draw two cards without replacement; what is the probability both are aces?" gives you $P(\text{2nd ace}\mid\text{1st ace})=3/51$ naturally, and (2) turns it into the joint probability

$$P(\text{both aces})=\frac{3}{51}\cdot\frac{4}{52}$$

By symmetry of the intersection, $A\cap B=B\cap A$, so (2) can equally be written

$$P(A\cap B)=P(B\mid A)\,P(A)$$

and both forms are used freely. Setting the two right-hand sides equal is, incidentally, one line away from Bayes' rule — though this course does not need it.

Where it goes next
Module 06 asks what happens when the conditioning changes nothing, i.e. when $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. Substituting that into (2) gives $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$ — the product rule for independent events, and the single most-used formula in lectures 3–4.
EXERCISE (slides 6–7) · roll a die

What is the probability of rolling a 3, given that the result is an odd number?

  1. Name the events. $A=\{\text{rolling a }3\}=\{3\}$ and $B=\{\text{odd number}\}=\{1,3,5\}$.
  2. Find the intersection. $A\cap B=\{3\}\cap\{1,3,5\}=\{3\}=A$. (Note $A\subseteq B$, so we are in property 2 above.)
  3. Unconditional probabilities, by the classical definition on a fair die: $$P(A\cap B)=P(\{3\})=\tfrac16,\qquad P(B)=\tfrac36=\tfrac12$$
  4. Apply the definition: $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\frac{1/6}{1/2}=\frac13$$
Insight
Check it against the picture. Learning "the result is odd" shrinks the world from six equally likely faces to three: $\{1,3,5\}$. Within that world, 3 is one of three equally likely outcomes, so its probability is $1/3$ — up from $1/6$. Conditioning on information that is compatible with $A$ makes $A$ more likely; the factor is exactly $1/P(B)=2$.
