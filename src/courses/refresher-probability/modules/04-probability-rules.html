The probability rules

lecture 2, slides 17–23 · everything else follows from the three axioms
READ FIRST Three axioms, and the rules they generate— ⊡ ✕

Module 03 gave three axioms and one derived formula. This module derives everything else you will use, and there is nothing more to add afterwards — the whole calculus of probability for events is in these few lines:

Complement rule$P(A)=1-P(A^c)$
Impossible event$P(\emptyset)=0$
Upper bound$P(A)\le1$ for every event $A$
Monotonicityif $A\subseteq B$ then $P(A)\le P(B)$
Union (general)$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$

Every one of them is proved by the same manoeuvre: rewrite the event as a union of disjoint pieces, then use Axiom 2. The pieces always come from the partition $\{A,A^c\}$ of module 02. If you follow that one thread you never have to memorise these — you can rederive any of them in two lines.

The move worth stealing
Whenever a probability looks hard, ask whether the complement is easy. "At least one" is nearly always easier as $1-P(\text{none})$; "more than four" as $1-P(\text{at most four})$. Half the exercises in lectures 2–5 are solved by that single reflex.
RULE The complement rule— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{\;P(A)=1-P(A^c)\;}\tag{2}$$

Proof. By definition, $A$ and $A^c$ are mutually exclusive ($A\cap A^c=\emptyset$) and collectively exhaustive ($A\cup A^c=\Omega$) — they form a partition of $\Omega$, exactly as verified in module 02. Therefore

$$1\overset{\text{Ax.3}}{=}P(\Omega)=P(A\cup A^c)\overset{\text{Ax.2}}{=}P(A)+P(A^c)\;\Longrightarrow\;P(A)=1-P(A^c)$$

Both axioms are used, and the disjointness needed by Axiom 2 comes for free from the definition of complement. $\;\square$

Why it is the workhorse
Combined with De Morgan (module 02), it converts unions into intersections. "At least one of $A_1,\dots,A_n$" becomes $$P\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)=1-P\!\left(\bigcap_i A_i^c\right)$$ and if the events are independent (module 06) the right-hand side is a plain product. That is the entire solution to several later exercises.
EX Three implications (slides 18–19)— ⊡ ✕

1. Show $P(\emptyset)=0$.

  1. The empty set is the complement of the sample space: $\emptyset=\Omega^c$.
  2. Apply the complement rule and Axiom 3: $$P(\emptyset)=P(\Omega^c)=1-P(\Omega)=1-1=0$$

The impossible event has probability zero, as it must.

2. Show $P(A)\le1$ for any event $A$.

  1. By the complement rule, $P(A)=1-P(A^c)$.
  2. By Axiom 1 applied to the event $A^c$, we have $P(A^c)\ge0$.
  3. Subtracting something non-negative from 1 cannot exceed 1, so $P(A)\le1$ ✓

3. Show that if $A\subseteq B$ then $P(A)\le P(B)$  (monotonicity).

  1. Since $A\subseteq B$, we can split $B$ using the partition $\{A,A^c\}$ — this is equation (2) of module 02 applied to $B$: $$B=(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A^c)=A\cup(B\cap A^c)$$ where $B\cap A=A$ precisely because $A$ sits inside $B$.
  2. The two pieces are disjoint, since $A\cap(B\cap A^c)\subseteq A\cap A^c=\emptyset$. So Axiom 2 applies: $$P(B)=P(A)+P(B\cap A^c)$$
  3. By Axiom 1, $P(B\cap A^c)\ge0$, hence $P(B)\ge P(A)$ ✓ $\;\square$
Insight
All three proofs are the same manoeuvre: cut the event along $\{A,A^c\}$, then add. Monotonicity is the formal version of something obvious — a bigger event cannot be less likely — but notice that "obvious" is not a proof, and that the argument also tells you by how much: exactly $P(B\cap A^c)$, the probability of the extra part.
RULE Probability of the union of non-disjoint sets— ⊡ ✕
$$\boxed{\;P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\;}\tag{3}$$

If $A\cap B\neq\emptyset$ — the two events overlap — then adding $P(A)$ and $P(B)$ counts the overlap twice. Subtracting $P(A\cap B)$ once repairs the double-counting. And if $A\cap B=\emptyset$, then $P(A\cap B)=P(\emptyset)=0$ and (3) collapses back to Axiom 2, as it must.

Proof

  1. Rewrite $A\cup B$ as a union of two mutually exclusive events, using relation (3) of module 02: $$A\cup B=A\cup(A^c\cap B)$$
  2. These are disjoint, since $A\cap(A^c\cap B)=\emptyset$. By Axiom 2, $$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(A^c\cap B)\tag{$\star$}$$
  3. Now handle the second term. The sets $A\cap B$ and $A^c\cap B$ form a partition of $B$ — this is equation (2) of module 02 applied to $B$ — so Axiom 2 gives $$P(B)=P(A\cap B)+P(A^c\cap B)\;\Longrightarrow\;P(A^c\cap B)=P(B)-P(A\cap B)$$
  4. Substituting into $(\star)$ proves (3). $\;\square$

Notice that step 3 is itself a useful formula in its own right, and one you will reach for constantly:

$$P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B)\qquad\text{"}A\text{ but not }B\text{"}$$

Read it as: take everything in $A$, remove the part that is also in $B$.

Push $P(A\cap B)$ to zero and the discs separate: the union becomes the plain sum $P(A)+P(B)$, which is Axiom 2. Push it to its maximum and one disc swallows the other: $A\subseteq B$, and $P(A\cup B)=P(B)$, which is monotonicity.

The four regions in the readout — $A$ only, both, $B$ only, neither — are the four blocks of the finest partition you can build from two events. They always sum to 1, and every probability you can ask about $A$ and $B$ is some sum of them. The "independent" preset sets $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)=0.30$; module 06 explains why that particular number is special.

EXERCISE (slides 21–23) · meteoSvizzera

MeteoSvizzera reports: a 60% chance of rain today; a 50% chance of rain tomorrow; a 30% chance that it does not rain on either day. Compute the probability that it will rain (a) today or tomorrow, (b) today and tomorrow, (c) today but not tomorrow, (d) on exactly one of the two days.

Set-up. Let $A=\{\text{rain today}\}$ and $B=\{\text{rain tomorrow}\}$, so $P(A)=0.6$ and $P(B)=0.5$. The third piece of information is about $C=\{\text{no rain either day}\}$, which as a set is

$$C=A^c\cap B^c,\qquad P(A^c\cap B^c)=0.3$$

Draw the Venn diagram before computing anything: two overlapping discs inside a rectangle, with the region outside both discs carrying 0.3.

a) Rain today or tomorrow: $P(A\cup B)$.

  1. The datum we were given is about the region outside both discs, so use the complement rule — and to identify that region, use De Morgan (module 02, equation 6): $$(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c$$
  2. Therefore $$P(A\cup B)=1-P\big((A\cup B)^c\big)=1-P(A^c\cap B^c)=1-0.3=\mathbf{0.7}$$

Note that neither $P(A)$ nor $P(B)$ was needed for this part — the "30% neither" already contains the answer, once De Morgan tells you what it is the complement of.

b) Rain today and tomorrow: $P(A\cap B)$.

  1. Rearrange the union rule (3) to solve for the intersection: $$P(A\cap B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cup B)$$
  2. $$P(A\cap B)=0.6+0.5-0.7=\mathbf{0.4}$$

A useful check: $0.6+0.5=1.1>1$, so the two events must overlap, and by at least $0.1$. They overlap by 0.4.

c) Rain today but not tomorrow: $P(A\cap B^c)$.

  1. Split $A$ along the partition $\{B,B^c\}$: $\;A=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap B^c)$, a union of disjoint pieces.
  2. By Axiom 2, $P(A)=P(A\cap B)+P(A\cap B^c)$, hence $$P(A\cap B^c)=P(A)-P(A\cap B)=0.6-0.4=\mathbf{0.2}$$

d) Rain on exactly one of the two days.

"Today or tomorrow, but not both" is the union of two disjoint events: rain today only, or rain tomorrow only.

$$(A\cap B^c)\cup(B\cap A^c)$$
  1. By the same argument as (c) applied to $B$: $$P(B\cap A^c)=P(B)-P(B\cap A)=0.5-0.4=0.1$$
  2. The two events are disjoint — you cannot have "rain today only" and "rain tomorrow only" simultaneously — so Axiom 2 applies: $$P\big((A\cap B^c)\cup(B\cap A^c)\big)=0.2+0.1=\mathbf{0.3}$$
  3. Alternative route. "Exactly one" is "at least one, minus both": $$P\big((A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)\big)=P(A\cup B)-P(A\cap B)=0.7-0.4=0.3\ \checkmark$$ This works because $A\cap B\subseteq A\cup B$, so the subtraction is the monotonicity argument of the previous panel.
Final check — the four regions must sum to 1
$$\underbrace{0.2}_{A\text{ only}}+\underbrace{0.4}_{\text{both}}+\underbrace{0.1}_{B\text{ only}}+\underbrace{0.3}_{\text{neither}}=1.0\ \checkmark$$ Always run this check on a two-event problem: the four blocks of the partition are the complete picture, and if they do not sum to 1 you have made an arithmetic error.
