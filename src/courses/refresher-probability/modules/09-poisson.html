09

The Poisson distribution

lecture 4, slides 37–42 · counting events with no upper limit
READ FIRST When the binomial cannot be used— ⊡ ✕

The binomial needs a fixed number of trials $n$. Many counting problems have no such number:

  • the number of calls to a call centre per minute;
  • the number of customers at a supermarket checkout in one hour;
  • the number of passengers arriving at the airport each day;
  • the number of cars through the San Gottardo tunnel every weekend.

In each case the count can be $0,1,2,3,\dots$ with no pre-set upper limit. There is no natural $n$ to plug into a binomial — you cannot say "there were exactly 4000 trials, each a potential phone call".

The Poisson distribution, introduced by Siméon-Denis Poisson in 1838, is built for exactly this: it models the number of events per unit of time (or per unit of space, or per page, or per lot) with unbounded support.

One parameter, two jobs
The Poisson has a single parameter $\lambda>0$, and it turns out to be both the mean and the variance of the distribution (modules 10 and 11). That is unusually rigid, and it is the main thing to check before using a Poisson on real data.
DEF The Poisson distribution— ⊡ ✕

Let $\lambda$ be a strictly positive constant, $\lambda>0$. A discrete random variable $X$ has a Poisson distribution, written $X\sim Po(\lambda)$, when its probability distribution is

$$\boxed{\;P(x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!},\qquad x=0,1,2,\dots\;}\tag{4}$$

$P(x)$ gives the probability that a number $x$ of events occurs in a given time interval.

The absence of an upper limit on $x$ is a feature, not a bug: a priori it is difficult to determine the maximum number of events, so a model that does not force you to name one is exactly what is wanted.

The limit on slide 38

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}P(x)=0$$

What does this mean? Although arbitrarily large counts are possible, they become vanishingly improbable: the factorial $x!$ in the denominator eventually overwhelms $\lambda^x$ in the numerator, whatever $\lambda$ is. Two consequences worth stating:

  • The model is well defined despite infinite support — the probabilities do sum to 1, since $\sum_{x\ge0}\lambda^x/x!=e^{\lambda}$ cancels the $e^{-\lambda}$ exactly: $$\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^x}{x!}=e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda}=1\ \checkmark$$
  • Unbounded support is not the same as "anything can plausibly happen". A call centre averaging 3 calls a minute could receive 50, but with probability around $10^{-30}$.
A useful special value
Setting $x=0$ in (4) gives $P(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}$, since $\lambda^0=1$ and $0!=1$. This one identity solves the call-centre exercise below by running it backwards to recover $\lambda$.
LINK Poisson as an approximation to the binomial— ⊡ ✕

The two distributions are closely related. If $n$ is large and $p$ is small, so that the product $np$ stays moderate, then

$$Bin(n,p)\;\approx\;Po(\lambda)\qquad\text{with}\qquad\lambda=np$$

The intuition: a binomial with many trials and a tiny success probability is "many chances, each nearly hopeless", which is exactly the situation the Poisson describes — rare events accumulating over a long exposure. Matching $\lambda=np$ matches the two means (module 10: $E[X]=np$ for the binomial, $E[X]=\lambda$ for the Poisson).

This is why the vaccine exercise below can be solved either way, and why the two answers agree to three decimals.

Push $\lambda$ up and watch the shape drift right and turn symmetric — for large $\lambda$ the Poisson starts to look like a normal distribution (module 13). Push it below 1 and almost all the mass piles onto $x=0$, which is the earthquakes preset.

EXERCISE (slides 39–40) · number of calls per minute at a call centre

At a call centre, the probability of receiving zero calls per minute is $0.0497871$. What is the probability of receiving six calls in one minute?

The parameter $\lambda$ is not given — it has to be recovered from the one probability we do know.

  1. Set up. Let $X$ be the number of calls per minute, $X\sim Po(\lambda)$.
  2. Use the special value at $x=0$. From formula (4), $$P(X=0)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^0}{0!}=e^{-\lambda}=0.0497871$$
  3. Solve for $\lambda$ by taking logarithms: $$-\lambda=\log(0.0497871)\;\Longrightarrow\;\lambda=-\log(0.0497871)\approx3$$ (Indeed $e^{-3}=0.0497871$ to seven decimals — the number was chosen to come out exactly.)
  4. Now answer the question. Apply (4) with $\lambda=3$ and $x=6$: $$P(X=6)=\frac{e^{-3}\,3^6}{6!}=\frac{0.0497871\times729}{720}=\frac{36.295}{720}\approx\mathbf{0.0504}$$ In R: dpois(6, lambda = 3) returns $\approx0.05$.
Insight
The structure here is worth noticing: one observed probability determines the whole distribution, because the Poisson has only one parameter. Give me any single Poisson probability and I can recover $\lambda$ and then compute every other one. That is the flip side of the rigidity noted at the top of the module.
EXERCISE (slides 41–42) · fever from a vaccine

The probability that a person develops a fever from a flu vaccine is 0.005. A total of 1000 people receive the vaccine. Using the Poisson as an approximation with $\lambda=1000\times0.005=5$, calculate: (a) that one person develops a fever; (b) that more than six do. And: which exact distribution should have been used?

  1. Set-up. $X$ counts the vaccinated people who develop a fever, approximated by $X\sim Po(\lambda)$ with $\lambda=5$, so $P(X=x)=e^{-5}5^x/x!$.
  2. (a) One person. $$P(X=1)=\frac{e^{-5}5^1}{1!}=5e^{-5}=5(0.0067379)\approx\mathbf{0.034}$$ In R: dpois(1, lambda=5) $\approx0.034$.
  3. (b) More than six. Use the complement to convert into a CDF: $$P(X>6)=1-P(X\le6)$$ In R: 1 - ppois(6, lambda=5) returns $\approx\mathbf{0.238}$.
    Equivalently, by hand, $P(X>6)=1-\sum_{x=0}^{6}e^{-5}5^x/x!$.
  4. Which exact distribution? The binomial: there are exactly $n=1000$ independent trials (the vaccinated people), each with the same success probability $p=0.005$. So the exact model is $X\sim Bin(1000,\,0.005)$.
  5. Compare. dbinom(1, 1000, 0.005) $\approx0.034$ and 1 - pbinom(6, 1000, 0.005) $\approx0.238$ — the Poisson approximation agrees to three decimal places, exactly as the large-$n$/small-$p$ argument promises.
Insight
Here the exact model does exist, so the Poisson is a convenience rather than a necessity. Historically that convenience was decisive — one exponential and a factorial beat computing $\binom{1000}{x}$ by hand. Today it matters more as a modelling insight: rare events over many independent opportunities are Poisson-shaped, whether or not you can identify the trials.
EXERCISE (slides 67–68) · earthquakes

In the past 100 years there have been 93 earthquakes of magnitude 6 on the Richter scale. What is the probability that 3 earthquakes, all of magnitude 6, occur in a single year?

  1. Choose the model. Let $X$ be the number of magnitude-6 earthquakes per year. This is a count of events per unit of time with no upper limit, so model it as $X\sim Po(\lambda)$.
  2. Estimate $\lambda$ from the data. Since $E[X]=\lambda$ is the expected number of events per unit of time (module 10), and the data give 93 events over 100 years, $$\lambda=\frac{93}{100}=0.93\ \text{earthquakes per year}$$
  3. Apply formula (4) with $x=3$: $$P(X=3)=\frac{e^{-0.93}(0.93)^3}{3!}=\frac{0.39455\times0.804357}{6}=\frac{0.31736}{6}\approx\mathbf{0.053}$$ In R: dpois(3, lambda = 0.93) returns $\approx0.053$.
Insight
Note the modelling step hidden in step 2: the sample average is used as the parameter, which is legitimate precisely because $E[X]=\lambda$ for a Poisson. The same move appears in the frogs exercise of module 08 ("1 out of 8 frogs on average" gives $p=1/8$) — in both cases the theoretical mean is set equal to the observed average. That is the method of moments, in its simplest form.
