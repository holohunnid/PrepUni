Value at Risk and Expected Shortfall

This module is the payoff of the previous sixteen. To measure downside risk / tail risk — exposure to large but unlikely losses, i.e. to negative returns in the left tail of a return distribution — various risk measures have been proposed. The two most widely used are Value at Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES).

They are calculated, for example, by trading desks to measure portfolio risk, and by regulators to control the exposure of banks and insurance companies and to set the amount of capital needed to cover possible losses.

Recall the definition of a quantile
The quantile $q_\alpha$ of the distribution of a continuous RV $X$ is the smallest value taken by $X$ such that the probability of observing a realisation less than $q_\alpha$ is equal to $\alpha$: $\;\mathbb{P}(X<q_\alpha)=\alpha$. The percentile is the quantile expressed as a percentage.
DEF Value at Risk— ⊡ ✕

Value at Risk at the probability level $\alpha$ (e.g. $\alpha=1\%$), written $VaR_\alpha$, of a return distribution $Y$ is the minimum loss that the portfolio return will suffer with probability $\alpha$ — where a loss is a negative realisation of $Y$. Formally:

$$\boxed{\;-VaR_\alpha:=q_\alpha\quad\text{where }q_\alpha\text{ satisfies }P(Y<q_\alpha)=\alpha\;}$$

so $VaR_\alpha=-q_\alpha$. VaR represents a loss and is expressed as a positive number — hence the minus sign, which converts the (negative) quantile into a (positive) loss figure.

How to read it

By definition of percentile, $VaR_{0.01}$ is the minimum loss with probability 1%: 1% of the returns will be smaller than $-VaR_{0.01}$, and 99% will be larger. More generally, given the VaR at $\alpha\%$, we know that $(1-\alpha)\%$ of the returns will be better than $-VaR$, while $\alpha\%$ will be worse.

In the level version, for a position with return $S_{t+1}-S_t$ (in CHF) over $[t,t+1]$:

$$VaR_\alpha:=-q_\alpha\ \text{ such that }\ \mathbb{P}_t\big(S_{t+1}-S_t<q_\alpha\big)=\mathbb{P}_t\big((S_{t+1}-S_t)+VaR_\alpha<0\big)=\alpha$$

Interpretation: the VaR is the amount in CHF that would have to be added to the position at time $t$ so that the return between $t$ and $t+1$ is negative only with probability $\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is small — usually 1% or 5%. That is exactly the regulatory reading: the capital buffer.

Computing it for a normal return

If $Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$, standardise as in module 13: $q_\alpha=\mu+\sigma z_\alpha$, where $z_\alpha$ is the standard normal quantile. Hence

$$\boxed{\;VaR_\alpha=-\big(\mu+\sigma z_\alpha\big)=-\mu-\sigma z_\alpha\;}$$

with the two values you will need:

$z_{0.01}=-2.3263$1% levelR: qnorm(0.01)
$z_{0.05}=-1.6449$5% levelR: qnorm(0.05)
DEF Expected Shortfall— ⊡ ✕

VaR is the loss at the $\alpha$ percentile of the distribution of returns, but it ignores any losses to the left of VaR — i.e. losses worse than VaR. Expected Shortfall addresses this problem.

The Expected Shortfall at level $\alpha$, $ES_\alpha$, is the expected value of the returns $Y$, conditional on $Y$ being less than $-VaR_\alpha$:

$$\boxed{\;ES_\alpha:=-\,\mathbb{E}_t\big[\,Y\;\big|\;Y<-VaR_\alpha\,\big]\;}$$

Again the minus makes it a positive loss figure. In the level version, for a position with return $S_{t+1}-S_t$:

$$ES_\alpha:=-\,\mathbb{E}_t\big[S_{t+1}-S_t\;\big|\;S_{t+1}-S_t<-VaR_\alpha\big]$$

This is a conditional expectation in the sense of module 16 — conditioning a continuous variable on the event "$Y$ lies in the left tail", then averaging over that shrunken, renormalised world.

Why regulators moved from VaR to ES
Two distributions can share the same VaR while one has a catastrophic tail beyond it and the other does not: VaR asks only where the 1% cut-off sits, never how bad things get past it. ES averages over the whole tail and therefore sees the difference. Always $ES_\alpha>VaR_\alpha$, since it averages outcomes all of which are worse than the cut-off.

Computing it for a normal return

For $Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$ there is a closed form:

$$\boxed{\;ES_\alpha=-\mu+\sigma\,\frac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}\;}$$

where $\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}$ is the standard normal density (not the CDF) evaluated at the quantile $z_\alpha$. The ratio $\varphi(z_\alpha)/\alpha$ is the average depth of the tail, in standard deviations.

$\alpha=0.01$$\varphi(-2.3263)=0.026652$$\varphi/\alpha=2.6652$
$\alpha=0.05$$\varphi(-1.6449)=0.103136$$\varphi/\alpha=2.0627$

Compare with $-z_{0.01}=2.3263$: the ES multiplier is larger, which is the closed-form statement that $ES>VaR$.

EXERCISE (slides 44–47) · VaR of a portfolio's annual return

Let $X_1\sim\mathcal{N}(0.01,\,0.10^2)$ and $X_2\sim\mathcal{N}(0.04,\,0.40^2)$ be the annual returns of two risky securities, with correlation $\rho=0.3$. Consider the portfolio investing 0.6 in $X_1$ and 0.4 in $X_2$:

$$Y=0.6\,X_1+0.4\,X_2$$

Compute the expected value and standard deviation of $Y$, find its distribution, and then calculate $VaR_\alpha$ at level $\alpha=0.01$.

Step 1 — expected value, by linearity (module 10). No independence needed:

$$E[Y]=0.6\,E[X_1]+0.4\,E[X_2]=0.6(0.01)+0.4(0.04)=0.006+0.016=\mathbf{0.022}$$

Step 2 — covariance from the correlation (module 15). Invert formula (12), $\rho=\text{Cov}/(\sigma_1\sigma_2)$:

$$\text{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.3\times0.10\times0.40=0.012$$

Step 3 — variance, by the $V(aX+bY)$ rule (module 15).

$$V[Y]=(0.6)^2V(X_1)+(0.4)^2V(X_2)+2(0.6)(0.4)\,\text{Cov}(X_1,X_2)$$ $$=0.36(0.01)+0.16(0.16)+0.48(0.012)=0.0036+0.0256+0.00576=0.03496$$ $$\sigma_Y=\sqrt{0.03496}\approx\mathbf{0.1870}$$

Note the diversification: the weighted average of the two volatilities would be $0.6(0.10)+0.4(0.40)=0.22$, so the portfolio's $0.187$ is genuinely lower — the gain comes from $\rho=0.3<1$.

Step 4 — the distribution of $Y$. A linear combination of jointly normal variables is itself normal, so

$$Y\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0.022,\;0.1869^2\big)$$

matching the red density on slide 45, which sits between the two assets': narrower than $X_2$, wider than $X_1$.

Step 5 — the 1% quantile. Standardise, using $z_{0.01}=-2.3263$:

$$q_{0.01}=\mu+\sigma z_{0.01}=0.022+0.1870\times(-2.3263)=0.022-0.4350=-0.4130$$

In R: qnorm(0.01, mean = 0.022, sd = 0.1870).

Step 6 — the VaR. Flip the sign to express it as a positive loss:

$$VaR_{0.01}=-q_{0.01}=\mathbf{0.413}\quad\text{i.e. about }41.3\%$$

Reading: with probability 1%, the portfolio will lose at least 41.3% of its value over the year. Equivalently, 99% of the time the annual return will be better than $-41.3\%$.

"At least", not "at most"
VaR is a minimum loss over the bad 1% of scenarios, not a worst case. The losses in that 1% could be far larger — VaR says nothing about how much larger. That silence is exactly what the next exercise repairs.
EXERCISE (slide 64) · Expected Shortfall of the same portfolio

With $Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0.02,\,0.1869^2)$ from the previous exercise, calculate $ES_\alpha$ at level $\alpha=0.01$.

  1. Recall the definition. $ES_\alpha=-E[Y\mid Y<-VaR_\alpha]=-E[Y\mid Y<q_{0.01}]$ — the average return over the worst 1% of outcomes, sign-flipped.
  2. Evaluate the standard normal density at the quantile. With $z_{0.01}=-2.3263$: $$\varphi(z_{0.01})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}=0.398942\times e^{-2.70578}=0.398942\times0.066807=0.026652$$
  3. Compute the tail-depth multiplier: $$\frac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}=\frac{0.026652}{0.01}=2.6652$$ In words: conditional on landing in the worst 1%, the return averages 2.6652 standard deviations below the mean — deeper than the 2.3263 at which the tail begins, as it must be.
  4. Apply the closed form: $$ES_{0.01}=-\mu+\sigma\frac{\varphi(z_\alpha)}{\alpha}=-0.022+0.1870\times2.6652=-0.022+0.4984=\mathbf{0.476}$$ i.e. about 47.6%.
  5. Compare with VaR: $ES_{0.01}=47.6\%$ against $VaR_{0.01}=41.3\%$. The expected shortfall is 6.3 percentage points worse — that gap is the average severity of the losses that VaR refuses to look at.
The chain of dependencies, worth tracing once
The final 47.6% rests on: linearity of expectation (module 10) for $\mu_Y$; the variance of a linear combination and the definition of correlation (module 15) for $\sigma_Y$; standardisation and quantiles of the normal (module 13) for $z_\alpha$; and conditional expectation (module 16) for the tail average. Six modules feeding one number — which is why this exercise closes the course.
