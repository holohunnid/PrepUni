X01●●L5 · slides 47–49Graph of a polynomial density
Given the continuous random variable $X$ whose density function is $$f(x)=\begin{cases}\tfrac34(-x^2+2x)&\text{for }x\in[0,2]\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$ draw a graph of the density function $f(x)$.
Solution
  1. First check it really is a density. On $[0,2]$ we have $-x^2+2x=x(2-x)\ge0$ ✓, so $f\ge0$. And the area: $$\int_0^2\tfrac34(2x-x^2)dx=\tfrac34\left[x^2-\tfrac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2=\tfrac34\left(4-\tfrac83\right)=\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac43=1\ \checkmark$$
  2. Shape. $f$ is a downward parabola through the roots $x=0$ and $x=2$, symmetric about their midpoint $x=1$.
  3. Peak. At $x=1$: $f(1)=\tfrac34(-1+2)=\tfrac34=0.75$. So the curve rises from $(0,0)$ to $(1,0.75)$ and falls back to $(2,0)$, and is identically zero outside $[0,2]$.
  4. In R, as the lecture suggests: f <- function(x){3/4 * (-x^2 + 2*x)} then curve(f, from = 0, to = 2). Or with points: x <- seq(0, 2, by = 0.01); y <- 3/4 * (-x^2 + 2*x); plot(x, y).
X02●●L5 · slides 50–51Expected value and variance of that density
For the same $f(x)=\tfrac34(-x^2+2x)$ on $[0,2]$, compute $E(X)$ and $V(X)$.
Solution
  1. Expected value. Apply $E(X)=\int x\,f(x)dx$ over the support: $$E(X)=\int_0^2 x\cdot\tfrac34(-x^2+2x)\,dx=\tfrac34\int_0^2(2x^2-x^3)\,dx$$
  2. Integrate term by term: $$=\tfrac34\left[\frac{2x^3}{3}-\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2=\tfrac34\left(\frac{16}{3}-4\right)=\tfrac34\cdot\frac{4}{3}=\mathbf{1}$$ As expected from the symmetry of the parabola about $x=1$ — a good check.
  3. Variance, from the definition with $E(X)=1$: $$V(X)=\int_0^2(x-1)^2\cdot\tfrac34(-x^2+2x)\,dx$$
  4. Expand the integrand. With $(x-1)^2=x^2-2x+1$ and $(-x^2+2x)$: $$(x^2-2x+1)(-x^2+2x)=-x^4+2x^3+2x^3-4x^2-x^2+2x=-x^4+4x^3-5x^2+2x$$ so $$V(X)=\tfrac34\int_0^2\left(-x^4+4x^3-5x^2+2x\right)dx$$
  5. Integrate: $$=\tfrac34\left[-\frac{x^5}{5}+x^4-\frac{5x^3}{3}+x^2\right]_0^2=\tfrac34\left(-\frac{32}{5}+16-\frac{40}{3}+4\right)$$
  6. Common denominator 15: $-\frac{96}{15}+\frac{240}{15}-\frac{200}{15}+\frac{60}{15}=\frac{4}{15}$. Hence $$V(X)=\tfrac34\cdot\frac{4}{15}=\frac{1}{5}=\mathbf{0.2}$$
  7. Cross-check with the shortcut $V=E(X^2)-\mu^2$: $$E(X^2)=\tfrac34\int_0^2(2x^3-x^4)dx=\tfrac34\left[\frac{x^4}{2}-\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_0^2=\tfrac34\left(8-\frac{32}{5}\right)=\tfrac34\cdot\frac{8}{5}=\frac{6}{5}$$ $$V(X)=\frac65-1^2=\frac15\ \checkmark$$
Insight
$\sigma=\sqrt{0.2}\approx0.447$ on a support of width 2 — the distribution is fairly concentrated, as the peaked parabola suggests. Compare with a uniform on $[0,2]$, which has $V=(b-a)^2/12=4/12=0.333$: putting more mass near the centre reduces the variance, exactly as it should.
X03●L5 · slides 39–40Wind speed — are these data normal?
The data below are measurements of wind speed (km/h). Can they be generated by a normal distribution?
9.10, 1.15, 5.84, 3.19, 8.99, 2.54, 6.68, 2.12, 1.86, 1.45, 3.99, 12.40, 7.37, 0.45, 9.41, 0.96, 3.10, 0.02, 12.74, 4.07, 11.19, 0.19, 26.87, 1.28, 1.01, 0.70, 12.36, 4.58, 6.74, 1.12, 4.20, 0.47, 8.52, 6.66, 9.64, 9.97, 0.70, 2.73, 2.99, 9.66, 0.79, 2.37, 5.24, 3.33, 4.56, 12.89, 7.14, 10.46, 8.47, 7.14
Solution
  1. Load the data and plot a histogram. In R:
    x <- c(9.10, 1.15, 5.84, ... , 8.47, 7.14)
    hist(x, main = "Histogram of wind speed frequencies")
    or for relative frequencies, which is the right comparison against a density:
    hist(x, freq = FALSE, main = "Histogram of wind speed relative frequencies")
  2. Read the shape. Both commands produce an asymmetric, positively skewed histogram: a tall block of observations near zero, thinning steadily to the right, with a lone extreme value at 26.87.
  3. Conclusion: no. The data cannot be approximated by a normal distribution. A normal density is symmetric about its mean, so a visibly skewed histogram rules it out.
  4. Two further tells, without any software.
    · A normal variable takes negative values with positive probability. Wind speed cannot be negative, and the data crowd against that floor — sample mean $\approx5.6$ with several observations below 0.5, so $\mu-2\sigma$ would be comfortably negative.
    · For a symmetric distribution the mean and median coincide. Here the median is about 4.4 while the mean is about 5.6: mean $>$ median, the signature of positive skew (module 13).
  5. What would fit better? A distribution on $[0,\infty)$ with a right tail — an exponential, a gamma, or a Weibull, the last being the standard choice for wind speed in the energy literature.
Insight
The lesson is that "assume normality" is an assumption to be checked, not a default. A histogram takes one line of code and rules the normal in or out immediately. Contrast with X04 below, where the data are plausibly symmetric and the normal assumption is stated explicitly in the question.
X04●●L5 · slides 52–53Mr Velovelo attempting the world record
The data are the lap times (in seconds) of Mr Velovelo. Assuming his lap time follows a normal distribution, how likely is it that he beats the world record of 210 seconds?
228.861, 244.317, 248.266, 220.612, 253.844, 233.554, 248.115, 256.724, 231.907, 244.235, 257.001, 213.283, 270.720, 298.489, 269.188, 243.685, 258.572, 229.280, 287.557, 268.814, 265.747, 232.483, 256.399, 238.834, 243.771, 238.600, 229.485, 231.825, 245.802, 216.023, 262.152, 247.644, 263.983, 255.393, 259.886, 220.338, 229.595, 241.060, 252.193, 272.575, 244.201, 275.231, 259.508, 273.482, 252.539, 236.864, 220.372, 253.110
Solution

The parameters are not given — they must be estimated from the data, and only then used in the normal model.

  1. Import the data.
    time <- c(228.861, 244.317, 248.266, ... , 220.372, 253.110)
  2. Estimate the mean and the standard deviation.
    meantime <- mean(time)  → about 249 seconds
    stdtime <- sd(time)  → about 19 seconds
  3. Translate the question. "Beating the record" means a lap time below 210 seconds, so the quantity wanted is a CDF value: $$P(T<210)=F(210)\qquad\text{with }T\sim\mathcal{N}(\text{meantime},\ \text{stdtime}^2)$$
  4. Compute it.
    pnorm(210, meantime, stdtime) returns $\approx\mathbf{0.0196}$, i.e. a probability of about 2%.
  5. By hand, via standardisation (module 13), to see where the 2% comes from: $$z=\frac{210-249}{19}\approx-2.05\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(Z<-2.05)\approx0.02$$ The record sits about two standard deviations below his average lap — rare, but not unreachable.
Insight
Three steps, and step 1 is the one students skip: estimate the parameters, then apply the model. Note also the direction of the inequality — a faster time is a smaller number, so beating a record is a left-tail event, not a right-tail one. Reading the question in the wrong direction here would give $1-0.0196=98\%$, an answer that fails the plausibility check instantly.
X05●●L6 · slide 10A joint density: finding the constant, and a probability
The joint pdf of two continuous RVs $X$ and $Y$ is $$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}x+cy^2&0\le x\le1\text{ and }0\le y\le1\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$ 1. Find the constant $c$.   2. Find $P(0\le X\le\tfrac12,\ 0\le Y\le\tfrac12)$.
Solution
  1. 1. The constant is fixed by property 3 of a joint density (module 14): the total volume must be 1. $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1(x+cy^2)\,dy\,dx=1$$
  2. Inner integral over $y$, treating $x$ as constant: $$\int_0^1(x+cy^2)dy=\left[xy+\frac{cy^3}{3}\right]_0^1=x+\frac{c}{3}$$
  3. Outer integral over $x$: $$\int_0^1\left(x+\frac c3\right)dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{cx}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac12+\frac c3$$
  4. Set equal to 1 and solve: $$\frac12+\frac c3=1\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac c3=\frac12\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{c=\frac32}$$ So $f(x,y)=x+\tfrac32y^2$ on the unit square — which is exactly the density used in the follow-up exercise on slide 19 (see X06).
  5. Check property 2: $x+\tfrac32y^2\ge0$ on the unit square ✓
  6. 2. The probability is the volume over the smaller square $[0,\tfrac12]^2$, by property 1: $$P\!\left(0\le X\le\tfrac12,\,0\le Y\le\tfrac12\right)=\int_0^{1/2}\!\!\int_0^{1/2}\left(x+\tfrac32y^2\right)dy\,dx$$
  7. Inner integral over $y$ from 0 to $\tfrac12$: $$\int_0^{1/2}\left(x+\tfrac32y^2\right)dy=\left[xy+\frac{y^3}{2}\right]_0^{1/2}=\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{16}$$
  8. Outer integral over $x$ from 0 to $\tfrac12$: $$\int_0^{1/2}\left(\frac x2+\frac1{16}\right)dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{4}+\frac{x}{16}\right]_0^{1/2}=\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{32}=\frac{3}{32}$$
  9. $$P=\frac{3}{32}=\mathbf{0.09375}$$
Insight
A quarter of the square holds only 9.4% of the probability, not 25% — because this density is smallest near the origin ($f(0,0)=0$) and largest at the far corner ($f(1,1)=2.5$). Uniformity is an assumption, and here it fails. The same "integrate to 1 to pin down the constant" move appears whenever a density is given up to a constant.
X06●●L6 · slide 19Marginal densities of that joint pdf
With $f(x,y)=x+\tfrac32y^2$ on $0\le x\le1$, $0\le y\le1$ (and 0 otherwise), find the marginal pdfs $f_X(x)$ and $f_Y(y)$.
Solution
  1. Marginal of $X$: integrate the other variable out, using formula (3) of module 14: $$f_X(x)=\int_y f(x,y)\,dy=\int_0^1\left(x+\tfrac32y^2\right)dy=\left[xy+\frac{y^3}{2}\right]_0^1=x+\frac12$$ so $$\boxed{\;f_X(x)=x+\tfrac12,\qquad 0\le x\le1\;}$$ and 0 elsewhere.
  2. Check it is a density: $f_X\ge0$ on $[0,1]$ ✓, and $$\int_0^1\left(x+\tfrac12\right)dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac x2\right]_0^1=\frac12+\frac12=1\ \checkmark$$
  3. Marginal of $Y$: integrate $x$ out, using formula (5): $$f_Y(y)=\int_x f(x,y)\,dx=\int_0^1\left(x+\tfrac32y^2\right)dx=\left[\frac{x^2}{2}+\tfrac32y^2x\right]_0^1=\frac12+\frac32y^2$$ so $$\boxed{\;f_Y(y)=\tfrac12+\tfrac32y^2,\qquad 0\le y\le1\;}$$ and 0 elsewhere.
  4. Check: $\int_0^1\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac32y^2\right)dy=\left[\frac y2+\frac{y^3}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac12+\frac12=1$ ✓
  5. Are $X$ and $Y$ independent? Test the factorisation of module 15: $$f_X(x)f_Y(y)=\left(x+\tfrac12\right)\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac32y^2\right)=\frac x2+\frac32xy^2+\frac14+\frac34y^2$$ which is not equal to $x+\tfrac32y^2$ — for instance at $(0,0)$ the product gives $\tfrac14$ while $f(0,0)=0$. So $X$ and $Y$ are dependent.
Insight
The mechanical rule: to get the marginal of $X$, integrate out $y$ (and vice versa) — "sum out the other one", exactly as in the discrete table where you sum along a row. Always verify the result integrates to 1; it catches a mis-set limit immediately.
X07●●L6 · slide 25Expectation of a function of two variables
The joint pdf of $X$ and $Y$ is $f(x,y)=x+y$ for $0\le x\le1$ and $0\le y\le1$, and 0 otherwise. Find $E(XY^2)$ and $E(X^2)$.
Solution

Use the rule from module 14: $E[g(X,Y)]=\iint g(x,y)f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ — with the joint density, not the marginals.

  1. Sanity check on the density first. $\int_0^1\!\int_0^1(x+y)dx\,dy=\int_0^1\left(\tfrac12+y\right)dy=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$ ✓
  2. $E(XY^2)$ with $g(x,y)=xy^2$: $$E(XY^2)=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 xy^2(x+y)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\left(x^2y^2+xy^3\right)dx\,dy$$
  3. The integrand splits into two products of a function of $x$ and a function of $y$, so each double integral factorises: $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1x^2y^2\,dx\,dy=\left(\int_0^1x^2dx\right)\left(\int_0^1y^2dy\right)=\frac13\cdot\frac13=\frac19$$ $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1xy^3\,dx\,dy=\left(\int_0^1x\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^1y^3dy\right)=\frac12\cdot\frac14=\frac18$$
  4. Add: $$E(XY^2)=\frac19+\frac18=\frac{8}{72}+\frac{9}{72}=\boxed{\frac{17}{72}\approx0.2361}$$
  5. $E(X^2)$ with $g(x,y)=x^2$. Here the function involves only $x$, so you may equally use the marginal — but the joint works directly: $$E(X^2)=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1x^2(x+y)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1\left[x^2y+\frac{x^2y^2}{2}\right]_0^1dx=\int_0^1\left(x^3+\frac{x^2}{2}\right)dx$$
  6. $$=\left[\frac{x^4}{4}+\frac{x^3}{6}\right]_0^1=\frac14+\frac16=\frac{3}{12}+\frac{2}{12}=\boxed{\frac{5}{12}\approx0.4167}$$
  7. Cross-check via the marginal. $f_X(x)=\int_0^1(x+y)dy=x+\tfrac12$, so $$E(X^2)=\int_0^1x^2\left(x+\tfrac12\right)dx=\frac14+\frac16=\frac5{12}\ \checkmark$$
Insight
Note when the marginal is enough and when it is not. $E(X^2)$ depends on $X$ alone, so either route works. $E(XY^2)$ mixes the variables, and no combination of the two marginals can produce it — the joint is genuinely needed. That is precisely why covariance, which is built on $E[XY]$, requires the joint distribution.
X08●●●L6 · slide 60Conditional expectation and variance of an exponential, given $X>1$
Let $X$ be a continuous random variable with exponential distribution and expected value equal to one. Compute: 1. the pdf of $X$ given the event $X>1$; 2. $E[X\mid X>1]$; 3. $V[X\mid X>1]$. Use $$f(x\mid A)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(x)}{P(A)}&x\in A\\0&x\notin A\end{cases}$$
Solution
  1. Identify the parameter. For $X\sim Exp(\lambda)$, $E[X]=1/\lambda$ (module 13). Given $E[X]=1$ we get $\lambda=1$, so $$f(x)=e^{-x},\quad x>0,\qquad F(x)=1-e^{-x}$$
  2. Probability of the conditioning event. $$P(A)=P(X>1)=1-F(1)=1-(1-e^{-1})=e^{-1}\approx0.368$$
  3. 1. The conditional density. Apply the given formula on $A=\{x>1\}$: $$f(x\mid X>1)=\frac{e^{-x}}{e^{-1}}=e^{-(x-1)}\quad\text{for }x>1,\qquad 0\text{ otherwise}$$
  4. Check it is a density: $\int_1^{\infty}e^{-(x-1)}dx=\left[-e^{-(x-1)}\right]_1^{\infty}=0-(-1)=1$ ✓
  5. Recognise the shape. Substituting $u=x-1$ turns $f(x\mid X>1)$ into $e^{-u}$ for $u>0$ — the original $Exp(1)$ density, shifted right by 1. In other words, $$X\mid X>1\;\sim\;1+Exp(1)$$ This is the celebrated memorylessness of the exponential: given that you have already waited one unit, the remaining wait has exactly the original distribution.
  6. 2. Conditional expectation. Either read it off the shift, $E=1+E[Exp(1)]=1+1=\mathbf{2}$, or integrate directly with $u=x-1$: $$E[X\mid X>1]=\int_1^{\infty}x\,e^{-(x-1)}dx=\int_0^{\infty}(u+1)e^{-u}du=\underbrace{\int_0^\infty ue^{-u}du}_{=1}+\underbrace{\int_0^\infty e^{-u}du}_{=1}=2$$
  7. 3. Conditional variance. A shift by a constant does not change a variance, so $$V[X\mid X>1]=V[Exp(1)]=\frac{1}{\lambda^2}=\mathbf{1}$$ Directly, with $u=x-1$ and the conditional mean 2: $$V=\int_1^{\infty}(x-2)^2e^{-(x-1)}dx=\int_0^{\infty}(u-1)^2e^{-u}du=E[(U-1)^2]=V[U]=1\ \checkmark$$ since $U\sim Exp(1)$ has mean 1.
Insight — why memorylessness matters
For most distributions, having waited a long time changes your outlook. For the exponential it does not: $P(X>s+t\mid X>s)=P(X>t)$ for all $s,t>0$. A machine whose lifetime is exponential does not age. That is a strong — and often unrealistic — assumption, and it is the exponential's defining property: it is the only continuous distribution with it.
X09●●L6 · slide 62Marginals and conditionals from a $4\times4$ table
The joint distribution of $X$ and $Y$ is
X=1X=2X=3X=4
Y=10.100.080.100.07
Y=20.020.200.040.07
Y=30.000.040.050.05
Y=40.050.030.050.05
1. Find the marginal distributions of $X$ and $Y$.   2. Find the conditional distributions of $X$ given $Y=1$, and of $Y$ given $X=1$.
Solution
  1. 1. Marginal of $Y$ — sum each row (formula 4, module 14): $$P(Y=1)=0.10+0.08+0.10+0.07=0.35$$ $$P(Y=2)=0.02+0.20+0.04+0.07=0.33$$ $$P(Y=3)=0.00+0.04+0.05+0.05=0.14$$ $$P(Y=4)=0.05+0.03+0.05+0.05=0.18$$ Check: $0.35+0.33+0.14+0.18=1.00$ ✓
  2. Marginal of $X$ — sum each column (formula 2): $$P(X=1)=0.10+0.02+0.00+0.05=0.17$$ $$P(X=2)=0.08+0.20+0.04+0.03=0.35$$ $$P(X=3)=0.10+0.04+0.05+0.05=0.24$$ $$P(X=4)=0.07+0.07+0.05+0.05=0.24$$ Check: $0.17+0.35+0.24+0.24=1.00$ ✓
  3. 2. Conditional of $X$ given $Y=1$ — divide the $Y=1$ row by its total 0.35: $$P(X=1\mid Y=1)=\frac{0.10}{0.35}=\frac27\approx0.2857,\qquad P(X=2\mid Y=1)=\frac{0.08}{0.35}=\frac{8}{35}\approx0.2286$$ $$P(X=3\mid Y=1)=\frac{0.10}{0.35}=\frac27\approx0.2857,\qquad P(X=4\mid Y=1)=\frac{0.07}{0.35}=\frac15=0.20$$ Check: $0.2857+0.2286+0.2857+0.2000=1.0000$ ✓
  4. Conditional of $Y$ given $X=1$ — divide the $X=1$ column by its total 0.17: $$P(Y=1\mid X=1)=\frac{0.10}{0.17}\approx0.5882,\qquad P(Y=2\mid X=1)=\frac{0.02}{0.17}\approx0.1176$$ $$P(Y=3\mid X=1)=\frac{0.00}{0.17}=0,\qquad P(Y=4\mid X=1)=\frac{0.05}{0.17}\approx0.2941$$ Check: $0.5882+0.1176+0+0.2941=0.9999\approx1$ ✓
  5. Are they independent? No. For instance $P(X=1,Y=3)=0$ while $P(X=1)P(Y=3)=0.17\times0.14=0.0238\neq0$. A single zero cell whose margins are both positive is enough to rule out independence.
Insight
The whole exercise is divide the row (or column) by its own total. The direction of the division is the only thing to get right: conditioning on $Y=1$ fixes the row and lets $X$ vary, so the row is what gets normalised. If your numbers do not sum to 1, you divided by the wrong margin.
X10●●●L6 · slide 63A separable joint density: marginals, independence, conditional
Consider the joint pdf $$f(x,y)=\alpha\beta e^{-\alpha x-\beta y},\qquad x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ \alpha>0,\ \beta>0$$ 1. Find the marginal densities of $X$ and $Y$.   2. Are $X$ and $Y$ independent? Why?   3. Compute the conditional density $f(x\mid y)$.
Solution
  1. First, note the factorisation. Using $e^{-\alpha x-\beta y}=e^{-\alpha x}e^{-\beta y}$, $$f(x,y)=\underbrace{\alpha e^{-\alpha x}}_{\text{depends on }x}\cdot\underbrace{\beta e^{-\beta y}}_{\text{depends on }y}$$ This observation will answer all three parts.
  2. 1. Marginal of $X$ — integrate $y$ out over $[0,\infty)$: $$f_X(x)=\int_0^{\infty}\alpha\beta e^{-\alpha x}e^{-\beta y}dy=\alpha e^{-\alpha x}\underbrace{\int_0^{\infty}\beta e^{-\beta y}dy}_{=1}=\alpha e^{-\alpha x}$$ so $\boxed{X\sim Exp(\alpha)}$, with $f_X(x)=\alpha e^{-\alpha x}$ for $x\ge0$.
    The inner integral is 1 because $\beta e^{-\beta y}$ is itself an exponential density (module 13).
  3. Marginal of $Y$, symmetrically: $$f_Y(y)=\int_0^{\infty}\alpha\beta e^{-\alpha x}e^{-\beta y}dx=\beta e^{-\beta y}\underbrace{\int_0^{\infty}\alpha e^{-\alpha x}dx}_{=1}=\beta e^{-\beta y}$$ so $\boxed{Y\sim Exp(\beta)}$.
  4. 2. Independence. Apply the test (7) of module 15: $$f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)=\alpha e^{-\alpha x}\cdot\beta e^{-\beta y}=\alpha\beta e^{-\alpha x-\beta y}=f(x,y)\ \checkmark$$ The joint density equals the product of the marginals for every $(x,y)$, so $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
    Why: because the density separates into a function of $x$ times a function of $y$, over a rectangular region. That is exactly what independence means.
  5. 3. Conditional density. By formula (16) of module 16: $$f(x\mid y)=\frac{f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}=\frac{\alpha\beta e^{-\alpha x-\beta y}}{\beta e^{-\beta y}}=\alpha e^{-\alpha x}=f_X(x)$$ The conditioning variable cancels completely: $\boxed{f(x\mid y)=\alpha e^{-\alpha x}}$, independent of $y$.
  6. This is the general fact from module 16 in action: for independent variables, conditioning changes nothing. Learning $Y=y$ — for any $y$ — leaves the distribution of $X$ exactly as it was.
Insight — the separability test
A shortcut worth carrying: if a joint density factors as $g(x)h(y)$ over a rectangular region, the variables are independent, and the marginals are $g$ and $h$ up to normalising constants. Both conditions are needed. Contrast with X05–X06, where $f(x,y)=x+\tfrac32y^2$ is a sum, not a product, and the variables turned out to be dependent. And the region matters too: a density that is constant on the triangle $\{0\le y\le x\le1\}$ is not separable, because the limits of one variable depend on the other.
