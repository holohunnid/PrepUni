Independence

lecture 3, slides 9–17 · when conditioning changes nothing
READ FIRST The definition, and why it is stated as a ratio— ⊡ ✕

Module 05 showed that conditioning on $B$ generally changes the probability of $A$. Independence is the name for the case where it does not:

$$\text{$A$ is independent of $B$}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(A\mid B)=P(A)$$

That is, the occurrence of event $B$ does not affect the probability of event $A$. The lecture's example: $P(\text{I change my haircut}\mid\text{influencer changes haircut})=P(\text{I change my haircut})$ — knowing what the influencer did tells you nothing about me.

The definition is also a test: to decide whether $A$ is independent of $B$, compute $P(A\mid B)$ and check whether it equals $P(A)$. Two numbers, one comparison.

Independent ≠ mutually exclusive
These get confused constantly and they are almost opposites. If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive with $P(A)>0$, then knowing $B$ occurred tells you $A$ definitely did not: $P(A\mid B)=0\neq P(A)$. So disjoint events with positive probability are maximally dependent, never independent. Disjointness is about the sets; independence is about the numbers.
RULE Independence and joint probability— ⊡ ✕

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, their joint probability — the probability that both occur — is the product of their individual probabilities:

$$\boxed{\;P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)\;}\tag{3}$$

Proof. Chain the multiplicative rule (2) of module 05 with the definition of independence:

$$P(A\cap B)\;\overset{\text{def. cond. prob.}}{=}\;P(A\mid B)\,P(B)\;\overset{\text{independence}}{=}\;P(A)\,P(B)\qquad\square$$

Formula (3) is usually taken as the working definition, because it is symmetric in $A$ and $B$ and because it needs no division — it survives even when $P(B)=0$.

More than two events

If $E_1,E_2,\dots,E_K$ are $K$ independent events, the probability that they all occur is the product of their probabilities:

$$P(E_1\cap E_2\cap\dots\cap E_K)=P(E_1)\,P(E_2)\cdots P(E_K)$$

This is why the lecture remarks that it is easy to compute probabilities of independent events: an intersection of $K$ events, which in general requires knowing the whole dependence structure, collapses to $K$ separate numbers multiplied together. Module 08 builds the entire binomial distribution out of exactly this move.

Combine with De Morgan
Independence handles intersections; the complement rule handles "at least one". Together: $$P(\text{at least one of }E_1,\dots,E_K)=1-P(E_1^c)\cdots P(E_K^c)$$ using De Morgan (module 02) to turn the union's complement into an intersection, then independence to factor it. This is the standard opening move for any "at least one" problem.
EXERCISE (slides 11–12) · independence is symmetric

Show that if $A$ is independent of $B$ — i.e. $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ — then $B$ is independent of $A$, i.e. $P(B\mid A)=P(B)$.

Solution. Start from the definition of conditional probability applied to $P(B\mid A)$, then substitute.

  1. By definition, $P(B\mid A)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}$ (requiring $P(A)>0$).
  2. By the multiplicative rule, $P(A\cap B)=P(A\mid B)\,P(B)$. Substitute this into the numerator: $$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B)\,P(B)}{P(A)}$$
  3. Independence of $A$ from $B$ means $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. Substitute again: $$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A)\,P(B)}{P(A)}=P(B)\qquad\square$$
Why this matters
Independence looked like a one-sided statement — "$A$ is independent of $B$" — but it turns out to be perfectly symmetric. That is why we are entitled to say "$A$ and $B$ are two independent events", with no direction, and why the symmetric formula (3) is the better definition. The asymmetry was an illusion of the notation.
EXERCISE (slides 13–14) · two students

Two students register for the same course. The first attends 80% of the time, the second 60%. Their attendances are independent. Find the probability that, on a given day, at least one student will be present.

  1. Name the events. $A=\{\text{first student is present}\}$ with $P(A)=0.8$; $B=\{\text{second student is present}\}$ with $P(B)=0.6$.
  2. Translate "at least one". At least one present means the first, or the second, or both — the union: $$A\cup B=\{\text{at least one student is present}\}$$
  3. Apply the union rule (module 04), since the two events are certainly not disjoint — both students can show up: $$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$$
  4. Use independence to evaluate the intersection: $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$. So $$P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A)P(B)$$
  5. Numbers: $$P(A\cup B)=0.8+0.6-(0.8\times0.6)=1.4-0.48=\mathbf{0.92}$$

The complement route, which is faster and generalises to $n$ students:

  1. "At least one present" is the complement of "neither present" $=A^c\cap B^c$ (De Morgan).
  2. Independence of $A$ and $B$ carries over to their complements, so $$P(A^c\cap B^c)=P(A^c)P(B^c)=(1-0.8)(1-0.6)=0.2\times0.4=0.08$$
  3. $$P(A\cup B)=1-0.08=\mathbf{0.92}\ \checkmark$$
Insight
Both routes give 0.92, but only the second scales. With ten students you would need every pairwise, triple, … intersection for the first method (inclusion–exclusion), whereas the complement route stays a single product $1-\prod_i(1-p_i)$. Whenever you see "at least one", reach for the complement first.
EXERCISE (slides 15–17) · families with two children — the classic counter-intuitive one

Consider a large sample of families with two children, not twins (a first child, then a second). Assume a boy and a girl are equally likely.

  1. What is the probability that both children are girls given that the first child is a girl?
  2. You ask the father: "Do you have at least one daughter?" He replies "Yes!". Given this, what is the probability that both children are girls given that at least one child is a girl?

Most people guess 50% for both. Take your own guess first — then do the mathematics.

Set-up. Order matters (first child, then second), so the sample space has four equally likely outcomes, writing $G$ for girl and $B$ for boy:

$$\Omega=\{GG,\;GB,\;BG,\;BB\},\qquad P(\text{each})=\tfrac14$$

Define the three events:

$A=\{\text{both children are girls}\}$$A=\{GG\}$$P(A)=1/4$
$B=\{\text{first child is a girl}\}$$B=\{GG,GB\}$$P(B)=2/4=1/2$
$C=\{\text{at least one child is a girl}\}$$C=\{GG,GB,BG\}$$P(C)=3/4$

Note that $A\subseteq B$ and $A\subseteq C$, so $A\cap B=A$ and $A\cap C=A$, both equal to $\{GG\}$.

1. Both girls, given the first is a girl.

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}=\frac{P(A)}{P(B)}=\frac{1/4}{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}$$

2. Both girls, given at least one is a girl.

$$P(A\mid C)=\frac{P(A\cap C)}{P(C)}=\frac{P(A)}{P(C)}=\frac{1/4}{3/4}=\frac{1}{3}$$

Why the two answers differ — this is the whole point.

  1. The numerators are identical: both conditions are compatible only with the single outcome $GG$.
  2. The denominators are not. $B$ is a strict subset of $C$: $B=\{GG,GB\}$ does not contain $BG$, while $C=\{GG,GB,BG\}$ does.
  3. So $C$ leaves more outcomes that are not in $A$ alive — two of them ($GB$ and $BG$) rather than one ($GB$). A larger surviving world with the same favourable part means a smaller ratio, hence $P(A\mid C)<P(A\mid B)$.
  4. Concretely: "the first is a girl" pins down a specific child and leaves only the second free — a genuine coin flip, hence $1/2$. "At least one is a girl" pins down nothing; it merely eliminates $BB$, leaving three equally likely families of which one is $GG$ — hence $1/3$.
The lesson
The intuition that fails is treating "at least one is a girl" as though it identified a particular child. It does not. Always write the conditioning event out as an explicit set and count. The moment you see $\{GG,GB,BG\}$ on paper, the answer $1/3$ is unavoidable — and the guess of 50% becomes visibly a guess about $B$, not about $C$.
