03

What is a probability

lecture 2, slides 2–12 · the classical definition, its limits, and Kolmogorov's axioms
READ FIRST Two questions that look like one— ⊡ ✕

"What is a probability?" hides two separate questions, and the lecture separates them carefully:

  1. What rules must probabilities obey? — answered once and for all by Kolmogorov's three axioms. This is the mathematics, and it is what the rest of the course is built on.
  2. How do you actually put a number on a particular event? — answered differently by the classical, frequentist and subjective approaches. This is the modelling, and it happens before the mathematics starts.

Keeping them apart matters. The axiomatic definition does not say how to compute $P(\omega_i)$; it says what any assignment of numbers must satisfy in order to be called a probability. You supply the numbers from the problem — by counting equally likely cases, by observing frequencies, or by expert judgement — and the axioms then tell you what follows.

Fix the interpretation first
A probability is a number between 0 and 1 saying how likely or plausible it is that a given event will occur. Probability 0 = impossible; probability 1 = certain. In real problems we rarely know an event is impossible or certain, so the probability usually lies strictly between 0 and 1. And crucially: probability is defined for events, that is, for subsets of the sample space — which is why module 01 came first.
DEF The classical definition— ⊡ ✕

Assuming all elementary outcomes in the sample space are equally likely, the probability of an event $A$ is

$$P(A)=\frac{\#\text{ favorable outcomes}}{\#\text{ possible outcomes}}$$

where "favorable" means the number of elementary outcomes in $A$, and "possible" the total number in $\Omega$.

Stated precisely: let $\Omega=\{\omega_1,\omega_2,\dots,\omega_N\}$ have a finite number $N$ of elementary outcomes, all equiprobable. Then for the event $A=\{\omega_1,\dots,\omega_{N_A}\}$,

$$\boxed{\;P(A)=\frac{N_A}{N}\;}$$

Example

Rolling a fair die, $\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, so $N=6$. For $A=\{1,3,6\}$ we have $N_A=3$ and

$$P(A)=\frac36=\frac12,\qquad P(\{2\})=\frac16$$
Notation
We often write $P(2)$ instead of $P(\{2\})$ to keep formulas readable. Remember what it really means: probability is defined for events, i.e. for sets. The shorthand drops the braces, not the concept.

Where it works, and where it breaks

In its favour: when it applies, it is simple and gives a direct calculation — you count, you divide, you are done.

Its three limitations, exactly as the lecture lists them:

  • it requires the elementary outcomes to be all equally likely — useless for a loaded die, or for tomorrow's stock return;
  • it requires the sample space to have a finite number of elementary outcomes — useless for "number of customers", where $\Omega=\mathbb{N}$;
  • most awkwardly, it is circular: "equally likely" already presupposes the notion of probability that we are trying to define.

These problems are what gave rise to the frequentist approach (probability as a long-run relative frequency) and the subjective approach (probability as a coherent degree of belief) — and, for the mathematics, to the axiomatic approach below.

DEF The axiomatic definition — Kolmogorov, 1930— ⊡ ✕

A logical–mathematical structure for calculating probabilities, consistent with the classical, frequentist and subjective approaches alike.

Let $\Omega$ be a sample space and $A$ an event, i.e. a subset of $\Omega$. A probability measure $P$ is a function assigning to each event $A$ a number $P(A)$, satisfying three axioms (statements taken to be true without proof):

Axiom 1$0\le P(A)\le1$ — a probability is a number between 0 and 1
Axiom 2if $A\cap B=\emptyset$, then $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$
Axiom 3$P(\Omega)=1$ — one of the elementary outcomes must occur

Axiom 2 is the only one with a hypothesis attached, and that hypothesis — mutual exclusivity — is the single most-violated condition in elementary probability. Probabilities of disjoint events add. Probabilities of overlapping events do not (module 04 supplies the correction).

Two remarks the lecture makes explicitly
(i) The axioms do not require the elementary outcomes $\omega_i$ to be equally likely — this is exactly the restriction that crippled the classical definition, and it is gone. A loaded die is no problem. (ii) The axioms do not say how to compute $P(\omega_i)$ or $P(A)$ concretely; those numbers come from the classical, frequentist or subjective approach. The axioms tell you what to do with them once you have them.

What the axioms already give you

Let $\Omega=\{\omega_1,\dots,\omega_N\}$ and $A=\{\omega_1,\dots,\omega_{N_A}\}$ with $N_A<N$. Then

$$P(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_A}P(\omega_i)\tag{1}$$

Proof. The elementary outcomes making up $A$ are mutually exclusive — only one outcome occurs (module 01) — so Axiom 2, applied repeatedly, lets their probabilities be added. $\;\square$

And if the elementary outcomes happen to be equally likely, Axiom 3 forces $N$ equal numbers to sum to 1, so each is $1/N$ and (1) gives

$$P(\omega_i)=\frac1N,\qquad P(A)=\frac{N_A}{N}$$

which is the classical formula, now derived rather than assumed. The axiomatic approach contains the classical one as a special case — that is what "consistent with" means.

EXERCISE (slides 7–8) · rolling a die

Calculate the probability of the following events when rolling a fair die.

All six faces are equally likely, so use $P(A)=N_A/N$ with $N=6$ throughout. The work is in translating each phrase into a set.

  1. $A=$ "the result is an even number". As a set, $A=\{2,4,6\}$, so $N_A=3$ and $$P(A)=\frac36=\frac12$$
  2. $B=$ "the result is a number less than or equal to 4". $B=\{1,2,3,4\}$, so $N_B=4$ and $$P(B)=\frac46=\frac23$$
  3. $C=$ "the result is a number divisible by 2". $C=\{2,4,6\}$, so $N_C=3$ and $$P(C)=\frac36=\frac12$$
Insight
$A$ and $C$ are described by different English phrases but are the same set, so they are the same event and necessarily have the same probability. This is why the first step is always to write the set down — it exposes when two descriptions are secretly identical, and when they are not.
EXERCISE (slides 13–14) · presidential election

Four candidates A, B, C, D. Polling gives A a 20% chance of winning and B a 40% chance. What is the probability that A or B wins?

  1. Translate: the event of interest is the union $\{A\text{ wins}\}\cup\{B\text{ wins}\}$.
  2. Check disjointness — this is the step that licenses everything else. Only one candidate wins the election, so the two events cannot both occur: $$\{A\text{ wins}\}\cap\{B\text{ wins}\}=\emptyset$$
  3. Apply Axiom 2: $$P(A\text{ or }B\text{ win})=P(\{A\text{ wins}\})+P(\{B\text{ wins}\})=0.2+0.4=\mathbf{0.6}$$
Note what was not needed
The classical definition is useless here — the four candidates are certainly not equally likely, and the 20% and 40% come from polling (a frequentist/subjective input). But Axiom 2 applies regardless of where the numbers came from. That is the whole point of axiomatising.
EXERCISE (slides 15–16) · urn

An urn contains 10 red balls, 6 black balls and 8 green balls. Calculate the probability of drawing a red or black ball.

  1. Total number of balls: $10+6+8=24$. Assuming each ball is equally likely to be drawn, the classical definition applies with $N=24$.
  2. Name the events: $A=$ "a red ball is drawn", $B=$ "a black ball is drawn", $C=$ "a red or black ball is drawn", so $C=A\cup B$.
  3. Individual probabilities by counting: $$P(A)=\frac{10}{24},\qquad P(B)=\frac{6}{24}$$
  4. Disjointness check: a single drawn ball cannot be both red and black, so $A\cap B=\emptyset$ ✓ and Axiom 2 applies.
  5. $$P(C)=P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)=\frac{10}{24}+\frac{6}{24}=\frac{16}{24}=\frac{2}{3}$$
Sanity check
The green balls give $P(\text{green})=8/24=1/3$, and "red or black" is exactly the complement of "green". Indeed $2/3=1-1/3$ ✓ — the complement rule of module 04, arriving one module early.
