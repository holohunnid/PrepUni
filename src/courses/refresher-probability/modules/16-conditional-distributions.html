Conditional distributions

lecture 6, slides 49–63 · the bivariate case of conditioning
READ FIRST Module 05, one level up— ⊡ ✕

Module 05 conditioned one event on another. Now, given the bivariate (joint) distribution of $(X,Y)$, we condition one random variable on the other: the distribution of $Y$ given $X=x$, for each possible given value $x$ of $X$, and vice versa.

Everything is derived from the single formula of module 05 — the lecture says so in a footnote, and it is worth taking literally:

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$$

Put $A=\{Y=y\}$ and $B=\{X=x\}$ and you have the whole module. What is new is only that we now let $y$ vary and ask whether the resulting object is itself a distribution — it is — and then compute its mean and variance.

Three objects, one per level
Conditional distribution $P(Y=y\mid X=x)$: a whole distribution, for each fixed $x$. Conditional expectation $E[Y\mid X=x]$: its mean, one number per $x$. Conditional variance $V[Y\mid X=x]$: its spread, one number per $x$. Each is computed from the one above it, exactly as in the unconditional case.
DEF Conditional distribution — discrete— ⊡ ✕

Let $x$ be a given/fixed value among the possible values of the discrete RV $X$. The conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X=x$ is obtained from the conditional probabilities

$$\boxed{\;P(Y=y\mid X=x)=\frac{P(Y=y,\,X=x)}{P(X=x)}\;}\tag{13}$$

as $y$ varies among the possible values of $Y$. In words: take the row (or column) of the joint table and divide it by its own total. The joint probabilities are the numerators; the marginal is the normalising denominator.

Symmetrically, the conditional distribution of $X$ given $Y=y$ is

$$P(X=x\mid Y=y)=\frac{P(X=x,\,Y=y)}{\underbrace{P(Y=y)}_{=P_Y(y)=\sum_{x}P(X=x,Y=y)}}\tag{14}$$

where the denominator is the marginal of $Y$ from module 14.

It really is a probability distribution

The map $y\mapsto P(Y=y\mid X=x)$ satisfies both characterising properties of module 07:

  1. $0\le P(Y=y\mid X=x)\le1$ for each $y$ — it is a ratio of a joint probability to a larger marginal one.
  2. $\sum_y P(Y=y\mid X=x)=1$ — because summing the numerators over $y$ gives exactly the marginal $P(X=x)$ in the denominator, by formula (2) of module 14.

So a conditional distribution can be treated like any other distribution: it has a mean, a variance, quantiles, everything.

Divide by the right margin
Conditioning on $X=x$ divides by $P(X=x)$ — the column total in the real estate layout. Conditioning on $Y=y$ divides by $P(Y=y)$, the row total. Using the wrong margin is the standard slip, and the giveaway is that your numbers will not sum to 1.
DEF Conditional density — continuous— ⊡ ✕

For continuous variables, $P(X=x)=0$ (module 12), so formula (13) would be $0/0$. The definition is rebuilt with densities, and the shape is identical:

$$\boxed{\;f(y\mid x)=\frac{f(x,y)}{\underbrace{f_X(x)}_{=\int_yf(x,y)dy}}\;}\tag{15}$$

the conditional pdf of $Y$ given $X=x$, for $y\in\text{Range}(Y)$. Symmetrically,

$$f(x\mid y)=\frac{f(x,y)}{\underbrace{f_Y(y)}_{=\int_xf(x,y)dx}}\tag{16}$$

Divide the joint density by the marginal density of the variable being conditioned on — a slice through the joint surface, rescaled so its area is 1.

Conditioning on an event (slide 60)

The same idea handles conditioning a single continuous variable on an event $A$ such as $\{X>1\}$:

$$f(x\mid A)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(x)}{P(A)}&x\in A\\[6pt]0&x\notin A\end{cases}$$

Cut the density down to the region $A$ and rescale by $P(A)$ so the area is 1 again — the exact continuous analogue of "shrink the world and renormalise" from module 05. This formula solves the exponential exercise in module 18.

Conditioning and independence (slide 61)
From the factorisation (7) of module 15 it follows that if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $$P(Y=y\mid X=x)=P(Y=y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(y\mid x)=f_Y(y)$$ and likewise with the roles swapped. Interpretation: if the variables are independent, the probabilities of events relating to one are unrelated to the other — conditioning changes nothing, which is precisely the definition of independence from module 06, now stated for whole distributions rather than single events.

The conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X=2$ divides the $X=2$ column by its total, $P(X=2)=0.45$:

$$P(Y=1\mid X=2)=\frac{P(X=2,Y=1)}{P(X=2)}=\frac{0.20}{0.45}=0.44$$ $$P(Y=2\mid X=2)=\frac{P(X=2,Y=2)}{P(X=2)}=\frac{0.25}{0.45}=0.56$$

and it satisfies the fundamental property:

$$\sum_yP(Y=y\mid X=2)=\frac{0.20+0.25}{0.45}=\frac{0.45}{0.45}=1\ \checkmark$$

Compare with the unconditional marginal $P(Y=1)=0.40$, $P(Y=2)=0.60$: learning $X=2$ has shifted probability from $Y=2$ towards $Y=1$. That shift is the dependence measured by the covariance in module 15.

DEF Conditional expected value— ⊡ ✕

The conditional expected value is the expected value of the conditional distribution — computed with the conditional probabilities in place of the ordinary ones:

$$E[Y\mid X=x]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_y y\,P(Y=y\mid X=x)&Y\text{ discrete}\\[10pt]\displaystyle\int y\,f(y\mid x)\,dy&Y\text{ continuous}\end{cases}\tag{17}$$

and symmetrically $E[X\mid Y=y]=\sum_x x\,P(X=x\mid Y=y)$.  (18)

Use the conditional weights
The conditional expected value of $Y$ given $X=x$ must be computed using the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X=x$ — never the marginal, never the joint. This is the same warning as in module 14, one level down.

Real estate example (slide 56)

Using the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X=2$ found above:

$$E(Y\mid X=2)=1\cdot P(Y=1\mid X=2)+2\cdot P(Y=2\mid X=2)=1\cdot\frac{0.20}{0.45}+2\cdot\frac{0.25}{0.45}=\frac{0.20+0.50}{0.45}=\mathbf{1.56}$$

Compare with the unconditional $E(Y)=1.60$ from module 14: knowing that Swiss sales came in at 2 lowers the expectation for Italy slightly, consistent with the negative covariance.

DEF Conditional variance— ⊡ ✕

The conditional variance is the variance of the conditional distribution:

$$V[Y\mid X=x]=E\big[(Y-E[Y\mid X=x])^2\;\big|\;X=x\big]=\sum_y\big(y-E[Y\mid X=x]\big)^2P(Y=y\mid X=x)\tag{23}$$

(with an integral against $f(y\mid x)$ in the continuous case). As in module 11, the computation is simplified by the shortcut formula:

$$\boxed{\;V[Y\mid X=x]=E[Y^2\mid X=x]-\big(E[Y\mid X=x]\big)^2\;}$$

Exercise (slide 59): compute $V(Y\mid X=2)$

  1. The conditional distribution, as exact fractions: $$P(Y=1\mid X=2)=\frac{0.20}{0.45}=\frac49,\qquad P(Y=2\mid X=2)=\frac{0.25}{0.45}=\frac59$$
  2. Conditional mean: $$E[Y\mid X=2]=1\cdot\tfrac49+2\cdot\tfrac59=\tfrac{4}{9}+\tfrac{10}{9}=\tfrac{14}{9}\approx1.5556$$
  3. Conditional second moment: $$E[Y^2\mid X=2]=1^2\cdot\tfrac49+2^2\cdot\tfrac59=\tfrac49+\tfrac{20}{9}=\tfrac{24}{9}=\tfrac83$$
  4. Shortcut formula: $$V[Y\mid X=2]=\tfrac83-\left(\tfrac{14}{9}\right)^2=\tfrac{216}{81}-\tfrac{196}{81}=\frac{20}{81}\approx\mathbf{0.247}$$
  5. Check against the unconditional $V(Y)=0.24$ (module 14): conditioning barely changed the spread here, though it did move the mean.
KEY Marginal moments from conditional ones — the law of total expectation— ⊡ ✕

Slide 57 derives a formula worth isolating, because it is the engine behind a great deal of applied probability. To compute $E(X)$ you may go through the conditional expectations instead of the marginal distribution:

$$\begin{aligned} E(X)&=\sum_x x\,P(X=x)=\sum_x x\sum_y P(X=x,Y=y)&&\text{(marginal from joint, module 14)}\\ &=\sum_x\sum_y x\,P(X=x,Y=y)&&\\ &=\sum_x\sum_y x\,P(X=x\mid Y=y)\,P(Y=y)&&\text{(multiplicative rule, module 05)}\\ &=\sum_y\left(\sum_x x\,P(X=x\mid Y=y)\right)P(Y=y)&&\text{(swap the order of summation)}\\ &=\sum_y E(X\mid Y=y)\,P(Y=y)&&\text{(definition 18)} \end{aligned}$$

So:

$$\boxed{\;E(X)=\sum_y E(X\mid Y=y)\,P(Y=y)\;}$$

The unconditional mean is the probability-weighted average of the conditional means. In compact notation this is $E(X)=E\big[E(X\mid Y)\big]$, the law of iterated expectations. The same applies to $Y$, and the same argument works for any moment.

Structurally this is the fundamental property of a partition from module 02 — the events $\{Y=y\}$ partition the sample space, and the calculation splits $E(X)$ across the blocks and reassembles it.

Why it is useful
Often the conditional problem is easy and the unconditional one is not. "What is the expected loss on a loan?" is hard; "what is the expected loss given default?" times "the probability of default", summed over the two cases, is routine. Splitting into cases, solving each, and reassembling with the weights is exactly this formula.

Check yourself: in the real estate example, $E(Y\mid X=1)=\frac{0.10+2(0.25)}{0.35}=\frac{0.60}{0.35}=1.714$, $E(Y\mid X=2)=1.556$, $E(Y\mid X=3)=\frac{0.10+2(0.10)}{0.20}=1.5$. Weighting: $1.714(0.35)+1.556(0.45)+1.5(0.20)=0.600+0.700+0.300=1.60=E(Y)$ ✓
