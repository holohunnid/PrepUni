Joint distributions and marginals

lecture 6, slides 5–25 · two random variables at once
READ FIRST Why one variable is not enough— ⊡ ✕

Countless applications concern relationships among multiple variables: sales of various products of a company across markets, co-movements of stocks within the tech sector or across sectors, the relationship between the minimum wage and consumption. Modelling each variable separately throws away exactly the thing you care about — how they move together.

The tool is the joint probability distribution. Everything here is stated for two variables for simplicity, and generalises directly to $N>2$.

The module has three moving parts, and keeping them straight is most of the battle:

joint $P(x,y)$ or $f(x,y)$the probability (density) that $X=x$ and $Y=y$ togetherdescribes the relationship
marginal $P_X(x)$ or $f_X(x)$the distribution of $X$ on its own, ignoring $Y$obtained by summing/integrating $Y$ out
conditional $P(y\mid x)$the distribution of $Y$ once $X=x$ is knownmodule 16
The warning on slide 20
Given the joint distribution of $(X,Y)$, expected values, variances and higher moments of the individual variables must be defined with respect to the corresponding marginal distributions — not the joint one. Get this wrong and every subsequent number is wrong. The panel below makes the correct computation explicit.
DEF Joint distribution — discrete case— ⊡ ✕

Let $X$ and $Y$ be discrete random variables. We are interested in the joint event in which $\{X=x\}$ and $\{Y=y\}$ both occur simultaneously — that is, their intersection:

$$(X=x,\,Y=y):=\{X=x\}\cap\{Y=y\}$$

Its probability, as $x$ and $y$ vary, is the joint probability distribution:

$$P(x,y):=P(X=x,Y=y):=P\big(\{X=x\}\cap\{Y=y\}\big)$$

It describes the relationship between the two random variables.

Properties

1.$\displaystyle P(a<X\le b,\ c<Y\le d)=\sum_{a\le x\le b}\ \sum_{c\le y\le d}P(X=x,Y=y)$
2.$P(X=x,Y=y)\ge0$
3.$\displaystyle\sum_x\sum_y P(X=x,Y=y)=1$

These are the two-variable versions of the properties in module 07: non-negativity, and total mass 1 — now summed over a grid rather than a line.

DEF Joint density and joint CDF— ⊡ ✕

Continuous case. The joint probability density function of two continuous RVs $X$ and $Y$ is a function $f(x,y)$ satisfying:

1.$\displaystyle P(a<X\le b,\ c<Y\le d)=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$
2.$f(x,y)\ge0$
3.$\displaystyle\iint f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=1$

Same three properties, with a double integral replacing the double sum. Geometrically $f(x,y)$ is a surface over the plane, and probability is the volume underneath it over a rectangle — the two-dimensional version of "area under the curve". The bivariate normal surface on slide 9 is the standard picture: a bell-shaped hill centred at $(\mu_x,\mu_y)$.

Joint cumulative distribution function

The joint CDF of a pair $(X,Y)$ is

$$F(x,y):=P(X\le x,\ Y\le y)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\sum_{X\le x}\sum_{Y\le y}P(X=x,Y=y)&\text{discrete}\\[10pt] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{y}f(u,v)\,dv\,du&\text{continuous} \end{cases}\tag{1}$$

Being a cumulative distribution function, it satisfies the two fundamental properties: $0\le F(x,y)\le1$, and $F$ is non-decreasing in $x$ and in $y$ separately.

DEF Marginal distributions— ⊡ ✕

It is fundamental to distinguish the joint distribution of $X$ and $Y$ from the distribution of each random variable individually. How do you recover the latter from the former? Sum out the other variable in the discrete case, integrate out in the continuous case.

The idea, with the example (slide 14)

Consider the event $\{Y=2\}$. It is the union of the joint events $\{(X=x,Y=2)\}$ as $x$ ranges over all possible values of $X$:

$$\{Y=2\}=\{(X=1,Y=2)\}\cup\{(X=2,Y=2)\}\cup\{(X=3,Y=2)\}$$

These are mutually exclusive (different $x$ values cannot both occur), so Axiom 2 lets us add:

$$P(Y=2)=P(1,2)+P(2,2)+P(3,2)=0.25+0.25+0.10=0.60$$

Exercise on slide 14: calculate $P(X=1)$. Same argument, summing over $y$ this time:

$$P(X=1)=P(1,1)+P(1,2)=0.10+0.25=\mathbf{0.35}$$

Definition

Marginal of $X$, discrete$\displaystyle P_X(x):=P(X=x)=\sum_{y\in\text{Range}(Y)}P(X=x,Y=y)$(2)
Marginal of $X$, continuous$\displaystyle f_X(x)=\int_y f(x,y)\,dy$(3)
Marginal of $Y$, discrete$\displaystyle P_Y(y):=P(Y=y)=\sum_x P(X=x,Y=y)$(4)
Marginal of $Y$, continuous$\displaystyle f_Y(y)=\int_x f(x,y)\,dx$(5)

Why the name "marginal"? Because in a table like the one above, these distributions appear literally at the margins: $P(X=x)$ along the last row, $P(Y=y)$ down the last column. The name is a description of where they are printed.

Marginals are probability distributions in their own right

The map $y\mapsto P(Y=y)$ is itself a legitimate probability distribution of $Y$, since it satisfies both characterising properties of module 07:

  1. $0\le P(Y=y)\le1$ for every possible $y$ — true in the example, where $y=1$ gives 0.40 and $y=2$ gives 0.60.
  2. $\sum_y P(Y=y)=1$ — in the example, $0.40+0.60=1$ ✓

The same applies to $X$ (exercise, slide 18): $0.35+0.45+0.20=1.00$ ✓, and each entry lies in $[0,1]$ ✓. So the marginal of $X$ is a valid distribution, and every tool from modules 07–11 applies to it directly.

Marginals do not determine the joint
Going from joint to marginal loses information and cannot be reversed. Many different joint tables share the same margins $(0.35,0.45,0.20)$ and $(0.40,0.60)$ — one of them is the independent case where every cell is the product of its margins, but others encode strong dependence. This is the whole reason the joint distribution is needed: the margins alone cannot tell you how the variables co-move.
RULE Moments from a joint distribution— ⊡ ✕

To compute $E[X]$ and $V[X]$ from a joint distribution, use the marginal of $X$ — that is the warning of slide 20. Substituting the marginal formula (2) shows how it looks in terms of the joint:

Discrete (slide 21)

$$E[X]=\sum_x x\,P_X(x)=\sum_x x\left(\sum_y P(X=x,Y=y)\right)=\sum_x\sum_y x\,P(X=x,Y=y)$$ $$V[X]=\sum_x(x-E[X])^2P_X(x)=\sum_x\sum_y(x-E[X])^2P(X=x,Y=y)\tag{6}$$

Continuous (slide 22)

$$E[X]=\int_x x\,f_X(x)\,dx=\int_x x\left(\int_y f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx=\int_x\!\int_y x\,f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$ $$V[X]=\int_x(x-E[X])^2f_X(x)\,dx=\int_x\!\int_y(x-E[X])^2f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$

Both readings are correct and give the same number. Use whichever is convenient: if you already have the marginal, use it; if you only have the joint table, sum over both indices.

Expectation of a function of $(X,Y)$ — slide 23

To compute $E[g(X,Y)]$ for a real function $g$, you must use the joint distribution, not the marginals:

$$\boxed{\;E[g(X,Y)]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_x\sum_y g(x,y)\,P(X=x,Y=y)&\text{discrete}\\[10pt]\displaystyle\int_x\!\int_y g(x,y)\,f(x,y)\,dx\,dy&\text{continuous}\end{cases}\;}$$

This is unavoidable: a function like $g(x,y)=xy$ mixes the two variables, so knowing each margin separately is not enough. Module 15 uses exactly this with $g(x,y)=xy$ to compute covariance.
