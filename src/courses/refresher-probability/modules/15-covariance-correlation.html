Independence, covariance and correlation

lecture 6, slides 27–45 · quantifying how two variables move together
READ FIRST From "are they related?" to "how much?"— ⊡ ✕

Module 14 gave the joint distribution, which completely describes the relationship between $X$ and $Y$. That completeness is also its problem: a whole table or surface is too much to communicate. This module compresses it into single numbers.

  1. Independence — a yes/no question: does the joint factor into the marginals?
  2. Covariance — a number measuring the intensity of linear co-movement, but in awkward units.
  3. Correlation — the same information rescaled to lie in $[-1,1]$, hence comparable across problems.

And two facts that must be kept apart, because confusing them is the classic error:

independent $\Rightarrow$ uncorrelatedalways true
uncorrelated $\Rightarrow$ independentfalse in general (true only for jointly Gaussian variables)
DEF Independence of random variables— ⊡ ✕

$X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if, for every possible value $x$ and $y$:

$$P(X=x,Y=y)=P_X(x)\,P_Y(y)\qquad\text{discrete}\tag{7}$$ $$f(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\qquad\text{continuous}$$

or equivalently, in terms of cumulative distribution functions,

$$F(x,y)=F_X(x)\,F_Y(y)\tag{8}$$

In words: the joint factors into the product of the marginals.

"For every" is the whole content
Independence is a statement about all pairs $(x,y)$ simultaneously. To prove independence you must check every cell; to disprove it, one failing cell suffices. Exam questions are almost always the second kind.

Connection to independence of events (slide 28)

Definition (7) is nothing but the independence of events from module 06, applied to the events $\{X=x\}$ and $\{Y=y\}$:

  • Events: $A,B$ independent if $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$.
  • Variables: $X,Y$ independent if $P\big(\{Y=y\}\cap\{X=x\}\big)=P(Y=y)P(X=x)$ for every $x,y$.

The only new ingredient is the quantifier: one pair of events versus every pair generated by the two variables.

Checking it on the real estate table (slide 29)

Take the single cell $x=1$, $y=1$:

$$P(Y=1,X=1)=0.10\qquad\text{versus}\qquad P(Y=1)P(X=1)=0.40\times0.35=0.14$$

Since $0.10\neq0.14$, $X$ and $Y$ are not independent. One cell was enough; no need to check the other five.

DEF Covariance— ⊡ ✕

If two variables are not independent they display some form of dependence, relationship or co-movement. To quantify the linear part of that dependence, use the covariance.

The covariance between $X$ and $Y$ (discrete or continuous) is

$$\boxed{\;\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]\;}\tag{9}$$

where $\mu_X=E(X)$ and $\mu_Y=E(Y)$. It is a number, and it is defined only for pairs of random variables.

Reading the sign

  • If $X$ tends to take high values ($X>\mu_X$) when $Y$ takes high values ($Y>\mu_Y$), and low when $Y$ is low, then the product $(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)$ is a positive×positive or negative×negative — positive on average.
  • If instead $X$ tends to be high exactly when $Y$ is low (and vice versa), the product is positive×negative — negative on average.

The computational formula (Exercise, slide 34)

Derive from (9) the equivalent expression

$$\boxed{\;\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E(X)E(Y)=E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y\;}\tag{11}$$

Solution. Expand the product inside (9) and use linearity of the expected value, remembering $\mu_X,\mu_Y$ are constants:

$$E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]=E[XY-\mu_YX-\mu_XY+\mu_X\mu_Y]$$ $$=E[XY]-\mu_YE[X]-\mu_XE[Y]+\mu_X\mu_Y=E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y-\mu_X\mu_Y+\mu_X\mu_Y=E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y\;\square$$

Formula (11) is equivalent to (9) but usually easier to compute — exactly parallel to $V(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2$ in module 11.

Exercise (slide 34): what is $\text{Cov}(X,X)$? Put $Y=X$ in (9):

$$\text{Cov}(X,X)=E[(X-\mu_X)(X-\mu_X)]=E[(X-\mu_X)^2]=V(X)$$

The covariance of a variable with itself is its variance — so variance is a special case of covariance, not a separate concept.

EXERCISE (slide 35) · independent ⟹ uncorrelated

Prove that if $X$ and $Y$ are independent random variables, then $\text{Cov}[X,Y]=0$.

  1. By formula (11) it suffices to show $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$.
  2. Compute $E[XY]$ with $g(x,y)=xy$ using the rule of module 14: $$E[XY]=\sum_x\sum_y xy\,P(X=x,Y=y)$$
  3. Apply independence, $P(X=x,Y=y)=P_X(x)P_Y(y)$: $$=\sum_x\sum_y xy\,P_X(x)P_Y(y)$$
  4. The double sum now factorises, because each factor depends on one index only: $$=\left(\sum_x x\,P_X(x)\right)\left(\sum_y y\,P_Y(y)\right)=E[X]\,E[Y]$$
  5. Substituting into (11): $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E[X]E[Y]-\mu_X\mu_Y=0$ $\;\square$

(The continuous proof is identical with $\iint$ in place of $\sum\sum$.)

Insight
The factorisation in step 4 is where independence earns its keep. Without it the double sum cannot be separated, and there is no reason for the covariance to vanish. Note also what the proof does not show: that a zero covariance forces the joint to factor. It does not — see the counterexample below.

Question (slide 36): if $X$ is sales in CHF and $Y$ in EUR, what are the units of $\text{Cov}(X,Y)$?

CHF × EUR — million CHF times million EUR. A mixed-currency product with no economic meaning at all, and no way to judge whether $-0.06$ is a large or small dependence. That defect is precisely what the correlation coefficient fixes.

DEF The linear correlation coefficient— ⊡ ✕

The sign of the covariance indicates the direction of co-variation. Its magnitude, however, is not very informative, because it depends on the scales in which $X$ and $Y$ are measured. The fix is to divide by both standard deviations:

$$\boxed{\;\rho=\rho_{XY}:=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}\;}\tag{12}$$

where $\sigma_X=\sqrt{V(X)}$ and $\sigma_Y=\sqrt{V(Y)}$. Since $\text{Cov}$ carries the units of $X$ times the units of $Y$, and so does $\sigma_X\sigma_Y$, the ratio is dimensionless. It has the same sign as the covariance, is always between $-1$ and $1$, and is unaffected by any rescaling of the variables.

Interpretation

$\rho=1$perfect positive linear dependence
$\rho=-1$perfect negative linear dependence
$\rho=0$no linear dependence — $X$ and $Y$ are called uncorrelated
$-1\le\rho\le1$always, whatever the distributions
The word "linear" is not decoration
$\rho=0$ says there is no linear dependence. There might well be another kind — a strong, perfectly deterministic, non-linear one. Slide 39 shows four scatter plots all with $\rho=0$: a formless cloud, but also a clean parabola, an inverted parabola, and a wave. Correlation is blind to every one of the last three.

Real estate example, completed. With $V(X)=0.5275$ and $V(Y)=0.24$ from module 14:

$$\sigma_X=\sqrt{0.5275}\approx0.7263,\qquad\sigma_Y=\sqrt{0.24}\approx0.4899$$ $$\rho_{XY}=\frac{-0.06}{0.7263\times0.4899}=\frac{-0.06}{0.3558}\approx\mathbf{-0.169}$$

Now the number is readable: a weak negative relationship, about $-17\%$ of the way to perfect opposition — and this figure would be unchanged if the sales were re-expressed in thousands, or in dollars.

EXERCISE 16 (slide 41) · zero correlation does not imply independence

Consider the joint distribution

$$P(X=-1,Y=1)=P(X=0,Y=0)=P(X=1,Y=1)=\tfrac13$$

Show that $\rho_{XY}=0$ but $X$ and $Y$ are not independent.

  1. Marginals. $X$ takes $-1,0,1$ each with probability $1/3$. $Y$ takes the value 1 in two of the three cells and 0 in one, so $$P_Y(1)=\tfrac23,\qquad P_Y(0)=\tfrac13$$
  2. Means. By symmetry of the $X$ values about zero, $$\mu_X=(-1)\tfrac13+0\cdot\tfrac13+1\cdot\tfrac13=0,\qquad \mu_Y=1\cdot\tfrac23+0\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac23$$
  3. $E[XY]$, summing over the three cells with non-zero probability: $$E[XY]=(-1)(1)\tfrac13+(0)(0)\tfrac13+(1)(1)\tfrac13=-\tfrac13+0+\tfrac13=0$$
  4. Covariance and correlation. By (11), $$\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y=0-0\cdot\tfrac23=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\rho_{XY}=0\ \checkmark$$
  5. But they are not independent. Test the cell $(x,y)=(0,1)$: $$P(X=0,Y=1)=0\qquad\text{versus}\qquad P_X(0)\,P_Y(1)=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac29\neq0$$ Factorisation (7) fails, so $X$ and $Y$ are dependent ✓
Why the correlation missed it
Look at the three points: $(-1,1)$, $(0,0)$, $(1,1)$. They lie exactly on the parabola $Y=X^2$ — so $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$. Knowing $X$ tells you $Y$ with certainty; the dependence could not be stronger. Yet $\rho=0$, because the relationship is symmetric about $x=0$ and has no linear component: the upward arm and the downward arm cancel. Correlation measures straight-line association only, and a symmetric U-shape has none.
The one exception worth knowing
Slide 42: if the joint distribution of $X$ and $Y$ is Gaussian (bivariate normal), then uncorrelated does imply independent. This is a special property of the normal family and is why "uncorrelated" and "independent" get used interchangeably in Gaussian models — a habit that is safe there and dangerous everywhere else.
RULE Variance of a sum— ⊡ ✕

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two random variables with $\mu_X=E(X)$, $\mu_Y=E(Y)$. Then

$$\boxed{\;V(X+Y)=V(X)+V(Y)+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\;}$$

Proof. Apply the definition of variance and expand:

$$V(X+Y)=E\big[(X+Y-(\mu_X+\mu_Y))^2\big]=E\big[((X-\mu_X)+(Y-\mu_Y))^2\big]$$ $$=E\big[(X-\mu_X)^2+(Y-\mu_Y)^2+2(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]=V(X)+V(Y)+2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\;\square$$

using linearity of $E$ in the last step, plus definitions (6) and (9).

Two consequences and two exercises

  • If $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ then $V(X+Y)=V(X)+V(Y)$ — variances simply add. This is what justified $V=np(1-p)$ for the binomial in module 11: a sum of independent Bernoullis, hence zero covariances.
  • Exercise (slide 43): verify $V(X-Y)=V(X)+V(Y)-2\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)$.
    Solution: write $X-Y=X+(-Y)$ and use the rule above. Since $V(-Y)=(-1)^2V(Y)=V(Y)$ and $\text{Cov}(X,-Y)=-\text{Cov}(X,Y)$ (the sign pulls out of the linear expression (9)), the cross-term flips sign. Note the minus: positively correlated variables have a less variable difference.
  • Exercise (slide 43), general form: verify $$\boxed{\;V(aX+bY)=a^2V(X)+b^2V(Y)+2ab\,\text{Cov}(X,Y)\;}$$ Solution: the deviation of $aX+bY$ from its mean is $a(X-\mu_X)+b(Y-\mu_Y)$. Squaring gives $a^2(X-\mu_X)^2+b^2(Y-\mu_Y)^2+2ab(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)$, and taking expectations term by term gives the result. Note the constants come out squared — unlike in $E(aX+bY)=aE(X)+bE(Y)$, where they come out linearly.

Drag $\rho$ from $+1$ down to $-1$ at fixed weights and watch the red curve narrow: the portfolio's standard deviation falls from the weighted average of the two (at $\rho=1$, no diversification at all) towards its minimum at $\rho=-1$. That narrowing is diversification, and the formula responsible is $V(aX+bY)=a^2V(X)+b^2V(Y)+2ab\,\text{Cov}$ — only the last term depends on $\rho$.

At the exercise's values ($\rho=0.3$, $w_1=0.6$) the readout gives $E[Y]=0.022$ and $\sigma_Y\approx0.1870$, i.e. $Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0.02,\,0.1869^2)$ as on slide 45 — and note $\sigma_Y$ sits below the weighted average $0.6(0.10)+0.4(0.40)=0.22$. Module 17 turns this distribution into a Value at Risk.
