Continuous random variables

lecture 5, slides 4–17 · when every single value has probability zero
READ FIRST What changes, and what does not— ⊡ ✕

The good news first: many features of discrete random variables — the CDF, expected value, variance, quantiles — also exist for continuous random variables and have exactly the same meaning. You are not starting over.

What changes is one thing, and everything in this module follows from it:

  • Unlike discrete RVs, a continuous random variable can take any value in an interval of real numbers, say $(a,b)$, so its possible realisations are infinitely many — uncountably so.
  • Unlike discrete RVs, for every continuous random variable $X$: $$\boxed{\;P(X=x)=0\quad\text{for any possible realisation }x\text{ of }X\;}$$

That second bullet demolishes the tool of module 07: the probability distribution $P(x)=P(X=x)$ is identically zero and tells you nothing. Two replacements are needed, and the lecture supplies both:

  1. the cumulative distribution function $F(x)=P(X\le x)$, which carries over unchanged and is now the primary object;
  2. the probability density function $f(x)$, a new object which is not a probability but whose area is.
Why $P(X=x)=0$ is not absurd
Ask for the probability that a delay is exactly 15.000000… seconds, to infinite precision. There are uncountably many candidate values and their probabilities must sum to 1, so each individual one must be zero. What is meaningful is a range: $P(14.5<X<15.5)$. Probability for continuous variables lives on intervals, never on points.
DEF The cumulative distribution function— ⊡ ✕

Exactly the same concept as in module 07. The CDF of a continuous random variable $X$ is

$$F(x)=P(X\le x)$$

and it satisfies the same two characterising properties, plus two limits worth memorising:

1)$0\le F(x)\le1$ for every value $x$
2)$F$ is increasing: $x_1<x_2\Rightarrow F(x_1)\le F(x_2)$
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0,\qquad\lim_{x\to+\infty}F(x)=1$

The one visible difference from the discrete case: the staircase of module 07 becomes a smooth S-shaped curve, with no jumps. Jumps are precisely where a discrete variable puts positive probability on a single point, and a continuous variable never does.

Probabilities of intervals

For a discrete or continuous RV, the probability that $X$ falls in $(a,b]$ is a difference of CDF values:

$$\boxed{\;P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a),\qquad\text{for any }a<b\;}\tag{1}$$

Proof (slide 9). Split the event $\{X\le b\}$ into two disjoint pieces using the value $a$:

$$\{X\le b\}=\{X\le a\}\cup\{a<X\le b\}$$

These are mutually exclusive, so by Axiom 2,

$$F(b)=P(X\le b)=P(X\le a)+P(a<X\le b)=F(a)+P(a<X\le b)$$

and rearranging gives (1). $\;\square$ Taking $a=-\infty$ recovers the trivial case

$$P(-\infty<X\le b)=F(b)-F(-\infty)=F(b)-0=F(b)\tag{2}$$
Strict versus weak inequalities
For a continuous RV the two are interchangeable, because the single point contributes nothing: $$F(x)=P(X\le x)=P(X<x),\qquad P(a<X<b)=P(a\le X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)$$ For a discrete RV this is false. Take the distribution with $P(-2,-1,0,1,2)=(0.2,0.3,0.2,0.2,0.1)$ and $F(1)=0.9$: here $P(X\le1)=0.9$ but $P(X<1)=P(X\le0)=F(0)=0.7$. The mass sitting exactly at 1 is the difference. Getting sloppy with $<$ versus $\le$ costs marks only in the discrete case — but it costs them reliably.
DEF The probability density function— ⊡ ✕

The probability density function (pdf) is defined only for continuous random variables. It plays a role similar to the probability distribution $P(x)$ of a discrete RV, although it is not a probability — it is a probability density.

Let $X$ be a continuous RV with CDF $F$, assumed differentiable. The pdf $f(x)$ is the derivative of the CDF:

$$\boxed{\;f(x)=F'(x)\;}$$

So $f(x)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the cumulative distribution function at the point $x$: it measures how fast probability is accumulating there. Where $F$ rises steeply, $f$ is large; where $F$ is flat, $f$ is near zero.

A density is a probability per unit length
This is why $f(x)$ may exceed 1 without contradiction. A uniform variable on $[0,0.5]$ has $f(x)=2$ everywhere on that interval — perfectly legitimate, because what must not exceed 1 is the area, not the height. Only areas are probabilities.

Probability as area under the density

Combine (1) with the fundamental theorem of calculus, $F'=f$:

$$\boxed{\;P(a<X<b)=F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\;}\tag{4}$$

The probability that $X$ takes a value in the interval $(a,b)$ equals the area under the probability density function between $a$ and $b$. With $a=-\infty$:

$$P(X<b)=F(b)=\int_{-\infty}^{b}f(x)\,dx$$

so the CDF at $b$ is the area under the pdf up to $b$ — the two functions are two views of the same information, related by differentiation one way and integration the other.

This also explains $P(X=x)=0$ from the top of the module: a single point is an interval of zero width, and $\int_a^a f=0$.

Two characterising properties

Exactly parallel to the discrete case, with an integral replacing the sum:

$$\text{1)}\quad f(x)\ge0\ \text{ for every }x,\qquad\qquad\text{2)}\quad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1$$

Property 1 says $F$ is non-decreasing (its slope is never negative); property 2 says the total area is 1, the continuous version of "the probabilities sum to 1". Any candidate density must be checked against both — that check is what determines the constant $c$ in several exercises later.

The working rule
Whenever a continuous problem asks for a probability, convert it into a difference of CDF values — $P(a<X<b)=F(b)-F(a)$, $P(X>a)=1-F(a)$ — and then evaluate $F$ from a formula or from software (punif, pexp, pnorm). Every exercise in module 13 is that single move plus arithmetic.
