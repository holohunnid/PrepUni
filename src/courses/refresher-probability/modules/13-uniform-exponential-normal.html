Every question reduces to one of three moves
(i) A probability: convert to $F(b)-F(a)$. (ii) A parameter: you are given one probability and must invert to find $\lambda$, $\mu$ or $\sigma$. (iii) A quantile: you are given a probability and must find the value $q_\alpha$ with $F(q_\alpha)=\alpha$. Identify which of the three you face before computing anything.
DEF Expected value and variance of a continuous RV— ⊡ ✕

The expected value and variance of continuous random variables have exactly the same meaning as in the discrete case. Only the machinery changes: the sum over values becomes an integral over the density.

continuousdiscrete (module 10–11)
Expected value$\displaystyle E(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,f(x)\,dx$$\displaystyle E(X)=\sum_x x\,P(x)$
Variance$\displaystyle V(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(x-E(X))^2f(x)\,dx$$\displaystyle V(X)=\sum_x(x-E(X))^2P(x)$

Everything proved in modules 10–11 carries over verbatim, because the proofs used only linearity and the fact that the weights sum (integrate) to 1:

  • $E(a+bX)=a+b\,E(X)$ and $E(X+Y)=E(X)+E(Y)$;
  • the shortcut $V(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2$, with $E(X^2)=\int x^2f(x)\,dx$;
  • $V(X)\ge0$, and $\sigma=\sqrt{V(X)}$ in the units of $X$.
DEF Uniform distribution— ⊡ ✕

A continuous RV has a uniform distribution on the interval $[a,b]$, written $X\sim U(a,b)$, if

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{b-a}&a\le x\le b\\[6pt]0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} \qquad F(x)=\begin{cases}0&x<a\\[4pt]\dfrac{x-a}{b-a}&a\le x\le b\\[6pt]1&x>b\end{cases}$$

The density is a flat rectangle of height $1/(b-a)$ and width $b-a$ — area exactly 1, as property 2 demands. The CDF rises linearly from 0 to 1 across the interval.

Quick question (slide 43): can $b<a$? No. The interval $[a,b]$ would be empty and the height $1/(b-a)$ would be negative, violating $f\ge0$. The definition requires $a<b$.

Moments (slides 43–44)

$$E(X)=\int_a^b x\,\frac{1}{b-a}\,dx=\frac{1}{b-a}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_a^b=\frac{1}{b-a}\cdot\frac{b^2-a^2}{2}=\boxed{\frac{a+b}{2}}$$

using $b^2-a^2=(b-a)(b+a)$. The mean is the midpoint — obvious from symmetry, and a good check that the integral was done right.

$$V(X)=\int_a^b\left(x-\frac{a+b}{2}\right)^2\frac{1}{b-a}\,dx=\boxed{\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}}$$

The variance depends only on the width of the interval, not on where it sits — shifting a uniform variable changes its mean but not its spread, as it must.

DEF Exponential distribution— ⊡ ✕

A random variable with an exponential distribution takes only positive values and is often used to model a waiting time. $T$ follows an exponential distribution with parameter $\lambda>0$, written $T\sim Exp(\lambda)$, if

$$f(t)=\begin{cases}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}&t>0\\0&t<0\end{cases}$$

A single parameter $\lambda>0$ characterises it. Larger $\lambda$ means faster decay, i.e. shorter typical waits.

The CDF (Exercise, slides 25–26)

Show that for $t>0$,

$$F(t)=P(T<t)=\int_0^t\lambda e^{-\lambda u}\,du=1-e^{-\lambda t}$$

Solution. An antiderivative of $e^{-\lambda u}$ is $e^{-\lambda u}/(-\lambda)$, so

$$\int_0^t\lambda e^{-\lambda u}du=\lambda\left[\frac{e^{-\lambda u}}{-\lambda}\right]_0^t=\lambda\left(\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{-\lambda}-\frac{1}{-\lambda}\right)=1-e^{-\lambda t}\qquad\square$$

Sanity checks: $F(0)=1-e^0=0$ ✓ and $F(t)\to1$ as $t\to\infty$ ✓. In Wolfram Alpha: Integrate lambda e^(-lambda u) for u from 0 to t.

Moments (slide 45)

$$E(T)=\int_0^{\infty}t\,\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda}},\qquad V(T)=\int_0^{\infty}\left(t-\frac1\lambda\right)^2\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\boxed{\frac{1}{\lambda^2}}$$

So $\sigma_T=1/\lambda=E(T)$: for an exponential the standard deviation equals the mean. And the interpretation of $\lambda$ falls out — if events arrive at a Poisson rate of $\lambda$ per unit time (module 09), the average wait between them is $1/\lambda$ units of time. The two distributions are two views of the same process.

EXERCISE (slides 27–28, 31) · median and quantiles of the exponential

The median $m$ is the value such that $P(T<m)=0.5$. Compute it for $T\sim Exp(\lambda)$.

  1. By definition $F(m)=0.5$, so using the CDF just derived: $$1-e^{-\lambda m}=0.5\;\Longrightarrow\;e^{-\lambda m}=0.5$$
  2. Take logarithms of both sides: $$-\lambda m=\log\!\left(\tfrac12\right)\;\Longrightarrow\;m=-\frac{\log(1/2)}{\lambda}$$
  3. Since $-\log(1/2)=\log 2$: $$\boxed{\;m=\frac{\log 2}{\lambda}\;}$$
  4. Example with $\lambda=1.5$: $m=\log(2)/1.5=0.693/1.5\approx0.46$.

The general quantile (slide 31). The quantile at level $\alpha\in(0,1)$, written $q_\alpha$, is the value with $P(T\le q_\alpha)=\alpha$, i.e. $F(q_\alpha)=\alpha$. Same algebra:

$$1-e^{-\lambda q_\alpha}=\alpha\;\Longrightarrow\;e^{-\lambda q_\alpha}=1-\alpha\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{q_\alpha=-\frac{\ln(1-\alpha)}{\lambda}}$$

Setting $\alpha=0.5$ recovers $q_{0.5}=-\ln(1/2)/\lambda=\ln(2)/\lambda=m$ ✓

Insight
Note $m=0.693/\lambda$ is below the mean $1/\lambda$ — the exponential is right-skewed, with a long tail of rare very long waits pulling the average above the typical value. Median $<$ mean is the signature of positive skew, and it is exactly what the wind-speed exercise in module 18 detects in real data.
EXERCISE (slides 20–21) · market order execution delay

A market order suffers a random processing delay $X$ (in seconds) uniformly distributed between 0 and 60: $X\sim U[0,60]$.

  1. The density. With $a=0$, $b=60$: $$f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{60}&0\le x\le60\\0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}$$
  2. Executed within the first 15 seconds. Integrate the density, i.e. take the area of a rectangle: $$P(X\le15)=\int_0^{15}\frac{1}{60}dx=\left[\frac{x}{60}\right]_0^{15}=\frac{15}{60}=\mathbf{0.25}$$ In R: punif(15, 0, 60).
  3. Takes more than 45 seconds. $$P(X>45)=\int_{45}^{60}\frac{1}{60}dx=\frac{60-45}{60}=\frac{15}{60}=\mathbf{0.25}$$ In R: 1 - punif(45, 0, 60). Equal to the previous answer by symmetry — both are 15-second windows out of 60.
  4. Average waiting time. The mean of a uniform is the midpoint: $$E[X]=\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{0+60}{2}=\mathbf{30}\text{ seconds}$$
The modelling assumption
This model is used in market microstructure for latency delays (network or processing), where uniformity is assumed for simplicity when the exact behaviour of the system between the minimum and maximum waiting times is unknown. It is a statement of ignorance, not of knowledge — which is exactly when a uniform is the right default.
DEF The normal (Gaussian) distribution— ⊡ ✕

The probability density function of a normal random variable is

$$\boxed{\;f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\;e^{-\frac12\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}\;}\tag{5}$$

where $-\infty<x<\infty$ are the possible values $X$ can take. The distribution is characterised by two parameters, $\mu=E[X]$ and $\sigma^2=V(X)>0$, and we write $X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$.

Being a density, $f$ satisfies both properties: $f(x)\ge0$ everywhere ✓ (an exponential is never negative) and $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)dx=1$ ✓ (the constant $1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$ is precisely what makes the area 1).

Its moments are $E(X)=\mu$ and $V(X)=\sigma^2$ — the parameters are the mean and variance, which is what makes the notation so convenient.

Reading the two parameters off the picture (slide 34): changing $\mu$ slides the bell left or right without changing its shape; changing $\sigma$ widens or narrows it around a fixed centre, flattening as it widens so the area stays 1.

Switch families and watch what stays the same: in every case the total area is 1, the shaded part is $F(b)$, and the unshaded part is $P(X>b)=1-F(b)$. Only the shape changes — symmetric bell, one-sided decay, flat rectangle.

The presets load the exercises worked below and in module 18. "Heights" puts $\mu=176$, $\sigma=6.5$, cut at 180, and the readout shows $F(180)\approx0.731$, so $P(X>180)\approx0.269$. "Post office" uses $\lambda=0.154$ with the cut at 1 minute, giving $\approx0.143$.

EXERCISE (slides 35–36) · heights of young people

Heights are normally distributed with mean 176 cm and standard deviation 6.5 cm: $X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu=176,\ \sigma^2=6.5^2)$.

  1. a) Taller than 180 cm. Convert to a CDF question using the complement: $$P(X>180)=1-P(X\le180)=1-F(180)$$ In R: pnorm(180, mean=176, sd=6.5) returns $\approx0.731$, so $$P(X>180)=1-0.731\approx\mathbf{0.269}$$
  2. b) Between 160 cm and 190 cm. A difference of CDF values (formula 1, module 12): $$P(160<X<190)=F(190)-F(160)$$ In R: pnorm(190, 176, 6.5) - pnorm(160, 176, 6.5) $\approx\mathbf{0.9773}$.

By hand, via standardisation. Any normal can be reduced to the standard normal $Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ through $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma$:

$$P(X>180)=P\!\left(Z>\frac{180-176}{6.5}\right)=P(Z>0.615)\approx0.269\ \checkmark$$ $$P(160<X<190)=P\!\left(\frac{-16}{6.5}<Z<\frac{14}{6.5}\right)=P(-2.46<Z<2.15)\approx0.9773\ \checkmark$$
Insight
Standardisation is why a single table of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ values sufficed for a century: every normal question becomes a standard-normal question after subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. The quantity $z=(x-\mu)/\sigma$ says how many standard deviations from the mean the value $x$ lies — the only thing a normal distribution cares about.
EXERCISE (slides 37–38) · the FBI interrogating a suspect

The FBI checks a suspect's heart rate, on the theory that people who lie under stress have an accelerated rate. Adult men average 70 bpm with standard deviation 10 bpm. The FBI records more than 80 bpm. Assuming heart rates are normal, what should they conclude?

  1. Restate the question properly. It is not "is he lying?" but: how unusual is it for an adult man to have 80 bpm?
  2. Let $X$ be beats per minute, $X\sim\mathcal{N}(70,10^2)$. Note $P(X=80)=0$, since $X$ is continuous — so asking for the probability of exactly 80 is useless. The meaningful quantity is the tail: $$P(X>80)=1-F(80)$$
  3. Standardising: $z=(80-70)/10=1$, so $P(X>80)=P(Z>1)\approx0.159$.
    In R: 1 - pnorm(80, mean=70, sd=10) returns $\approx\mathbf{0.159}$.
  4. Conclusion. About 16% of adult men can have more than 80 bpm — roughly one in six. Based on this information, 80 bpm does not appear to be so unusual: there is not enough evidence to conclude that the suspect is lying.
The reasoning to copy
Same logic as the frogs in module 08, opposite verdict. Compute how likely the observation would be if nothing unusual were happening; reject that assumption only if the answer is small. Here 16% is not small, so the evidence is weak. Note also what the calculation does not establish — a high heart rate would not prove lying either, only that the reading is unusual. Statistical evidence bounds plausibility; it does not identify causes.
