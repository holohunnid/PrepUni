02

Partitions and De Morgan's laws

lecture 1, slides 14–25 · how to break any event into disjoint pieces
READ FIRST The engine room of the whole course— ⊡ ✕

This module looks like bookkeeping and is not. It contains the two tools that make probability computable:

  1. A partition lets you cut any event into non-overlapping pieces. Since Kolmogorov's second axiom only permits adding probabilities of disjoint events, the ability to manufacture disjointness on demand is what turns the axioms into a calculus. Every "law of total probability" argument you will ever meet is this idea.
  2. De Morgan's laws convert between "not (this and that)" and "(not this) or (not that)". They are the reason the complement rule is so powerful: a question about a hard union can be flipped into a question about an easy intersection, and vice versa. The meteoSvizzera exercise in module 04 is solved almost entirely by one application of De Morgan.
The lecture's own metaphor
"A partition is like a pizza slicing rule." The slices must cover the whole pizza and must not overlap. Any topping you care about is then divided cleanly among the slices — which is exactly the fundamental property below.
RULES Distributive property and $n$ sets— ⊡ ✕

For any sets $A$, $B$, $C$:

$$A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)$$ $$A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)$$

These are the set analogues of the distributive law $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ for numbers — except that here it works both ways round, with $\cup$ distributing over $\cap$ as well as $\cap$ over $\cup$. Arithmetic has no counterpart to the first line.

Verifying the second one with Venn diagrams (Exercise, slides 14–15)

Draw three overlapping circles and shade in stages.

  1. Left side. First shade $B\cup C$: everything inside either circle $B$ or circle $C$. Then intersect with $A$: keep only the part of that shading that also lies inside $A$. What survives is the sliver of $A$ overlapping $B$, plus the sliver of $A$ overlapping $C$ (with the triple overlap counted once).
  2. Right side. Shade $A\cap B$ — the lens where $A$ and $B$ meet. Separately shade $A\cap C$. Their union is: the $A$–$B$ lens plus the $A$–$C$ lens.
  3. The two shaded regions are the same region, so the identity holds. The first identity is verified the same way, swapping the roles of $\cup$ and $\cap$.

Operations on $n$ sets

Everything generalises from two or three sets to $n$ with no change of meaning:

$$A_1\cup A_2\cup\dots\cup A_n=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_i,\qquad A_1\cap A_2\cap\dots\cap A_n=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_i$$

The union is "at least one of the $A_i$ occurs"; the intersection is "all of them occur". This notation is what will let us compute probabilities of non-trivial events later, such as "at least one of 800 inspected chips is defective" in module 08.

DEF Partition— ⊡ ✕

A partition of the sample space $S$ is a set of nonempty events that are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. Precisely, $\{A_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ is a partition of $S$ if:

  1. each $A_i$ is a nonempty subset of $S$, for $i=1,\dots,n$;
  2. $A_i\cap A_j=\emptyset$ for every $i\neq j$  (mutually exclusive — no overlaps);
  3. $A_1\cup A_2\cup\dots\cup A_n=S$  (collectively exhaustive — no gaps).

Conditions 2 and 3 are exactly the two properties introduced at the end of module 01, now demanded together. That combination is what makes a partition useful: whatever happens, exactly one $A_i$ occurs — never zero (by 3), never two (by 2).

Example (slide 17)

Rolling a die, $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, with $A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{1,3,5\}$, $C=\{6\}$.

$\{A,B\}$is a partition: $A\cap B=\emptyset$ ✓ and $A\cup B=S$ ✓
$\{B,C\}$not a partition: disjoint ✓, but $B\cup C=\{1,3,5,6\}\neq S$ — gaps at 2 and 4
$\{A,B,C\}$not a partition: exhaustive ✓, but $A\cap C=\{6\}\neq\emptyset$ — an overlap

Both failure modes are represented, which is why the lecture chose these three sets.

Check both conditions, every time
A collection can pass one and fail the other, as $\{B,C\}$ and $\{A,B,C\}$ show. "Disjoint" alone is not a partition; "covers everything" alone is not either.
EXERCISE (slides 18–19) · all partitions of $S=\{3,4,5\}$

Write all possible partitions of the set $S=\{3,4,5\}$.

Solution. Work by the number of blocks, and remember that blocks must be nonempty, disjoint, and cover everything.

  1. Three blocks — every element alone: $\;\{3\},\{4\},\{5\}$. Only one way to do it.
  2. Two blocks — one pair together, the remaining element alone. There are three ways to choose which pair: $$\{3,4\},\{5\}\qquad \{3\},\{4,5\}\qquad \{3,5\},\{4\}$$
  3. One block — everything together: $\;\{3,4,5\}=S$. Trivially a partition (disjointness is vacuous with a single block, and it covers $S$).

So there are five partitions in total. Each of them slices $S$ so that exactly one block contains any given outcome — verify this on, say, the outcome 4 in each of the five.

Insight
The count of partitions of an $n$-element set is the Bell number $B_n$: $B_1=1$, $B_2=2$, $B_3=5$, $B_4=15$. You are not asked to know that, but noticing that the answer is "five, not six" is a good check that you have not double-counted $\{3,4\},\{5\}$ and $\{5\},\{3,4\}$ — a partition is a set of blocks, so their order does not matter.
KEY The fundamental property of a partition— ⊡ ✕

Given any partition $\{A_1,\dots,A_n\}$ of $S$ and any event $D$, the following holds:

$$D=(D\cap A_1)\cup(D\cap A_2)\cup\dots\cup(D\cap A_n)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}(D\cap A_i)\tag{1}$$

In words: any event can be written as a union of disjoint events, using the blocks of a partition.

Why it is true

  • Every point of $D$ appears on the right. Take $\omega\in D$. Since the partition is collectively exhaustive, $\omega$ lies in some block $A_k$; hence $\omega\in D\cap A_k$, which is one of the terms.
  • Nothing extra appears. Each term $D\cap A_i$ is a subset of $D$, so the union cannot escape $D$.
  • The pieces do not overlap. For $i\neq j$, $(D\cap A_i)\cap(D\cap A_j)=D\cap(A_i\cap A_j)=D\cap\emptyset=\emptyset$, using mutual exclusivity. This is the part that matters.

The picture on slide 20: the blue rectangle is $S$, carved by blue lines into five blocks $A_1,\dots,A_5$; the red blob is $D$. Formula (1) says the red blob is exactly the collection of its own pieces, one per block, and those pieces never overlap.

Where you will use it
Immediately in module 04 (the complement rule and the union formula both come from the two-block partition $\{A,A^c\}$), and again in module 16, where conditioning on the blocks of a partition turns (1) into $P(D)=\sum_i P(D\mid A_i)P(A_i)$ — the law of total probability.
EXERCISE 1 (slides 22–23) · other useful set relations — the lecture leaves this blank

Verify with Venn diagrams that $\{A,A^c\}$ is a partition of $S$, and then verify (a), (b), (c) below.

First: $\{A,A^c\}$ is a partition. Check the three conditions of the definition.

  1. Both are subsets of $S$ ✓ (and both nonempty, provided $A$ is neither $\emptyset$ nor all of $S$).
  2. Mutually exclusive: $A\cap A^c=\emptyset$. By definition $A^c$ contains exactly the outcomes not in $A$, so no outcome can be in both.
  3. Collectively exhaustive: $A\cup A^c=S$. Every outcome of $S$ is either in $A$ or not in $A$; there is no third option.

On the Venn diagram: the white circle is $A$, the shaded rest of the rectangle is $A^c$; together they tile the rectangle with no overlap. This is the smallest useful partition, and it is the one that does the most work in this course.

(a) For every event $D$: $\;D=(D\cap A)\cup(D\cap A^c)$.  (equation 2)

  1. This is the fundamental property (1) applied to the two-block partition $\{A,A^c\}$ — nothing new to prove.
  2. Directly: take $\omega\in D$. Either $\omega\in A$, in which case $\omega\in D\cap A$; or $\omega\notin A$, i.e. $\omega\in A^c$, in which case $\omega\in D\cap A^c$. Either way $\omega$ is on the right. Conversely both terms are subsets of $D$, so the right side sits inside $D$.
  3. The two pieces are disjoint: $(D\cap A)\cap(D\cap A^c)=D\cap(A\cap A^c)=D\cap\emptyset=\emptyset$.
  4. Picture: draw $D$ as a blob straddling the boundary of circle $A$. The part inside the circle is $D\cap A$; the part outside is $D\cap A^c$. Cutting along the circle's edge splits $D$ into two non-overlapping halves.

(b) $\;A\cup B=A\cup(A^c\cap B)$.  (equation 3)

  1. Apply (a) to the event $B$: $\;B=(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A^c)$. Substituting into the left side, $$A\cup B=A\cup\big[(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A^c)\big]$$
  2. But $B\cap A\subset A$, so absorbing it into $A$ changes nothing: $A\cup(B\cap A)=A$. What remains is $$A\cup B=A\cup(A^c\cap B)$$
  3. Picture: shade the two overlapping circles. The left side shades both circles entirely. The right side shades all of $A$, then adds only the crescent of $B$ lying outside $A$. Same region — but now written as a union of two disjoint pieces, since $A\cap(A^c\cap B)=\emptyset$.
  4. Why anyone cares: this is precisely the trick used in module 04 to prove $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$. The axioms only add disjoint events, and (b) is how you rewrite a non-disjoint union as a disjoint one.

(c) If $A\cap B=\emptyset$ (mutually exclusive), then $A^c\cap B=B$.  (equation 4)

  1. Apply (a) to $B$ once more: $\;B=(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A^c)$.
  2. By hypothesis $B\cap A=A\cap B=\emptyset$, so the first term vanishes and $B=B\cap A^c=A^c\cap B$ ✓
  3. Picture: with $A$ and $B$ drawn as two separate, non-touching circles, all of $B$ already lies outside $A$ — so intersecting $B$ with "everything outside $A$" removes nothing.
  4. In words: if $A$ and $B$ cannot happen together, then "$B$ and not $A$" is just "$B$" — the extra clause is redundant.
EXERCISE 2 (slide 24) · De Morgan's laws — the lecture leaves this blank too

1. Verify with a Venn diagram that $(A^c)^c=A$.

  1. $A^c$ is everything in $S$ outside $A$. Taking the complement again means "everything outside that" — which is everything back inside $A$.
  2. Formally: $\omega\in(A^c)^c\iff\omega\notin A^c\iff\omega\in A$.
  3. Picture: shade the rectangle minus the circle, then shade the complement of that shading. You are back to the circle.
  4. This is the set analogue of "two negatives make a positive", and it is used in step 2 below.

2. Verify that $(A\cap B)$ and $(A^c\cup B^c)$ form a partition of $S$, i.e. that 2.1 $(A\cap B)\cap(A^c\cup B^c)=\emptyset$ and 2.2 $(A\cap B)\cup(A^c\cup B^c)=S$.

  1. 2.1 — no overlap. Distribute the intersection over the union: $$(A\cap B)\cap(A^c\cup B^c)=\big[(A\cap B)\cap A^c\big]\cup\big[(A\cap B)\cap B^c\big]$$ The first bracket contains $A\cap A^c=\emptyset$, so it is empty; the second contains $B\cap B^c=\emptyset$, so it is empty too. The union of two empty sets is empty ✓
    In words: a point in $A\cap B$ is in both $A$ and $B$, so it is outside neither — it cannot be in $A^c$ nor in $B^c$.
  2. 2.2 — no gaps. Take any $\omega\in S$ and ask whether it lies in $A\cap B$.
    · If yes, it is in the first set ✓
    · If no, then $\omega$ fails to be in at least one of $A$, $B$. If $\omega\notin A$ then $\omega\in A^c$; if $\omega\notin B$ then $\omega\in B^c$. Either way $\omega\in A^c\cup B^c$ ✓
    So every point of $S$ is in one of the two sets.
  3. Picture: shade the central lens $A\cap B$ in one colour, and everything else in the rectangle in another. The two colours tile the rectangle exactly — and "everything else" is precisely $A^c\cup B^c$.

3. Deduce De Morgan's laws.

  1. Two sets that form a partition of $S$ are each other's complement — that is what "disjoint and together everything" means. So step 2 immediately gives the first law: $$(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c\tag{5}$$
  2. For the second law, take complements of both sides of (5) applied to the sets $\tilde A=A^c$ and $\tilde B=B^c$. Write (5) with those: $(\tilde A\cap\tilde B)^c=\tilde A^c\cup\tilde B^c$, i.e. $$(A^c\cap B^c)^c=(A^c)^c\cup(B^c)^c=A\cup B$$ using part 1. Now complement both sides: $$A^c\cap B^c=(A\cup B)^c\tag{6}$$

So the two laws are:

$$\boxed{(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c}\qquad\qquad\boxed{(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c}$$

In words, and this is how to remember them: "not (both $A$ and $B$)" is the same as "not $A$, or not $B$"; and "not ($A$ or $B$)" is the same as "not $A$ and not $B$". Push the complement inside and the operation flips: $\cap$ becomes $\cup$, $\cup$ becomes $\cap$.

De Morgan for $n$ sets (slide 25)

The same statement, with the same proof, for any number of events:

$$(A_1\cap A_2\cap\dots\cap A_n)^c=A_1^c\cup A_2^c\cup\dots\cup A_n^c\tag{7}$$ $$(A_1\cup A_2\cup\dots\cup A_n)^c=A_1^c\cap A_2^c\cap\dots\cap A_n^c\tag{8}$$

Law (8) is the one you will use most: "none of the $A_i$ occurs" $=$ "not (at least one occurs)". Combined with the complement rule of module 04, it turns the awkward event "at least one" into the easy event "none" — which for independent events is just a product. That single move solves the two-students exercise in module 06 and the "more than four frogs" exercise in module 08.

The pattern to internalise
Whenever a problem says "at least one", immediately write $P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})$. The justification is the complement rule plus De Morgan (8), and it is almost always faster than the direct route.
