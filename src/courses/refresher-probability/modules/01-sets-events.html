01

Sets and events

lecture 1, slides 2–13 · the language probability is written in
READ FIRST Why a probability course starts with set theory— ⊡ ✕

Probability is not defined for numbers or for sentences. It is defined for sets. Before you can write $P(\text{something})$, that something has to be expressed as a subset of a list of possible outcomes — and the vocabulary for doing that is set theory.

The chain of translation runs like this, and it is worth memorising because every exercise in lectures 1–3 is an instance of it:

a random experimentrolling a die, tossing a coin, next quarter's return
its elementary outcomes $\omega_i$$1,2,3,4,5,6$ — exactly one of them happens
the sample space $S$the set of all of them, $\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$
an event $E$any subset, e.g. "even" $=\{2,4,6\}$
"$A$ or $B$", "$A$ and $B$", "not $A$"$A\cup B$, $A\cap B$, $A^c$

Once the words on the right-hand side are set operations, the probability calculus of lecture 2 applies mechanically. The lecture says this directly: set operations are intuitive thanks to Venn diagrams, and they form the basis for probability calculus.

The one habit to build
When a problem says "will the launch succeed in Switzerland or Italy or both?", write $A\cup B$. When it says "the stock market rises and the bond market falls", write $A\cap B$. Translating the English into sets first, before reaching for any formula, is most of the work in lectures 1–3.
DEF Sets, subsets, the basic vocabulary— ⊡ ✕

A set is a collection of elements — in this course, the elements are numbers. Ordering does not matter: the sets $\{2,3\}$ and $\{3,2\}$ are equal. Nor does repetition: $\{a,a,b\}=\{a,b\}$.

Sets you will meet constantly

Natural numbers$\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$
Integers$\mathbb{Z}=\{\dots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\dots\}$
Real numbers$\mathbb{R}$
Closed interval$[2,3]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:2\le x\le3\}$ — endpoints included
Open interval$(-20,2)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:-20<x<2\}$ — endpoints excluded
Half-open$[4,6)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:4\le x<6\}$

A set can also be defined by stating a property its elements satisfy rather than listing them. Note how the same property gives different sets depending on where the elements are drawn from:

$$B=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid x\ge5\}=[5,\infty),\qquad D=\{x\in\mathbb{N}\mid x\ge5\}=\{5,6,7,\dots\}$$

Three relations

  • Subset. $A\subset B$ if every element of $A$ is also an element of $B$. Examples: $\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}$, and $\{2,6\}\subset\{2,4,5,6\}$.
  • Equality. Two sets are equal if they have exactly the same elements. So $\{1,2,3\}=\{3,2,1\}$.
  • Empty set. The set with no elements, written $\emptyset$, is the null set or empty set. It will turn out to be the "impossible event".
DEF Experiment, outcome, sample space, event— ⊡ ✕

A random experiment is a process leading to two or more possible results, where you cannot predict with certainty which will occur: tossing a coin, rolling a die, counting the customers arriving at a service desk in a fixed period. The requirement that makes it workable is that you must be able to list all possible outcomes.

An elementary outcome $\omega_i$ is one possible result. The defining property, and it is doing real work:

Exactly one, always
One elementary outcome, and only one, occurs once the experiment is performed. Elementary outcomes are therefore mutually exclusive by construction — you can never get both a 3 and a 5 on one roll. This is what will let us add their probabilities in lecture 2.

The sample space $S$ is the set of all elementary outcomes:

Tossing a coin$S=\{\text{head},\text{tail}\}$
Rolling a die$S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$
Customers at a desk$S=\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,3,\dots\}$ — infinite, but still listable

An event $E$ is a subset of elementary outcomes in the sample space. Getting an even number when rolling a die is the event $E=\{2,4,6\}$.

An event occurs when one of the elementary outcomes contained in it takes place. So if the die shows 4, the event "even" occurred, the event $\{1,2,3\}$ also occurred, and the event "odd" did not.

Two special events
$S$ itself is an event — the certain event, since some outcome must occur. $\emptyset$ is an event — the impossible event, since it contains no outcome that could occur. Lecture 2 assigns them probability 1 and 0 respectively.
DEF The four set operations— ⊡ ✕

Throughout, the running example of the lecture: rolling a die, $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, with $A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{1,3,5\}$, $C=\{6\}$.

Union $A\cup B$"$A$ or $B$ or both" occur. $\;A\cup B=S$, $\;B\cup C=\{1,3,5,6\}$, $\;A\cup C=A$
Intersection $A\cap B$"$A$ and $B$" occur together. $\;A\cap C=\{6\}$, $\;A\cap B=\emptyset$
Complement $A^c$ (also $\overline{A}$)$A^c=S\setminus A$ — everything in $S$ not in $A$. $\;A^c=B$, $\;C^c=\{1,2,3,4,5\}$
Difference $A\setminus B$elements in $A$ but not in $B$. Note $A\setminus B=A\cap B^c$. $\;\{2,3,4\}\setminus\{4,5\}=\{2,3\}$

The identity $A\setminus B=A\cap B^c$ in the last row is worth noticing now: it means difference is not a new operation, just intersection with a complement. Every set expression in this course can be written with union, intersection and complement alone — which is exactly why the three axioms of lecture 2 suffice.

EXERCISE (slides 9–10) · events in the rolling of a die

$S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$, $A=\{1,2\}$, $B=\{2,4,5\}$. Find:

  1. $A\cup B$. Everything in either set, listed once: $\{1,2\}\cup\{2,4,5\}=\{1,2,4,5\}$. (The shared element 2 is not written twice — sets do not count repetitions.)
  2. $A\cap B$. Only what is in both: $\{2\}$.
  3. $A^c$ and $B^c$. $A^c=S\setminus\{1,2\}=\{3,4,5,6\}$ and $B^c=S\setminus\{2,4,5\}=\{1,3,6\}$.
  4. Verify $(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c$. Left side: $(A\cup B)^c=\{1,2,4,5\}^c=\{3,6\}$. Right side: $$A^c\cap B^c=\{3,4,5,6\}\cap\{1,3,6\}=\{3,6\}$$ Equal ✓. This is De Morgan's second law, proved in general in module 02.
  5. $B\setminus A$. In $B$ but not in $A$: $\{4,5\}$. Check against the identity $B\setminus A=B\cap A^c=\{2,4,5\}\cap\{3,4,5,6\}=\{4,5\}$ ✓.
EXERCISE (slides 11–12) · which events occurred?

The realised roll is 2. With $A=\{\text{odd}\}=\{1,3,5\}$ and $B=\{1,2,3\}$, decide for each of $A$, $B$, $A\cap B$, $A\cup B$, $A\setminus B$, $B\setminus A$, $A^c$ whether it occurred.

The rule is mechanical: an event occurred if and only if the realised outcome belongs to it. So compute each set and check whether $2$ is inside.

$A=\{1,3,5\}$$2\notin A$did not occur
$B=\{1,2,3\}$$2\in B$occurred
$A\cap B=\{1,3\}$$2\notin$did not occur
$A\cup B=\{1,2,3,5\}$$2\in$occurred
$A\setminus B=\{5\}$$2\notin$did not occur
$B\setminus A=\{2\}$$2\in$occurred
$A^c=\{2,4,6\}$$2\in$occurred

So $B$, $A\cup B$, $B\setminus A$ and $A^c$ occurred; $A$, $A\cap B$ and $A\setminus B$ did not — matching the lecture's answer on slide 12.

Insight
Four events occurred at once on a single roll, and that is not a contradiction: only the elementary outcomes are mutually exclusive, never the compound events built from them. Note also the pattern — exactly one of $A$ and $A^c$ occurs, always.
DEF Mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive— ⊡ ✕

Two properties of a collection of events, and the two that matter most for the rest of the course. Running example again: $S=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$ with $A=\{2,4,6\}$, $B=\{1,3,5\}$, $C=\{6\}$.

  • Mutually exclusive (also disjoint, incompatible): events that share no elementary outcome, i.e. whose intersection is empty. Example: $B$ and $C$ are mutually exclusive because $B\cap C=\emptyset$. In words, either $B$ or $C$ occurs, or neither — but never both.
  • Collectively exhaustive: events whose union is the entire sample space $S$. Example: $A$ and $B$ are collectively exhaustive, since $A\cup B=S$; so are $A$, $B$ and $C$ together, since $A\cup B\cup C=S$.

Read them as two separate guarantees, and keep them apart in your head:

mutually exclusive"at most one of them can happen" — no overlap
collectively exhaustive"at least one of them must happen" — no gaps

Notice that $A,B,C$ are collectively exhaustive but not mutually exclusive: $A\cap C=\{6\}\neq\emptyset$. The two properties are independent of each other, and module 02 studies the case where both hold at once — a partition, the single most useful structure in the whole course.

Why lecture 2 needs this
Kolmogorov's second axiom only lets you add probabilities for mutually exclusive events: $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$ requires $A\cap B=\emptyset$. Adding probabilities of overlapping events double-counts the overlap, which is why module 04 needs the correction term $-P(A\cap B)$. Almost every mistake in elementary probability is a missing disjointness check.

Check yourself: with a die, are $\{1,2\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ mutually exclusive? Are they collectively exhaustive? (Neither: they share the outcome 2, and their union $\{1,2,3\}$ misses 4, 5, 6.)
