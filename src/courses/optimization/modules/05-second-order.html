05

Second-order conditions

the Hessian decides: maximum, minimum, or saddle
READ FIRST Where calculus meets linear algebra— ⊡ ✕

Module 04 produced candidates. This module classifies them, and it does so by reducing a calculus question to a linear algebra one you have already solved.

The argument is the same Taylor expansion as in one variable, written in $n$ dimensions. At a stationary point the gradient term vanishes, leaving

$$f(\mathbf x^*+\mathbf h)-f(\mathbf x^*)=\underbrace{\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf h}_{=\;0}+\tfrac12\,\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H(\mathbf x^*)\,\mathbf h+o(\|\mathbf h\|^2)$$

So whether every small move raises or lowers $f$ is decided entirely by the sign of the quadratic form $\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf h$:

$\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf h<0$ for all $\mathbf h\ne\mathbf 0$every move lowers $f$ ⇒ local maximum
$\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf h>0$ for all $\mathbf h\ne\mathbf 0$every move raises $f$ ⇒ local minimum
both signs occursome moves up, some down ⇒ saddle

Those three cases have names — negative definite, positive definite, indefinite — and they are precisely the classification of quadratic forms from linear algebra module 08. Nothing new needs to be invented; the second derivative test in $n$ variables is the definiteness test, and the tools (leading principal minors, eigenvalue signs) transfer unchanged.

Chapter map
The Hessian and its symmetry → the classification theorem → the fast $2\times2$ test → leading principal minors for general $n$ → eigenvalues → saddle points → the semidefinite case, where the test goes silent.
DEF The Hessian and the classification theorem— ⊡ ✕

The Hessian is the matrix of second partial derivatives, evaluated at the point of interest:

$$H(\mathbf x)=\begin{pmatrix} f_{x_1x_1}&\cdots&f_{x_1x_n}\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ f_{x_nx_1}&\cdots&f_{x_nx_n}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{in two variables}\quad H=\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\ f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}$$

By Young's theorem (calculus module 09) $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$, so $H$ is symmetric — which is exactly the hypothesis every definiteness test needs, and the reason its eigenvalues are all real.

Second-order conditions, at a stationary point $\mathbf x^*$
Sufficient:
$H(\mathbf x^*)$ negative definite $\Rightarrow$ strict local maximum
$H(\mathbf x^*)$ positive definite $\Rightarrow$ strict local minimum
$H(\mathbf x^*)$ indefinite $\Rightarrow$ saddle point (this one is conclusive too — it rules out both)
Necessary:
local maximum $\Rightarrow$ $H(\mathbf x^*)$ negative semidefinite
local minimum $\Rightarrow$ $H(\mathbf x^*)$ positive semidefinite

The gap between the two halves is the semidefinite-but-not-definite case ($\det H=0$), where the quadratic term vanishes in some direction and higher-order terms take over. There the test is silent — see the last panel.

Trap
The SOC classifies a stationary point. Applying it at a point where $\nabla f\ne\mathbf 0$ is meaningless — the linear term dominates and the expansion above does not apply. Always solve the FOC first.
Trap
Checking only $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ is not enough. For $f(x,y)=x^2-3xy+y^2$ both are $+2$, yet $\det H=4-9=-5<0$ and the origin is a saddle. The cross-partial carries essential information, and ignoring it is the single most common error in this module.

Check yourself: why must $H$ be evaluated at the stationary point rather than symbolically? (Answer: because $H$ generally varies with $\mathbf x$; a function can be convex in one region and concave in another. The exception is a quadratic objective, whose Hessian is constant.)

TIP The $2\times2$ test — memorise this one— ⊡ ✕

For two variables the whole classification reduces to two numbers. Write $A=f_{xx}$, $B=f_{xy}$, $C=f_{yy}$, and

$$D=\det H=AC-B^2$$
The rule
$D>0$ and $A<0$  ⇒ local maximum
$D>0$ and $A>0$  ⇒ local minimum
$D<0$  ⇒ saddle point (whatever $A$ is)
$D=0$  ⇒ inconclusive

Worth understanding rather than memorising blindly. $D=AC-B^2>0$ forces $A$ and $C$ to share a sign, so the two axis-slices curve the same way and $A$ alone reveals which. $D<0$ means the cross term $B^2$ beats the product $AC$ — the surface is being twisted enough that some direction curves the opposite way, which is a saddle.

$f(x,y)=x^3-3xy+y^3$ — the module 04 candidates

From module 04 the stationary points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Second partials: $f_{xx}=6x$, $f_{yy}=6y$, $f_{xy}=-3$.

At $(0,0)$: $H=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3\\-3&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $D=0-9=-9<0$ ⇒ saddle.

At $(1,1)$: $H=\begin{pmatrix}6&-3\\-3&6\end{pmatrix}$, so $D=36-9=27>0$ and $A=6>0$ ⇒ strict local minimum, value $f(1,1)=1-3+1=-1$.

Is it global? No: $f(x,0)=x^3\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$. The FOC and SOC are local statements, and here there is no global minimum at all.

$f(x,y)=-2x^2-3y^2+2xy+8x+4y$

Module 04 found the stationary point $(2.8,1.6)$. Here $f_{xx}=-4$, $f_{yy}=-6$, $f_{xy}=2$.

$D=(-4)(-6)-2^2=24-4=20>0$ and $A=-4<0$ ⇒ strict local maximum.

And because the objective is quadratic, $H$ is the same at every point, so $f$ is strictly concave everywhere and the point is the unique global maximum (module 03, Theorem 2).

Check yourself: classify the stationary point of $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-4x-6y+5$. (Answer: $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=4>0$, $A=2>0$ ⇒ strict local minimum at $(2,3)$; constant Hessian ⇒ global.)

FIGURE Definiteness, seen in every direction at once— ⊡ ✕

The surface is the quadratic form $q(x,y)=\tfrac12\big(a\,x^2+2b\,xy+c\,y^2\big)$, whose Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\ b&c\end{pmatrix}$ everywhere. The right panel plots $q$ along the unit circle — that is, the value of the quadratic form in every direction $\theta$. Definiteness is literally whether that curve stays on one side of zero.

Read the right panel and the classification falls out:

  • Entirely above zero ⇒ positive definite ⇒ local minimum. Contours are nested ellipses.
  • Entirely below ⇒ negative definite ⇒ local maximum.
  • Crosses zero ⇒ indefinite ⇒ saddle. The crossings are the directions in which the surface is flat to second order, and the contours become hyperbolas with those directions as asymptotes.
  • Touches zero without crossing ⇒ semidefinite ⇒ the test is silent; the form vanishes along one direction and higher-order terms decide.

The maximum and minimum of the right-hand curve are exactly the two eigenvalues of $H$, attained in the eigenvector directions drawn on the left. That is what "the eigenvalues measure curvature along the principal axes" means, made visible.

IDEA 1 Testing definiteness for general $n$— ⊡ ✕

Two standard tests, both from linear algebra module 08. Use whichever is cheaper.

Test 1 — leading principal minors

Let $D_k$ be the determinant of the top-left $k\times k$ block of $H$.

Positive definite$D_1>0,\;D_2>0,\;\dots,\;D_n>0$ — all positive
Negative definite$D_1<0,\;D_2>0,\;D_3<0,\dots$ — alternating, starting negative; i.e. $(-1)^kD_k>0$
Indefinite$D_n\ne0$ but neither pattern holds
Inconclusive$D_n=0$ — leading minors cannot settle semidefiniteness

The alternating pattern for negative definiteness is not arbitrary: $H$ is negative definite exactly when $-H$ is positive definite, and each $k\times k$ determinant picks up a factor $(-1)^k$.

Trap
Leading principal minors test strict definiteness only. For semidefiniteness you must check all principal minors (every symmetric selection of rows and columns), not just the top-left ones. $H=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ has $D_1=0,D_2=0$; it is negative semidefinite, but the leading-minor test cannot say so.

Test 2 — eigenvalues

Since $H$ is symmetric, its eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ are all real, and

all $\lambda_i>0$positive definite ⇒ minimum
all $\lambda_i<0$negative definite ⇒ maximum
mixed signsindefinite ⇒ saddle
some $\lambda_i=0$, rest one signsemidefinite ⇒ inconclusive

Two shortcuts that often settle a $2\times2$ instantly, since $\det H=\prod\lambda_i$ and $\operatorname{tr}H=\sum\lambda_i$:

  • $\det H<0$ in two variables ⇒ the eigenvalues have opposite signs ⇒ saddle, no further work.
  • $\det H>0$ ⇒ same signs ⇒ the sign of the trace (or of $f_{xx}$) tells you which.
Which test?
Minors are faster to compute by hand for $n=2,3$. Eigenvalues are more informative — they also tell you which directions are the steep ones, which matters for the principal-component reading of a covariance matrix in module 11.
TRAP Saddles, and the silent case— ⊡ ✕

Saddle points

A saddle satisfies the FOC in full and is neither a maximum nor a minimum: it is a minimum along some directions and a maximum along others. The canonical example is $f(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ at the origin, where $f(x,0)=x^2$ has a minimum and $f(0,y)=-y^2$ has a maximum.

Saddles are the reason the FOC cannot be trusted alone in several variables. There is no comparably common one-dimensional phenomenon — $x^3$ at $0$ is the closest analogue, and it is a degenerate case rather than the generic one. In $n$ dimensions saddles are typical: a random symmetric matrix is far more likely to have mixed-sign eigenvalues than to be definite, and the more variables you have the more likely a stationary point is a saddle.

Why numerical optimisers care
Gradient methods stall at saddles — the gradient is zero, so there is nothing to follow — even though escape directions exist. In high-dimensional problems this, not local minima, is the usual obstacle, and it is why second-order information is worth computing.

When $\det H=0$: the test is silent

Semidefinite means the quadratic term vanishes along at least one direction, so the $o(\|\mathbf h\|^2)$ remainder decides. All three of these have $H=\mathbf 0$ at the origin:

$f=x^4+y^4$strict local minimum
$f=-x^4-y^4$strict local maximum
$f=x^4-y^4$saddle

Three ways forward when this happens:

  1. Examine slices. If $f$ increases along one line through the point and decreases along another, it is a saddle — one line each way settles it.
  2. Use higher-order terms. Expand further and look at the first non-vanishing term.
  3. Use global structure. If $f$ is concave on the whole domain, a stationary point is a global maximum regardless of what the Hessian does at that point (module 03). This is usually the cleanest escape.
A silent case resolved by structure

$f(x,y)=-x^4-y^2$. FOC: $-4x^3=0$ and $-2y=0$, so $(0,0)$.

$H(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$: $D=0$, inconclusive.

But $-x^4$ and $-y^2$ are each concave, and a sum of concave functions is concave. So $f$ is concave everywhere and $(0,0)$ is the global maximum by module 03's Theorem 2 — no Hessian needed.

Check yourself: $f(x,y)=x^2y^2$ has $H=\mathbf 0$ at the origin. Minimum or not? (Answer: a minimum — $f\ge0$ everywhere with $f(0,0)=0$ — but the SOC cannot see it. A direct argument beats the test here.)
