03

Convexity & concavity

the structure that turns local answers into global ones
READ FIRST Why this is the most important module in the course— ⊡ ✕

Module 01 left us with a genuine problem: calculus is local, optimisation questions are global, and no amount of differentiating at a single point can close that gap. This module closes it.

The resolution is a structural assumption rather than a computation. If the objective is concave, then

  • every local maximum is automatically a global maximum;
  • the first-order condition stops being merely necessary and becomes sufficient — solving $f'=0$ finishes the problem;
  • if the concavity is strict, the maximiser is unique.

That is an enormous return on one assumption, and it explains a fact about economics that otherwise looks like laziness: utility functions are assumed concave, production functions are assumed concave, cost and risk functions are assumed convex. These assumptions are not primarily about realism. They are what makes the marginal conditions everyone writes down actually characterise the optimum instead of merely being consistent with it.

The mirror image holds throughout: convex functions are for minimisation. Minimising a convex function is the easy problem, and "convex optimisation" is a well-solved field for exactly the reasons above.

DEF Convex sets — the domain has to behave too— ⊡ ✕
Definition
A set $S$ is convex if for every $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in S$ and every $\lambda\in[0,1]$, $$\lambda\mathbf{x}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{y}\in S.$$

In words: the straight segment joining any two points of $S$ stays inside $S$. No dents, no holes, no separate pieces. As $\lambda$ runs from 1 to 0 the expression $\lambda\mathbf x+(1-\lambda)\mathbf y$ traces exactly that segment — a linear combination whose weights are non-negative and sum to one, which is why it is called a convex combination.

ConvexNot convex
intervals; $\mathbb{R}^n$; $\mathbb{R}^n_+$the union of two disjoint intervals
half-spaces $\{\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\le b\}$a ring or annulus

The useful closure property
An intersection of convex sets is convex. Since each linear constraint $\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\le b$ or $\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=b$ defines a convex set, any feasible set defined by linear constraints is convex. (Unions are not — that is why "buy asset A or asset B but not both" is a genuinely hard problem while "hold any mix" is easy.)
Finance link
A budget set $\{\mathbf x\ge\mathbf 0:\;\mathbf p^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\le m\}$ is an intersection of half-spaces, hence convex. So is the set of fully-invested portfolios $\{\mathbf w:\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1=1\}$. Adding a short-sale ban $\mathbf w\ge\mathbf 0$ keeps it convex. Adding "at most 5 holdings" destroys convexity — and that is exactly the constraint that makes real portfolio construction computationally hard.

Check yourself: is $\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=1\}$ (the circle itself, not the disc) convex? (Answer: no — the chord between two points on the circle passes through the interior, which is not in the set. The disc $x^2+y^2\le1$ is convex.)

IDEA 1 Concave and convex functions— ⊡ ✕
Definition (chord test)
On a convex set $S$, $f$ is concave if for all $\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in S$ and $\lambda\in[0,1]$ $$f\big(\lambda\mathbf x+(1-\lambda)\mathbf y\big)\;\ge\;\lambda f(\mathbf x)+(1-\lambda)f(\mathbf y)$$ and convex if the inequality is reversed ($\le$). Replace $\ge$ by $>$ (for $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf y$, $\lambda\in(0,1)$) for strictly concave.

Both sides are easy to read. The left is the function evaluated at a point between $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf y$; the right is the chord — the straight line joining $(\mathbf x,f(\mathbf x))$ to $(\mathbf y,f(\mathbf y))$ — evaluated at the same place. So:

Concavethe curve lies above the chord — it bulges up, ∩
Convexthe curve lies below the chord — it dips down, ∪

Note the definition needs no derivatives. That matters: $f(x)=-|x|$ is concave despite having no derivative at 0, and the theorems below apply to it in full.

Terminology clash — read carefully
Calculus books say "concave up" for what economics calls convex, and "concave down" for concave. This course uses the economics words throughout: convex = ∪ = $f''\ge0$, concave = ∩ = $f''\le0$. Also: a function is concave $\iff$ $-f$ is convex, so every result has a mirror image and you only ever need to learn one side.

The tangent characterisation (differentiable case)

An equivalent and often more useful description:

$$f\text{ concave}\iff f(\mathbf y)\le f(\mathbf x)+\nabla f(\mathbf x)^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)\quad\text{for all }\mathbf x,\mathbf y$$

A concave function lies below all of its tangents. This is the fact that proves the big theorem below, and it has a plain reading: the linear approximation always over-estimates a concave function, so if the tangent at $\mathbf x$ is flat, nothing anywhere can be higher than $f(\mathbf x)$. For convex functions the tangent lies below and under-estimates.

Check yourself: in calculus module 06 you estimated $\ln(1.05)\approx0.05$ from the tangent at $x=1$, and the true value is $0.0488$. Which way does that confirm? (Answer: the estimate is too high, so $\ln$ lies below its tangent — $\ln$ is concave ✓.)

FIGURE The chord test, run by hand— ⊡ ✕

Pick a function and two points. The amber chord joins $(x_1,f(x_1))$ to $(x_2,f(x_2))$; the readout compares the curve and the chord at the midpoint. For a concave function the curve wins every time, for every pair — and one failing pair is enough to disqualify it.

What to notice:

  • ln x and √x — the curve is above the chord for every pair, and below the tangent everywhere. Concave.
  • x² and eˣ — the mirror image. Convex.
  • x³ — concave on the left half, convex on the right. Pick both points on one side and the test passes; straddle zero and it fails. This is why "concave" is a statement about a whole domain, never about a point.
  • wavy — fails badly, and this is the shape that makes optimisation hard: several local maxima, none of which the FOC can distinguish.
  • −|x| — concave with a corner. No derivative at 0, yet the maximum is unambiguously there. Concavity does not need smoothness.
IDEA 2 Testing with derivatives— ⊡ ✕

The chord definition is the right definition but a poor test. With derivatives it becomes mechanical.

One variable
For $f$ twice differentiable on an interval:
$f''(x)\ge0$ for all $x$ $\iff$ $f$ is convex
$f''(x)\le0$ for all $x$ $\iff$ $f$ is concave
$f''(x)>0$ everywhere $\Rightarrow$ strictly convex (the converse fails — see the trap)
Several variables
For $f$ twice continuously differentiable on a convex set, with Hessian $H(\mathbf x)$:
$H(\mathbf x)$ positive semidefinite for all $\mathbf x$ $\iff$ $f$ convex
$H(\mathbf x)$ negative semidefinite for all $\mathbf x$ $\iff$ $f$ concave
$H(\mathbf x)$ negative definite for all $\mathbf x$ $\Rightarrow$ $f$ strictly concave

Two things deserve emphasis. First, the condition must hold at every point of the domain, not just at the critical point — that is the whole difference between a local classification (module 5) and a global structural claim (module 6). Second, semidefiniteness is checked with all principal minors, not just the leading ones; the leading-minor shortcut is only valid for strict definiteness. Linear algebra module 08 has the tests.

Trap
$f''>0$ everywhere is sufficient for strict convexity but not necessary. $f(x)=x^4$ is strictly convex, yet $f''(0)=0$. So finding $f''=0$ at one point does not break strict convexity — you have to look at the whole picture.

Operations that preserve concavity

Rather than differentiating a monster, decompose it:

Sum$f,g$ concave $\Rightarrow f+g$ concave
Positive scaling$f$ concave, $c>0$ $\Rightarrow cf$ concave
Negation$f$ concave $\Rightarrow -f$ convex
Affine composition$f$ concave $\Rightarrow f(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)$ concave
Minimumthe pointwise min of concave functions is concave (max, for convex)
Concave increasing of concave$\varphi$ concave and increasing, $f$ concave $\Rightarrow\varphi(f)$ concave

Note what is missing: products and quotients of concave functions are generally neither. $f=g=x$ are both concave (they are linear) but $fg=x^2$ is convex.

Is $f(\mathbf w)=\boldsymbol\mu^{\mathsf T}\mathbf w-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ concave?

The first term is linear, hence concave. The second is $-\tfrac\gamma2$ times a quadratic form with $\Sigma$ positive semidefinite (every covariance matrix is), so $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ is convex and its negative multiple is concave. A sum of concave functions is concave ✓.

Directly: $H=-\gamma\Sigma$, negative semidefinite for $\gamma>0$. So mean–variance utility is concave, and module 6 will conclude that its FOC alone locates the global optimum.

IDEA 3 The three theorems — the payoff— ⊡ ✕
Theorem 1 — local implies global
If $f$ is concave on a convex set $S$, then every local maximum of $f$ on $S$ is a global maximum.

Why. Let $\mathbf x^*$ be a local max and suppose some $\mathbf y\in S$ has $f(\mathbf y)>f(\mathbf x^*)$. Look at points on the segment between them, $\mathbf z_\lambda=\lambda\mathbf y+(1-\lambda)\mathbf x^*$, which stay in $S$ because $S$ is convex. Concavity gives

$$f(\mathbf z_\lambda)\ \ge\ \lambda f(\mathbf y)+(1-\lambda)f(\mathbf x^*)\ >\ f(\mathbf x^*)$$

for every $\lambda\in(0,1]$. Taking $\lambda$ tiny puts such a point arbitrarily close to $\mathbf x^*$ while still beating it — contradicting local maximality. So no such $\mathbf y$ exists. Notice that both convexity of $S$ and concavity of $f$ were used.

Theorem 2 — the FOC becomes sufficient
If $f$ is concave and differentiable, then $\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)=\mathbf 0$ is sufficient (as well as necessary) for $\mathbf x^*$ to be a global maximum.

Why. Use the tangent characterisation: $f(\mathbf y)\le f(\mathbf x^*)+\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\mathbf x^*)$. With $\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)=\mathbf 0$ the last term vanishes, leaving $f(\mathbf y)\le f(\mathbf x^*)$ for all $\mathbf y$ — global maximality, in one line. This is the single most valuable theorem in the course: under concavity, solving the FOC finishes the problem, with no second-order check and no comparison of candidates.

Theorem 3 — strictness gives uniqueness
If $f$ is strictly concave, the maximiser (if one exists) is unique. Without strictness the set of maximisers is still convex — a flat plateau, never two isolated peaks.

Why. If $\mathbf x_1\ne\mathbf x_2$ were both maximisers with common value $M$, then strict concavity gives $f\big(\tfrac12\mathbf x_1+\tfrac12\mathbf x_2\big)>\tfrac12M+\tfrac12M=M$ — a feasible point strictly better than the maximum. Contradiction.

Concave $f$, convex $S$maximisation is easy: FOC suffices
Convex $f$, convex $S$minimisation is easy: FOC suffices
Convex $f$, maximisehard — the maximum sits at a corner of $S$; the FOC finds the minimum
Jensen, and why concavity is risk aversion
The chord inequality applied to a random variable is Jensen's inequality: for concave $u$, $$E[u(X)]\le u\big(E[X]\big).$$ A concave utility means a gamble is worth less than its expected value received for certain — that is risk aversion. The same inequality appears in the probability course, and $-u''/u'$ is the Arrow–Pratt coefficient measuring how concave $u$ is.

Check yourself: $f(x,y)=-(x-1)^2-(y+2)^2$. How many steps to the global maximum? (Answer: $H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$ is negative definite everywhere ⇒ strictly concave. So solve $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$: $(1,-2)$, unique global maximum. No SOC check, no candidate comparison.)

TIP Quasi-concavity — the weaker assumption economics actually needs— ⊡ ✕

Concavity is stronger than many economic arguments require, and it has an awkward feature: it is not preserved by monotone transformations. Utility is only meaningful up to such a transformation ($u$ and $u^3$ describe the same preferences), yet $\sqrt{xy}$ is concave while $xy$ is not. Something is wrong with using concavity to describe preferences.

Definition
$f$ is quasi-concave if its upper contour sets $$U_k=\{\mathbf x\in S: f(\mathbf x)\ge k\}$$ are convex for every $k$. Equivalently: $f\big(\lambda\mathbf x+(1-\lambda)\mathbf y\big)\ge\min\{f(\mathbf x),f(\mathbf y)\}$.

The contour reading is the useful one: the set of bundles at least as good as a given one is convex. That is precisely the standard picture of indifference curves bowed towards the origin, and it is what "diminishing marginal rate of substitution" means geometrically.

Facts worth carrying:

  • Concave $\Rightarrow$ quasi-concave. The converse is false.
  • Quasi-concavity is preserved by increasing transformations — so it is a property of preferences, not of the particular utility representation. This is why economics states its assumptions in terms of quasi-concavity.
  • Both $\sqrt{xy}$ and $xy$ are quasi-concave, as they must be, since they represent the same preferences.
What you give up
Quasi-concavity is enough to guarantee that the set of maximisers is convex and — with strict quasi-concavity and a convex feasible set — that the maximiser is unique. It is not enough for Theorem 2: a critical point of a quasi-concave function need not be a maximum at all. $f(x)=x^3$ is quasi-concave on $\mathbb{R}$ (its upper contour sets are half-lines, hence convex), yet $f'(0)=0$ at a point that is not an optimum.

So: quasi-concavity for describing preferences and getting uniqueness; full concavity when you want the FOC to certify a global maximum on its own.

For Cobb–Douglas $F(K,L)=K^\alpha L^\beta$, the sum $\alpha+\beta$ is the degree of homogeneity: $<1$ decreasing returns (and $F$ concave, so profit maximisation is well behaved), $=1$ constant returns (concave, but the optimal scale is indeterminate), $>1$ increasing returns (not concave — the firm wants to grow without bound, and the problem has no interior solution). The mathematics and the economics fail at exactly the same point.
