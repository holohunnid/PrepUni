11

Finance applications

portfolio choice is a constrained optimisation problem, and nothing else
READ FIRST The same problem, over and over— ⊡ ✕

Modern portfolio theory is a Lagrange problem. So is least squares, so is maximum likelihood, so is the consumer problem. This module works through them, and the point is not the individual results but the recognition that they are all one template:

Minimum-variance portfoliominimise a convex quadratic subject to one linear constraint
Efficient frontierthe same, with two linear constraints
Mean–variance utilitymaximise a concave quadratic subject to one linear constraint
Ordinary least squaresminimise a convex quadratic, unconstrained
Maximum likelihoodmaximise a log-likelihood, usually unconstrained
Consumer choicemaximise a concave (or quasi-concave) utility subject to a budget

Every row is convex programming: convex or concave objective, convex feasible set. By module 08's third avoidance route and module 10's sufficiency theorem, the first-order conditions are necessary and sufficient in every case — which is precisely why all of these have clean closed-form answers, and why finance can be taught with calculus rather than with numerical search.

One structural fact recurs and is worth stating early: a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is always positive semidefinite, because $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w=\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf R)\ge0$ — a variance cannot be negative. So $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ is a convex function, always. Minimising risk is therefore always the easy direction.

IDEA 1 The minimum-variance portfolio— ⊡ ✕

With $n$ assets, weights $\mathbf w$, and covariance matrix $\Sigma$, the portfolio variance is $\sigma_p^2=\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$. Requiring full investment gives

$$\min_{\mathbf w}\ \mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w\quad\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1=1$$
Solving it in matrix form
  1. $\mathcal L=\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w-\lambda(\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1-1)$.
  2. FOC (using $\nabla(\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w)=2\Sigma\mathbf w$ from module 04): $\;2\Sigma\mathbf w-\lambda\mathbf 1=\mathbf 0$.
  3. So $\mathbf w=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$ — the weights are proportional to $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$, whatever $\lambda$ is.
  4. Impose the constraint to pin down the scale: $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1=1$.
  5. $$\boxed{\ \mathbf w^*=\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}{\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}\ }$$

Why no second-order check is needed: $\Sigma$ is positive semidefinite, so the objective is convex; the constraint is linear, so the feasible set is affine hence convex. By module 08, the FOC is sufficient for a global minimum. If $\Sigma$ is positive definite (no redundant assets) the solution is unique.

Notice the two-step structure — direction first, scale second. It recurs in every portfolio formula below: the FOC gives $\mathbf w$ up to a constant, and the constraint fixes the constant. Recognising it saves a great deal of algebra.

Two assets, explicitly

With $\mathbf w=(w,1-w)$ and $\sigma_{AB}=\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B$:

$$\sigma_p^2(w)=w^2\sigma_A^2+(1-w)^2\sigma_B^2+2w(1-w)\sigma_{AB}$$

a one-variable unconstrained problem, because substituting the constraint eliminated a variable (module 01). Setting $\dfrac{d\sigma_p^2}{dw}=0$:

$$\boxed{\ w^*=\frac{\sigma_B^2-\sigma_{AB}}{\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2-2\sigma_{AB}}\ }$$

and the second derivative is $2(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2-2\sigma_{AB})=2\operatorname{Var}(R_A-R_B)\ge0$, confirming a minimum and showing the denominator can only vanish when the two assets are perfectly correlated with equal volatility.

Reading the formula
At $\rho=1$ the expression reduces to $w^*=\dfrac{\sigma_B}{\sigma_B-\sigma_A}$ and the minimum variance is zero — with perfectly correlated assets you can hedge one exactly with the other (using a short position). At $\rho=-1$ you also get zero variance, this time with both weights positive. Between those extremes, diversification reduces risk without eliminating it, and the lower $\rho$, the greater the benefit.

Check yourself: $\sigma_A=15\%$, $\sigma_B=25\%$, $\rho=0$. Find $w^*$. (Answer: $\sigma_{AB}=0$, so $w^*=\frac{0.0625}{0.0225+0.0625}=0.735$ — 73.5% in the lower-volatility asset. With zero correlation the weights are inversely proportional to the variances.)

IDEA 2 The efficient frontier: two constraints— ⊡ ✕

Minimum variance ignores return. Adding a target gives the problem Markowitz actually posed:

$$\min_{\mathbf w}\ \tfrac12\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu=\mu_0,\;\;\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1=1$$

Two equality constraints, so two multipliers (module 07):

$$\mathcal L=\tfrac12\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w-\gamma(\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu-\mu_0)-\lambda(\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1-1)$$

FOC: $\;\Sigma\mathbf w=\gamma\boldsymbol\mu+\lambda\mathbf 1$, hence

$$\mathbf w^*=\Sigma^{-1}\big(\gamma\boldsymbol\mu+\lambda\mathbf 1\big)$$

with $\gamma,\lambda$ determined by substituting back into the two constraints — a $2\times2$ linear system. The structure is again direction then scale, now with two basis directions $\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$.

Two-fund separation
Because $\mathbf w^*$ is a linear combination of the two fixed vectors $\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$, every efficient portfolio is a mix of just two of them. Solve the problem for two target returns and you can generate the whole frontier by combining those two solutions. This is a direct consequence of the FOC being linear in $\mathbf w$.

Tracing $\mu_0$ over its range gives $\sigma_p(\mu_0)$, a hyperbola in $(\sigma,\mu)$ space. Its leftmost point is the minimum-variance portfolio of Idea 1 (the case where the return constraint is dropped, i.e. $\gamma=0$); the upper branch is the efficient frontier, and the lower branch is dominated — same risk, less return.

$\gamma$ is a shadow price
By module 09, $\gamma=\dfrac{\partial(\tfrac12\sigma_p^2)}{\partial\mu_0}$ — the extra variance you must accept per unit of extra required return. It is the marginal price of return in units of risk, and its reciprocal is the slope of the frontier. A multiplier that a portfolio manager would actually quote.

Adding a risk-free asset

Maximising the Sharpe ratio $\dfrac{\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu-r_f}{\sqrt{\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w}}$ subject to $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1=1$ gives the tangency portfolio

$$\mathbf w_{\text{tan}}\;\propto\;\Sigma^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol\mu-r_f\mathbf 1\big)$$

normalised to sum to one. Same shape once more: $\Sigma^{-1}$ times a vector of excess returns, scaled to satisfy the constraint. Geometrically it is where a line from $(0,r_f)$ is tangent to the frontier — a tangency condition, exactly as in module 07, because maximising a ratio is maximising the slope of that line.

Check yourself: why does $\Sigma^{-1}$ appear in every formula? (Answer: the FOC is $\Sigma\mathbf w=(\text{something})$, a linear system; solving it inverts $\Sigma$. Portfolio optimisation is a quadratic objective, so its FOC is linear — which is why the whole subject reduces to linear algebra.)

FIGURE The frontier, and what correlation does to it— ⊡ ✕

Two assets: A with $\mu_A=6\%$, $\sigma_A=15\%$; B with $\mu_B=10\%$, $\sigma_B=25\%$. Sweeping the weight $w$ in A traces a curve in risk–return space. Change the correlation and watch the curve bend.

What the correlation slider demonstrates:

  • ρ = 1 — the curve collapses to a straight line between the two assets. No diversification benefit at all: risk is just a weighted average. (Extending beyond the segment, with a short position, still reaches zero variance.)
  • ρ < 1 — the curve bows left of that line. The horizontal gap is the diversification benefit, and it grows as $\rho$ falls.
  • ρ = −1 — the curve becomes two straight segments meeting the vertical axis: a combination with exactly zero risk exists. Perfect hedging.
  • The min-variance portfolio (green ring) moves as $\rho$ changes, and for low $\rho$ it can sit well inside the two assets — holding some of the riskier asset genuinely reduces total risk.

Everything below the green ring is dominated: for the same risk you could have had more return. That is what "efficient" means, and it is why only the upper branch is drawn in colour.

IDEA 3 Utility maximisation and risk aversion— ⊡ ✕

Rather than fixing a target return, let the investor trade risk against return directly. The standard mean–variance objective is

$$\max_{\mathbf w}\ \mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu-\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1=1$$

with $\gamma>0$ the coefficient of risk aversion. Module 03 already established this objective is concave: linear minus a positive multiple of a convex quadratic. So the FOC is sufficient.

FOC: $\;\boldsymbol\mu-\gamma\Sigma\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf 1$, giving

$$\mathbf w^*=\frac{1}{\gamma}\Sigma^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol\mu-\lambda\mathbf 1\big)$$

with $\lambda$ fixed by the constraint. As $\gamma\to\infty$ the investor becomes infinitely risk averse and $\mathbf w^*$ converges to the minimum-variance portfolio of Idea 1 — the return term is drowned out. As $\gamma$ falls, the investor slides up the efficient frontier. Risk aversion selects a point on the frontier; it does not change the frontier itself.

Where concavity is doing the economics
Module 03 showed that concave utility is risk aversion, via Jensen's inequality. Here that same concavity is what makes the optimisation well posed: a risk-loving investor would have a convex objective, the FOC would locate a minimum, and the optimum would sit at a corner of the feasible set (module 03's last table). Convex problems and sensible economics coincide, and that is not a coincidence.

The general consumer problem

$\max u(\mathbf x)$ subject to $\mathbf p^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=m$ gives, for each pair of goods,

$$\frac{\partial u/\partial x_i}{\partial u/\partial x_j}=\frac{p_i}{p_j}$$

after dividing one FOC by another to eliminate $\lambda$ (module 07). Equivalently $\dfrac{\partial u/\partial x_i}{p_i}=\lambda$ for every good: the last euro spent on each good must deliver the same marginal utility. If it did not, shifting a euro would improve things — which is exactly the improvement argument that motivated the FOC in module 04.

Check yourself: $u=x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$, prices $p_x,p_y$, income $m$. Find the demands. (Answer: MRS $=y/x=p_x/p_y$ gives $p_yy=p_xx$; with the budget $p_xx+p_yy=m$ this yields $x^*=\frac{m}{2p_x}$, $y^*=\frac{m}{2p_y}$ — spend half the income on each, the Cobb–Douglas rule.)

IDEA 4 Estimation is optimisation too— ⊡ ✕

Ordinary least squares

From module 04: minimising $\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2$ gives the normal equations $X^{\mathsf T}X\boldsymbol\beta=X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y$, with Hessian $2X^{\mathsf T}X\succeq0$. Convex, so the FOC is sufficient for a global minimum — and unique when $X$ has full column rank.

Constrained regression is then a Lagrange problem. Imposing $R\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf r$ (say, "the coefficients sum to one"):

$$\mathcal L=\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2-\boldsymbol\lambda^{\mathsf T}(R\boldsymbol\beta-\mathbf r)$$

and the multiplier $\boldsymbol\lambda$ measures how much fit is sacrificed to satisfy the restriction — a shadow price in units of squared error. That quantity is the basis of the classical $F$-test for linear restrictions: a restriction that costs little fit is one the data do not reject.

Maximum likelihood

Choose parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ to maximise the probability of the data:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ \ell(\boldsymbol\theta)=\arg\max_{\boldsymbol\theta}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln f(x_i\mid\boldsymbol\theta)$$

Two module-01 ideas are visible here. Working with the log-likelihood is legitimate because $\ln$ is strictly increasing, so it does not move the maximiser — and it converts a product into a sum, which is what makes the FOC tractable. The FOC $\nabla\ell=\mathbf 0$ is called the score equation.

MLE for the normal mean

With $x_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $\sigma$ known,

$$\ell(\mu)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)^2$$

FOC: $\dfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)=0\Rightarrow\hat\mu=\bar x$.

SOC: $\ell''=-n/\sigma^2<0$ everywhere ⇒ $\ell$ is strictly concave ⇒ $\bar x$ is the unique global maximum.

The sample mean is not merely a natural estimator; it is the solution of an optimisation problem. And note that the same FOC minimises $\sum(x_i-\mu)^2$ — least squares and normal-error maximum likelihood are the same problem, which is why they agree.

The Hessian returns as information
The negative of the log-likelihood Hessian, $-\ell''(\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})$, is the observed Fisher information, and its inverse is the estimator's asymptotic variance. A sharply peaked (strongly concave) likelihood means a precisely estimated parameter; a flat one means a poorly identified parameter. Curvature at the optimum is not just a classification device — it is the standard error.

Check yourself: why is it legitimate to maximise $\ell=\ln L$ rather than $L$? (Answer: $\ln$ is strictly increasing, so it preserves the ordering of values and hence the argmax — module 01. Only the optimal value changes.)
