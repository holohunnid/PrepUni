04

First-order conditions in $n$ variables

flat in every direction at once
READ FIRST One equation becomes $n$ equations— ⊡ ✕

In one variable an interior optimum needed $f'(x^*)=0$: no direction of movement improves things, and there were only two directions to worry about. With $n$ variables there are infinitely many directions, so the requirement looks much stronger — and, pleasantly, it is not.

The reason is the directional derivative formula from calculus module 09. The rate of change of $f$ at $\mathbf x$ in a unit direction $\mathbf u$ is

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf x)=\nabla f(\mathbf x)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u$$

a single dot product. Demanding it be zero for every $\mathbf u$ forces $\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf 0$ — because a vector orthogonal to everything is the zero vector (take $\mathbf u=\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$ and the dot product becomes $\|\nabla f\|$, which is zero only when the gradient is). So infinitely many conditions collapse into exactly $n$ scalar equations, one per variable.

There is an even shorter way to see it, and it is the one to remember: if the gradient is not zero, it points uphill. Move a little along $\nabla f$ and $f$ strictly increases; at an interior point that move is allowed, so you were not at a maximum. A maximum can only sit where there is no uphill direction at all.

DEF The first-order condition— ⊡ ✕
FOC — necessary condition
If $f$ is differentiable and has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $\mathbf x^*$ of the feasible set, then $$\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)=\mathbf 0,\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\mathbf x^*)=\dots=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(\mathbf x^*)=0.$$

A point satisfying this is a stationary or critical point. In two variables it is two equations in two unknowns:

$$f_x(x^*,y^*)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_y(x^*,y^*)=0$$

Three readings of the same statement, each useful in a different context:

Slicesholding $y$ fixed, $f$ is flat in $x$; holding $x$ fixed, flat in $y$. Every one-dimensional slice through the point is at a critical point of its own.
Geometrythe tangent plane to the surface $z=f(x,y)$ is horizontal.
Contours$\|\nabla f\|=0$, so the level curves degenerate — a peak, a pit, or a crossing point (a saddle).
Trap
Both partials must vanish at the same point. Solving $f_x=0$ gives a curve of points, not a point; solving $f_y=0$ gives another curve. The stationary points are where those curves intersect. Solving one equation and stopping is the most common error in this module.
Trap
Exactly as in one variable, the FOC is necessary, not sufficient. A stationary point may be a maximum, a minimum, or a saddle — a point that is a maximum along one direction and a minimum along another. Saddles have no one-dimensional analogue worth the name, and they are why module 05 exists.

Check yourself: find the stationary points of $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2-4x-6y+5$. (Answer: $f_x=2x-4=0$ and $f_y=2y-6=0$, so the unique stationary point is $(2,3)$.)

EX Solving the system — three cases— ⊡ ✕

The FOC is a system of equations, and how you solve it depends entirely on whether it is linear.

1 · Linear system (quadratic objective)

$f(x,y)=-2x^2-3y^2+2xy+8x+4y$.

$f_x=-4x+2y+8=0$ and $f_y=-6y+2x+4=0$.

From the first, $y=2x-4$. Substituting: $-6(2x-4)+2x+4=0\Rightarrow-10x+28=0\Rightarrow x=2.8$, then $y=1.6$.

A quadratic objective always gives a linear FOC system — which is why every quadratic optimisation problem in finance reduces to solving $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$, and why the linear algebra course is a prerequisite for the portfolio problems in module 11.

2 · Nonlinear system — substitute and factor

$f(x,y)=x^3-3xy+y^3$.

$f_x=3x^2-3y=0\Rightarrow y=x^2$.   $f_y=-3x+3y^2=0\Rightarrow x=y^2$.

Substitute the first into the second: $x=(x^2)^2=x^4$, so $x^4-x=0$, i.e. $x(x^3-1)=0$, giving $x=0$ or $x=1$.

Stationary points: $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.

Two candidates from one system. Module 05 will find that $(1,1)$ is a local minimum and $(0,0)$ is a saddle — the FOC alone cannot tell them apart.

3 · An economic setup: two inputs

A firm chooses $L,K\ge0$ to maximise $\pi=pF(L,K)-wL-rK$ with $F(L,K)=L^{1/3}K^{1/3}$, $p=6$, $w=1$, $r=1$.

$\pi_L=2L^{-2/3}K^{1/3}-1=0$ and $\pi_K=2L^{1/3}K^{-2/3}-1=0$.

Dividing one by the other: $\dfrac{K^{1/3}L^{-2/3}}{L^{1/3}K^{-2/3}}=\dfrac{K}{L}=1$, so $K=L$.

Substituting back: $2L^{-2/3}L^{1/3}=2L^{-1/3}=1\Rightarrow L^{1/3}=2\Rightarrow L=K=8$.

Each FOC says value of the marginal product = input price: $p\,\partial F/\partial L=w$. Dividing the two FOCs cancels the awkward common factor and leaves the cost-minimising input ratio — a standard and very useful trick with Cobb–Douglas.

Method
Write both equations. If linear, solve directly. If not: solve one for a variable and substitute, or divide one FOC by the other (which kills common multiplicative terms), or subtract them to exploit symmetry. Always report all solutions — nonlinear systems routinely have several, and dropping one loses the answer.
FIGURE Two slices, one condition— ⊡ ✕

The left panel is the contour map with the gradient arrow at the chosen point. The right panels are the two slices through that point: $f(x,y_0)$ with $y$ frozen, and $f(x_0,y)$ with $x$ frozen. The FOC holds exactly when both slices are flat at the marked point — and the arrow disappears.

Press jump to the stationary point for each surface and compare:

  • min and max — both slices become flat, and both curve the same way. The point is a genuine extremum.
  • saddle — both slices are flat, so the FOC is satisfied in full, yet one slice curves up and the other down. Moving along $x$ you are at a minimum; moving along $y$, at a maximum. This is exactly what the FOC cannot detect.
  • tilted — a quadratic with a cross term $xy$. The stationary point is still found by $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$, but now the axis slices are misleading: the interesting directions are the eigenvector directions, which is what module 05 formalises.

Away from the stationary point the arrow points uphill, and it is always perpendicular to the contour. That "there is still an uphill direction" is the proof that a non-stationary interior point cannot be an optimum.

TRAP When the FOC does not apply— ⊡ ✕

The theorem has three hypotheses, and real problems break all of them. Learn to check each before writing $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$.

HypothesisWhat breaks itWhat to do instead
Interiorthe optimum is on a boundary of $S$ (e.g. $w_i=0$ under a short-sale ban)check boundaries separately; module 10's KKT conditions
Differentiablekinks: $|x|$, $\max\{\cdot\}$, piecewise definitionsexamine the pieces and the kinks by hand
Local extremum existsunbounded objective, non-compact feasible setcheck limits / coercivity, or invoke concavity
Why economics assumes interior solutions
Textbook conditions like "$MRS$ = price ratio" or "value of marginal product = wage" are interior FOCs. They fail at corners. When a model predicts zero consumption of a good, or a portfolio pinned at its short-sale bound, the equality is replaced by an inequality. Stating "assume an interior solution" is not a throwaway phrase — it is the hypothesis that licenses the entire calculation.

The boundary matters in $n$ dimensions too

The closed-interval method of module 02 generalises, but the boundary is now a surface rather than two points, so you cannot simply enumerate it. The full procedure for a compact feasible set is:

  1. Find the stationary points in the interior and evaluate $f$ at each.
  2. Optimise $f$ restricted to the boundary — itself a constrained problem, which is what module 07 solves.
  3. Compare all the values.

This is one honest reason the Lagrange method matters: even a purely "unconstrained" problem on a bounded region needs constrained optimisation to handle its edges.

TIP What the FOC looks like in matrix form— ⊡ ✕

Most objectives in finance are quadratic, and for those the FOC has a closed form worth memorising. With $A$ symmetric,

$$f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x+\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x+c \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \nabla f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$$

so the FOC is the linear system $A\mathbf x^*=-\mathbf b$, with the unique solution $\mathbf x^*=-A^{-1}\mathbf b$ whenever $A$ is invertible. Two gradient rules generate everything:

$\nabla\big(\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x\big)$$=\mathbf b$
$\nabla\big(\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x\big)$$=2A\mathbf x$  (for symmetric $A$)
Hessian of $\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x$$=A$ — constant, independent of $\mathbf x$

The last line is why quadratic problems are so well behaved: the Hessian is the same everywhere, so a local classification is automatically a global statement about concavity or convexity. Solve the FOC once and you are done.

Least squares as a quadratic FOC

Minimise $S(\boldsymbol\beta)=\|\mathbf y-X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2=\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y-2\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y+\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}X\boldsymbol\beta$.

$\nabla S=-2X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y+2X^{\mathsf T}X\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf 0$, giving the normal equations

$$X^{\mathsf T}X\boldsymbol\beta=X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(X^{\mathsf T}X)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y$$

The Hessian is $2X^{\mathsf T}X$, positive semidefinite for any $X$ (since $\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}X^{\mathsf T}X\mathbf v=\|X\mathbf v\|^2\ge0$), so $S$ is convex and by module 03's Theorem 2 the FOC alone certifies a global minimum. If $X$ has full column rank the Hessian is positive definite and the minimiser is unique.

This is the same result the linear algebra course derives by projection. Two routes, one answer: OLS is an optimisation problem whose FOC happens to be a linear system.

Check yourself: for $f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x-\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x$ with $A$ positive definite, where is the minimum? (Answer: $\nabla f=A\mathbf x-\mathbf b=\mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf x^*=A^{-1}\mathbf b$ — unique and global, since $A\succ0$ makes $f$ strictly convex.)
