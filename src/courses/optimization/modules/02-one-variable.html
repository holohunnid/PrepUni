02

Optimisation in one variable

the template that every later module generalises
READ FIRST Learn the shape of the argument here— ⊡ ✕

You already know how to find maxima of a function of one variable — calculus module 06 did it. This module redoes it as an optimisation problem, because the logical structure is what generalises, and if you learn it properly in one dimension you will recognise it in every later module.

The structure has two halves, and keeping them apart is the single most useful habit in the course:

Necessary conditionevery optimum must satisfy it — so it generates candidates. It may also flag points that are not optima.
Sufficient conditionanything satisfying it is an optimum — so it certifies. It may miss some genuine optima.

$f'(x)=0$ is necessary (at an interior optimum, for a differentiable $f$) and not sufficient. $f'(x^*)=0$ together with $f''(x^*)<0$ is sufficient for a strict local maximum and not necessary. Every "$\nabla f=\mathbf 0$" and "Hessian negative definite" statement later is the same pair of sentences in more variables.

DEF First-order condition — and the word "interior"— ⊡ ✕
FOC (Fermat)
If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $x^*$ of the feasible set, and $f$ is differentiable at $x^*$, then $$f'(x^*)=0.$$

Why. Suppose $f'(x^*)>0$. Then $f$ is strictly increasing at $x^*$, so moving a little to the right raises $f$ — and being interior guarantees a little to the right is allowed. So $x^*$ was not a maximum. Symmetrically for $f'(x^*)<0$. The only remaining possibility is $f'(x^*)=0$.

Read that argument again, because it shows exactly where each hypothesis is used, and both hypotheses fail in problems you will meet:

  • Interior. At an endpoint you cannot move both ways. On $[0,1]$ the function $f(x)=x$ has its maximum at $x=1$ with $f'(1)=1\ne0$ — the FOC simply does not apply there. This is a corner solution, and it is why endpoints are always checked separately.
  • Differentiable. $f(x)=-|x|$ has a maximum at $0$ where $f'$ does not exist.
Critical points
$x^*$ is a critical (stationary) point if $f'(x^*)=0$ or $f'(x^*)$ fails to exist. Interior optima are always critical points — so the critical points are the complete candidate list for the interior.
Trap
The FOC is necessary, not sufficient. $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$ and no extremum there at all. A solved FOC gives you candidates, never an answer. Writing "$f'=0$ at $x=2$, therefore the maximum is at $x=2$" is an unfinished argument, and examiners mark it as one.

Check yourself: the FOC found $x=0$ for $f(x)=x^3$ on $[-1,2]$. Is the maximum there? (Answer: no. Compare candidates: $f(-1)=-1$, $f(0)=0$, $f(2)=8$. The maximum is the endpoint $x=2$.)

IDEA 1 Second-order conditions— ⊡ ✕

Given a critical point, the second derivative usually settles what it is — because curvature is exactly the missing information.

Sufficient SOC
Suppose $f'(x^*)=0$.
If $f''(x^*)<0$ then $x^*$ is a strict local maximum.
If $f''(x^*)>0$ then $x^*$ is a strict local minimum.
If $f''(x^*)=0$ the test is silent.

The reason is the Taylor expansion of calculus module 06. Near a critical point the linear term vanishes, so

$$f(x^*+h)-f(x^*)=\underbrace{f'(x^*)h}_{=\,0}+\tfrac12 f''(x^*)h^2+o(h^2)\;\approx\;\tfrac12 f''(x^*)h^2$$

Since $h^2>0$ for every $h\ne0$, the sign of $f''$ alone decides whether every small move raises or lowers $f$. This is worth remembering as the engine of the whole course: in $n$ dimensions the same expansion produces $\tfrac12\mathbf{h}^{\mathsf T}H\mathbf{h}$, a quadratic form, and "the sign of $f''$" becomes "the definiteness of $H$".

The necessary version, which is weaker

Do not confuse the sufficient condition with its necessary counterpart:

Necessary at a local max$f''(x^*)\le0$ — note the weak inequality
Sufficient for a local max$f'(x^*)=0$ and $f''(x^*)<0$ — strict

The gap between them is exactly the $f''=0$ case, where anything can happen. All three of these have $f'(0)=f''(0)=0$:

$f(x)=-x^4$local (and global) maximum at 0
$f(x)=x^4$local minimum at 0
$f(x)=x^3$neither — an inflection
When the SOC is silent, use the first-derivative test
Look at the sign change of $f'$ across $x^*$: $+\to-$ is a maximum, $-\to+$ a minimum, no change is neither. It never fails, it works at non-differentiable points, and it needs no second derivative. Treat the SOC as the fast route and the sign table as the reliable one.

Check yourself: $f(x)=x^4-4x$. Classify its critical point. (Answer: $f'=4x^3-4=0$ at $x=1$; $f''=12x^2$ so $f''(1)=12>0$ ⇒ strict local minimum.)

FIGURE The candidate list, built and compared— ⊡ ✕

The objective is $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5$, so $f'(x)=3(x-3)(x+1)$ with critical points at $x=-1$ and $x=3$. Move the interval and watch the candidate list change — critical points drop out when they fall outside, and the winner switches between interior peaks and endpoints.

Closed-interval method
1. Find all critical points of $f$ in $(a,b)$. 2. Evaluate $f$ at each of them and at both endpoints. 3. The largest value is the global maximum, the smallest the global minimum.

No classification is needed — you are just comparing numbers, and Weierstrass guarantees the winner of the list really is the global optimum. This is the finite-list escape from the local/global problem of module 01, and it is the most reliable method in the course whenever the feasible set is a closed interval.

TRAP Corner solutions and unbounded domains— ⊡ ✕

Corners

A corner solution is an optimum at a boundary point of the feasible set. At a corner the FOC is replaced by an inequality, because only one direction of movement is allowed. On $[a,b]$:

Max at the left end $a$$f'(a)\le0$ — moving right must not help
Max at the right end $b$$f'(b)\ge0$ — moving left must not help

That "equality becomes inequality at a boundary" is not a one-dimensional curiosity. It is precisely the shape of the Kuhn–Tucker conditions in module 10, where a binding constraint replaces $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x=0$ with $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x\le0$.

Corners are economically normal
A consumer who buys none of a good, an investor at a short-sale bound of $w_i=0$, a firm producing zero — all are corner solutions. Marginal conditions like "MRS = price ratio" hold only at interior optima; at a corner they become inequalities. Assuming an interior solution when the data force a corner is a substantive modelling error, not a technicality.

Unbounded domains

On $\mathbb{R}$ or $[0,\infty)$ there are no endpoints to check, so Weierstrass does not apply and a maximum need not exist. Two standard repairs:

  1. Check the limits. Compute $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)$. If $f\to-\infty$ at both ends and $f$ is continuous, a global maximum exists and must be among the critical points — the search has been confined to a bounded region. This is coercivity.
  2. Use concavity. If $f$ is concave, any critical point is automatically a global maximum with no limit-checking at all (module 3).
$\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\;-x^2+4x-1$

$f'=-2x+4=0\Rightarrow x^*=2$, $f''=-2<0$ everywhere.

The domain is unbounded, so justify the global claim: $f''<0$ everywhere means $f$ is strictly concave, hence $x^*=2$ is the unique global maximum, $f(2)=3$. (Or: $f\to-\infty$ as $x\to\pm\infty$, so the max exists and must be the single critical point.)

Check yourself: does $\max_{x\ge0}\,\ln(1+x)-\tfrac12 x$ have a solution? (Answer: yes. $f'=\frac{1}{1+x}-\frac12=0$ at $x=1$, and $f''=-\frac{1}{(1+x)^2}<0$, so $f$ is strictly concave and $x^*=1$ is the global maximum.)

TIP Setting up a word problem— ⊡ ✕

Most marks in applied questions are lost before any calculus happens. The setup is a fixed routine:

  1. Name the variables and say what they mean, with units.
  2. Write the objective as a formula.
  3. Write the constraint(s) — including the implicit ones like $q\ge0$.
  4. Reduce to one variable by substituting the constraint.
  5. State the domain, including what is physically or economically meaningful.
  6. Only now: FOC, SOC, compare candidates, and answer the question that was asked.
Monopoly pricing

Demand $p=100-2q$; cost $C(q)=20q+200$. Choose $q\ge0$ to maximise profit.

  1. Objective: $\pi(q)=pq-C(q)=(100-2q)q-20q-200=80q-2q^2-200$.
  2. Domain: $q\ge0$ and (for $p\ge0$) $q\le50$ — a closed interval, so Weierstrass applies ✓.
  3. FOC: $\pi'=80-4q=0\Rightarrow q^*=20$.
  4. SOC: $\pi''=-4<0$ ⇒ strict local max; and since $\pi''<0$ everywhere, $\pi$ is strictly concave, so it is global.
  5. Endpoints: $\pi(0)=-200$, $\pi(50)=4000-5000-200=-1200$. Both worse ✓.
  6. Answer: produce $q^*=20$, charge $p^*=60$, earn $\pi^*=600$.

The FOC rearranges to $100-4q=20$, i.e. marginal revenue = marginal cost. Every marginal condition in economics is an FOC in disguise.

Two habits
Check the domain endpoints even when the interior answer looks obvious — it costs two evaluations. And answer in the units asked for: if the question wants the price, do not stop at the quantity.
