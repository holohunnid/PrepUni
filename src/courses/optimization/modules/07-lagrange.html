07

The Lagrange method

equality constraints, and the tangency that explains them
READ FIRST Why $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$ stops working— ⊡ ✕

Every argument so far rested on one sentence: if the gradient is not zero, move along it and improve. Once a constraint is imposed, that move may be illegal — it takes you off the constraint set. So a constrained optimum can sit at a point where $\nabla f\ne\mathbf 0$, and the unconstrained FOC is simply the wrong condition.

What replaces it? At a constrained optimum you cannot improve using only the moves you are allowed. The allowed moves are those along the constraint curve, so the requirement is that $f$ has no first-order change in any feasible direction. That is a weaker demand than $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$, and it is exactly what the Lagrange condition encodes.

Consider the problem

$$\max_{x,y} f(x,y)\quad\text{subject to}\quad g(x,y)=c$$

Sometimes you can dodge it entirely: solve the constraint for $y$, substitute, and you have an unconstrained problem in one variable (module 01). That is often the fastest route and you should use it when it works. It fails when the constraint cannot be solved explicitly ($x^3+y^3=xy$), when substitution destroys a symmetry, when there are many variables and many constraints, or when you actually want the multiplier — which, as module 09 shows, is one of the most economically informative numbers in the whole problem.

IDEA 1 The geometry: level curves must be tangent— ⊡ ✕

Picture the contour map of $f$ with the constraint curve $g=c$ drawn across it. Walking along the constraint, you cross contours of $f$ and its value rises or falls. Where can you be at a maximum?

The argument
If the constraint curve crosses a level curve of $f$ at a point, then locally the constraint has points on both sides of that level curve — one direction along the constraint leads to higher $f$. So you can improve, and the point is not optimal.

The only points where no improvement is available are those where the constraint curve is tangent to a level curve of $f$.

Now translate tangency into algebra using a fact from calculus module 09: the gradient is perpendicular to the level curve. Two curves are tangent at a point exactly when their normals are parallel. So at the optimum, $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$ must be parallel — which means one is a scalar multiple of the other:

Lagrange condition
$$\nabla f(\mathbf x^*)=\lambda\,\nabla g(\mathbf x^*)$$ for some scalar $\lambda$, the Lagrange multiplier.

A second, equivalent reading is worth having because it is the one that generalises to many constraints. Decompose $\nabla f$ into a component along the constraint and a component perpendicular to it. Improvement is possible exactly when the along-the-constraint component is non-zero. So optimality means that component vanishes — i.e. $\nabla f$ is entirely perpendicular to the constraint, and since $\nabla g$ is also perpendicular to it, the two must be parallel.

Trap
Tangency is necessary, not sufficient — exactly like $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$. The constrained minimum, and any other stationary point along the constraint, satisfies the same condition. The method delivers candidates; module 08 classifies them.

Check yourself: why does the sign or size of $\lambda$ not matter for the tangency argument? (Answer: the argument only needs the two gradients to be parallel; $\lambda$ is whatever scale factor makes that an equation. Its value carries separate information — see module 09.)

DEF The Lagrangian and the recipe— ⊡ ✕

The Lagrange condition plus the constraint is a system of three equations in three unknowns $(x,y,\lambda)$. There is a device that generates all three at once. Define the Lagrangian

$$\mathcal{L}(x,y,\lambda)=f(x,y)-\lambda\big(g(x,y)-c\big)$$

and set all three of its partial derivatives to zero:

$\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial x}=f_x-\lambda g_x=0$these two are $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$
$\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial y}=f_y-\lambda g_y=0$
$\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial \lambda}=-\big(g(x,y)-c\big)=0$this recovers the constraint $g=c$

That is the whole trick: treating $\lambda$ as an extra variable turns a constrained problem in two variables into an unconstrained stationary-point problem in three. The third equation is not an extra assumption — it is the constraint, reappearing automatically.

The recipe
1. Write $\mathcal L=f-\lambda(g-c)$. 2. Set $\mathcal L_x=\mathcal L_y=\mathcal L_\lambda=0$. 3. Solve the system for all $(x,y,\lambda)$. 4. Evaluate $f$ at each solution and compare (or classify — module 08).

On the sign convention

Some texts write $\mathcal L=f+\lambda(g-c)$ or $f+\lambda(c-g)$. All are correct and give the same $\mathbf x^*$; only the sign of $\lambda$ changes. This course uses $\mathcal L=f-\lambda(g-c)$ throughout, because with that choice $\lambda$ has the clean interpretation $\lambda=\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}c$ — the rate at which the optimal value improves when the constraint is relaxed (module 09). Pick one convention and keep it.

Solving the system in practice
The system is usually nonlinear, and the standard move is to eliminate $\lambda$ first: divide the first FOC by the second to get $$\frac{f_x}{f_y}=\frac{g_x}{g_y}$$ a single equation in $x$ and $y$ with no multiplier in it. Combine that with the constraint and you have two equations in two unknowns. Recover $\lambda$ at the end if the question asks for it.
You have seen this equation before
$f_x/f_y=g_x/g_y$ with $f$ a utility and $g$ a budget $p_1x+p_2y$ reads $$\frac{MU_1}{MU_2}=\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$ — marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio. The central condition of consumer theory is a Lagrange FOC with $\lambda$ divided out.
FIGURE Walk along the constraint and watch the gradients line up— ⊡ ✕

Move the point along the amber constraint curve. The green arrow is $\nabla f$, the red arrow is $\nabla g$. Everywhere except the optimum they point in different directions — and the component of $\nabla f$ along the constraint tells you which way to walk to improve. At the optimum the two arrows become parallel and the constraint is tangent to a contour of $f$.

What the figure is showing:

  • Away from the optimum, $\nabla f$ has a component along the constraint. That component is the slope of the right-hand curve — walk that way and $f$ improves.
  • At the optimum the right-hand curve is flat and $\nabla f$ has no along-constraint component, so it is entirely perpendicular to the constraint — i.e. parallel to $\nabla g$. The readout's angle between them drops to 0° or 180°.
  • $\nabla f$ is generally not zero there. The unconstrained FOC fails, which is precisely why a new condition was needed.
  • In the min case the arrows are anti-parallel and $\lambda<0$. Parallel or anti-parallel are both "$\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$" — the sign lives in $\lambda$.

The right-hand panel is the substitution method made visible: $f$ restricted to the constraint is a one-variable function, and the Lagrange condition is its ordinary FOC.

EX Three complete solutions— ⊡ ✕
1 · $\max xy$ subject to $x+y=4$
  1. $\mathcal L=xy-\lambda(x+y-4)$.
  2. $\mathcal L_x=y-\lambda=0$;  $\mathcal L_y=x-\lambda=0$;  $\mathcal L_\lambda=-(x+y-4)=0$.
  3. The first two give $x=y=\lambda$. The constraint then gives $2x=4$, so $x=y=2$ and $\lambda=2$.
  4. $f^*=4$.

Check by substitution: $\max_x x(4-x)$ has its peak at $x=2$ with value 4 ✓. And $\lambda=2$ predicts that raising the budget from 4 to 5 raises the maximum by about 2 — the true new value is $6.25$, up $2.25$ ✓ (module 09).

2 · $\max x+y$ subject to $x^2+y^2=4$
  1. $\mathcal L=x+y-\lambda(x^2+y^2-4)$.
  2. $1-2\lambda x=0$ and $1-2\lambda y=0$ ⇒ $x=y=\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}$ (note $\lambda\ne0$, else $1=0$).
  3. Constraint: $2x^2=4\Rightarrow x=\pm\sqrt2$, giving the two candidates $(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)$ and $(-\sqrt2,-\sqrt2)$.
  4. Values: $2\sqrt2\approx2.83$ and $-2\sqrt2$. So the maximum is $2\sqrt2$ at $(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)$ with $\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}$; the other candidate is the minimum.

Two candidates, and the method found both extremes at once. The circle is compact and $f$ continuous, so Weierstrass guarantees both exist and comparing values settles which is which — no second-order test needed.

3 · $\min x^2+y^2$ subject to $x+2y=5$
  1. $\mathcal L=x^2+y^2-\lambda(x+2y-5)$.
  2. $2x-\lambda=0$ and $2y-2\lambda=0$ ⇒ $x=\lambda/2$, $y=\lambda$, hence $y=2x$.
  3. Constraint: $x+2(2x)=5\Rightarrow 5x=5\Rightarrow x=1$, $y=2$, $\lambda=2$.
  4. $f^*=1+4=5$, so the shortest distance from the origin to the line is $\sqrt5$.

The answer $y=2x$ says the optimal point lies along $(1,2)$ — the normal direction of the line. Minimising distance to a line is orthogonal projection, exactly as in the linear algebra course. Two methods, one answer.

TRAP Constraint qualification, and several constraints— ⊡ ✕

When the method can fail

Constraint qualification
The Lagrange condition is necessary at a constrained optimum provided $$\nabla g(\mathbf x^*)\ne\mathbf 0.$$ A point where $\nabla g=\mathbf 0$ is a singular point of the constraint and must be checked separately.

Why it is needed. The whole argument said "the tangent direction to the constraint is the perpendicular of $\nabla g$". If $\nabla g=\mathbf 0$ there is no well-defined normal, the constraint set may have a cusp or a self-crossing, and the tangency picture collapses.

The standard counterexample

$\min x^2+y^2$ subject to $(x-1)^3=y^2$.

The constraint has a cusp at $(1,0)$, which is clearly the closest feasible point to the origin. But $\nabla g=(3(x-1)^2,-2y)=(0,0)$ there, so no $\lambda$ can satisfy $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ — indeed $\nabla f=(2,0)\ne\mathbf 0$.

The Lagrange system has no solution, yet the problem has one. The method did not err; its hypothesis failed.

In exam problems the qualification almost always holds — for a linear constraint $\nabla g$ is a non-zero constant vector — but you should be able to state it, and to check it when the constraint is nonlinear.

Several constraints

With $m$ equality constraints $g_1=c_1,\dots,g_m=c_m$ on $n$ variables (needing $m<n$, else the constraints generically pin the point down and there is nothing to optimise), give each its own multiplier:

$$\mathcal L=f-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_j\big(g_j-c_j\big),\qquad \nabla f=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\lambda_j\nabla g_j$$

The geometry is unchanged in spirit: $\nabla f$ must lie in the span of the constraint gradients — that is, it has no component in any feasible direction. The constraint qualification becomes: the gradients $\nabla g_1,\dots,\nabla g_m$ must be linearly independent at $\mathbf x^*$, which is the rank condition from the linear algebra course.

The FOC system has $n+m$ equations ($n$ from $\partial\mathcal L/\partial x_i$, $m$ from $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\lambda_j$) in $n+m$ unknowns.

Two constraints in portfolio theory
Minimising $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ subject to $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1=1$ (fully invested) and $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\mu=\mu_0$ (target return) is exactly this case, with two multipliers. Module 11 solves it and the answer traces the efficient frontier.

Check yourself: how many equations does the Lagrange system have for 4 variables and 2 constraints? (Answer: $4+2=6$, in the unknowns $x_1,\dots,x_4,\lambda_1,\lambda_2$.)
