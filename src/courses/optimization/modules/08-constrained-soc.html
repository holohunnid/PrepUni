08

Second-order conditions with constraints

the bordered Hessian — and the three ways to avoid it
READ FIRST The ordinary Hessian gives the wrong answer— ⊡ ✕

The Lagrange conditions of module 07 are necessary, so they produce candidates. Classifying them needs a second-order test — but not the one from module 05, and it is worth seeing exactly why before learning the replacement.

Take $\max xy$ subject to $x+y=4$, whose solution is $(2,2)$. The unconstrained Hessian of $f=xy$ is

$$H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$$

with $\det H=-1<0$: indefinite. The module 05 test would call $(2,2)$ a saddle point. And it is right — as an unconstrained statement. The function $xy$ genuinely has no maximum at $(2,2)$; move to $(3,3)$ and it rises to 9.

But $(3,3)$ is not feasible. The constrained problem only ever compares points on the line $x+y=4$, and along that line $(2,2)$ is a clear maximum. So the right question is not "is $f$ curving down in every direction?" but

The right question
Is $f$ curving down in every feasible direction — that is, in the directions that stay (to first order) on the constraint?

The bordered Hessian is the device that answers exactly that question. It takes the Hessian of the Lagrangian and borders it with the constraint gradient, and its determinants encode the curvature of $f$ restricted to the constraint surface. In two variables there is only one feasible direction (the tangent to the constraint), so only one number needs checking.

FIGURE A constrained maximum that is not an unconstrained one— ⊡ ✕

The objective is $f(x,y)=xy$ with constraint $x+y=4$. Slide the point along the constraint. The two profiles on the right show $f$ along the feasible direction (green, tangent to the constraint) and along the infeasible perpendicular direction (red).

At the optimum $(2,2)$:

  • The green profile is flat and curving down — a genuine maximum along the constraint. This is what the constrained SOC must detect.
  • The red profile is not even flat: it slopes upward, because $\nabla f=(2,2)\ne\mathbf 0$. Leaving the constraint improves $f$ — which is the whole reason the unconstrained FOC fails here.
  • The unconstrained Hessian is indefinite everywhere, so module 05's test says "saddle". Restricted to the feasible direction, the curvature is $-1$: negative, hence a maximum. Only the restriction matters.

Away from the optimum, the green profile has non-zero slope — that slope is the along-constraint component of $\nabla f$ from module 07, and it is what the Lagrange condition sets to zero.

DEF The bordered Hessian— ⊡ ✕

For one constraint $g(x,y)=c$ and two variables, form the $3\times3$ matrix

$$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & g_x & g_y\\ g_x & \mathcal L_{xx} & \mathcal L_{xy}\\ g_y & \mathcal L_{yx} & \mathcal L_{yy} \end{pmatrix}$$

where all entries are evaluated at the candidate $(x^*,y^*,\lambda^*)$, and $\mathcal L_{xx}$ etc. are second derivatives of the Lagrangian, not of $f$:

$$\mathcal L_{xx}=f_{xx}-\lambda g_{xx},\qquad \mathcal L_{xy}=f_{xy}-\lambda g_{xy},\qquad \mathcal L_{yy}=f_{yy}-\lambda g_{yy}$$
Trap
Use $\mathcal L$, not $f$. The two coincide only when the constraint is linear (then $g_{xx}=g_{xy}=g_{yy}=0$), which is the common exam case — but the moment the constraint is nonlinear, forgetting the $-\lambda g_{ij}$ terms gives a wrong classification. Also: $\lambda$ must be the value found from the FOC, not left symbolic.
The rule for $n=2$, $m=1$
$$\det\bar H>0\;\Rightarrow\;\text{local constrained }\textbf{maximum}$$ $$\det\bar H<0\;\Rightarrow\;\text{local constrained }\textbf{minimum}$$ $$\det\bar H=0\;\Rightarrow\;\text{inconclusive}$$

The signs look inverted relative to the unconstrained case, where a positive determinant meant a minimum. That is an artefact of the bordering row and column, not something to reason about — memorise the rule in this form: positive means maximum.

$\max xy$ subject to $x+y=4$

From module 07: $(x^*,y^*,\lambda^*)=(2,2,2)$. Here $g_x=g_y=1$ and all second derivatives of $g$ vanish, so $\mathcal L_{ij}=f_{ij}$: $\mathcal L_{xx}=0$, $\mathcal L_{xy}=1$, $\mathcal L_{yy}=0$.

$$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\1&0&1\\1&1&0\end{pmatrix}$$

Expanding along the first row: $\det\bar H=0(0-1)-1(0-1)+1(1-0)=0+1+1=2>0$.

So $(2,2)$ is a local constrained maximum ✓ — the opposite of what the unconstrained test claimed.

A nonlinear constraint: $\max x+y$ subject to $x^2+y^2=4$

From module 07: $(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)$ with $\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}$. Now $g_{xx}=g_{yy}=2$, $g_{xy}=0$, and $f_{ij}=0$, so

$\mathcal L_{xx}=0-\lambda(2)=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $\mathcal L_{yy}=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $\mathcal L_{xy}=0$. Also $g_x=g_y=2\sqrt2$.

$$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\sqrt2&2\sqrt2\\ 2\sqrt2&-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}&0\\ 2\sqrt2&0&-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\end{pmatrix}$$

$\det\bar H=0\left(\tfrac12\right)-2\sqrt2\left(2\sqrt2\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2})-0\right)+2\sqrt2\left(0+2\sqrt2\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)=-2\sqrt2(-2)+2\sqrt2(2)=8\sqrt2>0$

Maximum ✓, agreeing with the direct comparison of the two candidates. Note the $-\lambda g_{ij}$ terms were the only non-zero second derivatives here — omitting them would have left a singular matrix and no answer at all.

IDEA 1 The general pattern, and how to avoid needing it— ⊡ ✕

For $n$ variables and $m$ constraints the bordered Hessian is $(n+m)\times(n+m)$:

$$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf 0_{m\times m} & G\\ G^{\mathsf T} & \mathcal L_{\mathbf{xx}} \end{pmatrix},\qquad G=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla g_1^{\mathsf T}\\ \vdots\\ \nabla g_m^{\mathsf T}\end{pmatrix}$$
The rule
Examine the last $n-m$ leading principal minors, of orders $2m+1,\;2m+2,\;\dots,\;n+m$.

Local maximum: the signs alternate, the first (order $2m+1$) having the sign of $(-1)^{m+1}$.
Local minimum: all have the same sign, namely that of $(-1)^{m}$.

Two sanity checks on that rule. With $m=1,n=2$ there is one minor, of order 3, and the max condition asks for sign $(-1)^2=+$ — matching the boxed rule above ✓. With $m=1,n=3$ there are two minors, of orders 3 and 4; a maximum needs $+,-$ and a minimum needs $-,-$.

Trap
The number of minors is $n-m$, the number of genuinely free directions. If $n=m$ there are none — the constraints determine the point completely and there is nothing to classify.

Three ways to avoid the bordered Hessian

In practice you rarely need it, and examiners generally accept any correct argument. Prefer these, in this order:

  1. Compare the candidates. If the constraint set is compact (a circle, an ellipse, a budget simplex) and $f$ is continuous, Weierstrass guarantees both a maximum and a minimum exist. The Lagrange system produces finitely many candidates; evaluate $f$ at each and the largest is the maximum. No second-order work at all. This is what module 07's second example did.
  2. Substitute and use one-variable calculus. If the constraint can be solved for one variable, do it and apply the ordinary second-derivative test to the resulting one-variable function. For $\max xy$ s.t. $x+y=4$: $h(x)=x(4-x)$ has $h''=-2<0$ — a maximum, in one line.
  3. Use concavity. If $f$ is concave and the constraint is linear, the feasible set is convex, so module 03's theorem applies directly: any Lagrange stationary point is a global constrained maximum. This is the strongest conclusion of the three, and it is why the portfolio problems in module 11 need no second-order check.
The case that matters most
Minimising a convex quadratic $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ subject to linear constraints: convex objective, convex (affine) feasible set. The Lagrange FOC is then necessary and sufficient for a global minimum. Every mean–variance problem in finance is of this form, which is exactly why it is solvable in closed form.

Check yourself: you maximise a concave $f$ subject to $\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x=b$ and find one Lagrange candidate. What can you conclude, and what work is left? (Answer: it is the global constrained maximum — the feasible set is affine hence convex, and $f$ is concave. No second-order test is needed.)
