Worked examples

30 exam-style problems, full solutions, one insight each
HOW TO USE Attempt first, then open the solution

Each problem is written the way assessment questions are. Cover the solution, write your own on paper, then compare. The Insight box at the end is the transferable idea; if you only reread one thing per example, reread that. Difficulty: ● routine · ●● standard · ●●● needs two ideas combined. Ordered to follow modules 01–11.

A recurring instruction: every solution must say why the answer is global — by concavity, by a compact candidate list, or by coercivity. A local classification alone is an incomplete answer.

W01●setupMaximum, maximiser, supremum
For $f(x)=4-(x-2)^2$, find the maximum and the maximiser on (a) $\mathbb{R}$, (b) $[0,1]$, (c) $(0,1)$.
Solution
  1. (a) $f'(x)=-2(x-2)=0$ at $x=2$; $f''=-2<0$ everywhere, so $f$ is strictly concave and $x^*=2$ is the global maximiser. Maximum $=4$, maximiser $=2$.
  2. (b) On $[0,1]$ the critical point $x=2$ is outside the interval. Since $f'(x)=-2(x-2)>0$ for $x<2$, $f$ is increasing throughout $[0,1]$.
    Compare endpoints: $f(0)=0$, $f(1)=3$. Maximum $=3$ at $x=1$ (a corner solution); minimum $=0$ at $x=0$.
  3. (c) On the open interval the same values are approached but never attained. $\sup f=3$ and $\inf f=0$, but neither a maximum nor a minimum exists.
Insight
Three answers from one function, decided entirely by the feasible set. Part (b) is a corner solution the FOC cannot find; part (c) fails Weierstrass because the interval is not closed. Writing "$\max=3$" in (c) asserts an $x^*$ that does not exist — use $\sup$.
W02●setupMonotone transformation
Maximise $f(x,y)=x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$ subject to $x+y=10$, $x,y>0$, by transforming the objective.
Solution
  1. $\ln$ is strictly increasing, so $\arg\max f=\arg\max\ln f$. Maximise instead $$\ln f=\tfrac12\ln x+\tfrac12\ln y$$ which is a sum of concave functions, hence concave.
  2. $\mathcal L=\tfrac12\ln x+\tfrac12\ln y-\lambda(x+y-10)$.
  3. $\dfrac{1}{2x}=\lambda$ and $\dfrac{1}{2y}=\lambda$ ⇒ $x=y$.
  4. Constraint: $x=y=5$, and $\lambda=\tfrac{1}{10}$.
  5. Global: concave objective, linear (hence convex) constraint set ⇒ the FOC is sufficient.
  6. Report the value of the original objective: $f^*=\sqrt{5}\sqrt{5}=5$.
Insight
The transformation turned a product into a sum and a non-obvious problem into a concave one — the same trick that makes maximum likelihood tractable. Two things not to forget: the maximiser is unchanged but the value is not, so exponentiate before reporting; and $\lambda$ is the multiplier for the transformed objective, so it is not the shadow price of the original.
W03●one variableFOC, SOC, and a global claim
Maximise $f(x)=8\ln x-2x$ over $x>0$. Justify that your answer is global.
Solution
  1. FOC: $f'(x)=\dfrac8x-2=0\Rightarrow x^*=4$.
  2. SOC: $f''(x)=-\dfrac{8}{x^2}<0$ for all $x>0$.
  3. Global argument: $f''<0$ on the entire domain, so $f$ is strictly concave there. By module 03's Theorem 2 the stationary point is the unique global maximum — no comparison of candidates needed.
  4. Value: $f(4)=8\ln4-8\approx11.09-8=3.09$.
Insight
Note the wording of step 3. "$f''(4)<0$" would only give a local maximum; "$f''<0$ everywhere" gives concavity and hence a global one. On an unbounded domain like $(0,\infty)$ that distinction is essential, because there are no endpoints to fall back on.
W04●●one variableClosed-interval method
Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $f(x)=x^3-3x^2-9x+5$ on $[-2,4]$.
Solution
  1. Existence: $f$ is continuous and $[-2,4]$ is compact, so by Weierstrass both exist.
  2. Critical points: $f'(x)=3x^2-6x-9=3(x-3)(x+1)=0$ at $x=-1$ and $x=3$. Both lie in $(-2,4)$ ✓.
  3. Evaluate at critical points and both endpoints:
    $f(-2)=-8-12+18+5=3$
    $f(-1)=-1-3+9+5=10$
    $f(3)=27-27-27+5=-22$
    $f(4)=64-48-36+5=-15$
  4. Compare: absolute maximum $=10$ at $x=-1$; absolute minimum $=-22$ at $x=3$.
Insight
No classification was needed — only comparison, licensed by Weierstrass. Note that the endpoint $x=4$ beat the critical point $x=3$ for the minimum-adjacent ranking but did not win anything: had the interval been $[-2,5]$, $f(5)=50-75-45+5=-65$ would have made the endpoint the minimum instead. Endpoints always earn their place on the list.
W05●●one variableA corner solution
Maximise $f(x)=x^2-2x$ on $[0,\tfrac12]$. Verify the appropriate boundary condition.
Solution
  1. FOC: $f'(x)=2x-2=0$ at $x=1$ — outside the interval, so there is no interior candidate.
  2. On $[0,\tfrac12]$, $f'(x)=2x-2\le-1<0$, so $f$ is strictly decreasing.
  3. Compare endpoints: $f(0)=0$ and $f(\tfrac12)=\tfrac14-1=-\tfrac34$.
  4. Maximum $=0$ at the corner $x=0$; minimum $=-\tfrac34$ at $x=\tfrac12$.
  5. Boundary condition check: a maximum at the left endpoint requires $f'(0)\le0$. Here $f'(0)=-2\le0$ ✓ — moving right does not help.
Insight
At a corner the FOC is replaced by an inequality. Reporting "no maximum, since $f'\ne0$ anywhere in the interval" is the error this problem is built to catch. This inequality is the one-dimensional shadow of the KKT conditions in module 10.
W06●●one variableAn optimisation word problem
An open-top box is made from a $12\times12$ sheet by cutting equal squares of side $x$ from the corners and folding up the sides. Find the $x$ that maximises the volume.
Solution
  1. Objective: base $(12-2x)\times(12-2x)$, height $x$, so $V(x)=x(12-2x)^2$.
  2. Domain: $0\le x\le6$ (beyond 6 the base has negative side). Compact ⇒ Weierstrass applies ✓.
  3. FOC: $V'(x)=(12-2x)^2+x\cdot2(12-2x)(-2)=(12-2x)\big[(12-2x)-4x\big]=(12-2x)(12-6x)$.
  4. Zero at $x=6$ and $x=2$.
  5. Compare candidates and endpoints: $V(0)=0$, $V(2)=2(8)^2=128$, $V(6)=0$.
  6. Answer: cut squares of side $x=2$; maximum volume $128$ cubic units.
Insight
Factoring $V'$ rather than expanding to a quadratic saved all the work and exposed both roots at once. The two zero-volume endpoints are a useful sanity check: a volume problem whose interior candidate did not beat them would signal an algebra error.
W07●●one variableUnbounded domain, coercivity
Maximise $f(x)=-x^4+4x^2$ over $\mathbb{R}$. Does a maximum exist, and is it unique?
Solution
  1. Existence: the domain is unbounded, so Weierstrass does not apply directly. But $f(x)\to-\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$ (the $-x^4$ term dominates), so $f$ is coercive and a global maximum exists among the critical points.
  2. FOC: $f'(x)=-4x^3+8x=-4x(x^2-2)=0$ at $x=0$ and $x=\pm\sqrt2$.
  3. SOC: $f''(x)=-12x^2+8$. At $x=0$: $f''=8>0$ ⇒ local minimum. At $x=\pm\sqrt2$: $f''=-24+8=-16<0$ ⇒ local maxima.
  4. Compare: $f(0)=0$; $f(\pm\sqrt2)=-4+8=4$.
  5. Answer: global maximum $=4$, attained at both $x=\sqrt2$ and $x=-\sqrt2$. Not unique.
Insight
Coercivity supplied existence without concavity — and it had to, because $f$ is not concave ($f''(0)>0$). Non-uniqueness follows: strict concavity is what forces a single maximiser, and here the symmetry $f(-x)=f(x)$ makes two.
W08●●convexityConcave, convex, or neither?
Classify on $\mathbb{R}^2$: (a) $f(x,y)=-x^2-4y^2+2xy$, (b) $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-3xy$.
Solution
  1. (a) $f_{xx}=-2$, $f_{yy}=-8$, $f_{xy}=2$, so $H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\2&-8\end{pmatrix}$ — constant, since $f$ is quadratic.
  2. Leading minors: $D_1=-2<0$, $D_2=16-4=12>0$. Alternating starting negative ⇒ negative definite ⇒ $f$ is strictly concave on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  3. (b) $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&-3\\-3&2\end{pmatrix}$, $D_1=2>0$ but $D_2=4-9=-5<0$.
  4. Neither all-positive nor alternating ⇒ indefinite ⇒ $g$ is neither convex nor concave.
Insight
In (b) both diagonal entries are positive, so each axis-slice is convex — yet the function is not. The cross term $-3xy$ is large enough to create a downward direction. This is the trap of module 05: never classify from $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ alone. Because both objectives are quadratic, one Hessian computation settles the structure everywhere.
W09●●convexityWhich sets are convex?
Determine whether each is convex: (a) $\{(x,y):x+2y\le4,\;x\ge0\}$, (b) $\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\ge1\}$, (c) $\{(x,y):xy\ge1,\;x>0\}$.
Solution
  1. (a) Convex. Each inequality defines a half-space, which is convex, and an intersection of convex sets is convex.
  2. (b) Not convex. Counterexample: $(1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$ are both in the set, but their midpoint $(0,0)$ has $0^2+0^2=0<1$ — outside. One failing pair is enough.
  3. (c) Convex. On $x>0$ the condition $xy\ge1$ is $y\ge1/x$, and $1/x$ is a convex function there. The region lying above the graph of a convex function (its epigraph) is convex.
  4. Check with a chord: $(1,1)$ and $(4,0.25)$ both satisfy $xy=1$; their midpoint $(2.5,0.625)$ has $xy=1.5625\ge1$ ✓ — the curve bulges away from the chord, as an epigraph must.
Insight
To disprove convexity, produce one explicit pair — as in (b). To prove it, do not hunt for pairs: recognise the set as an intersection of half-spaces, a ball, or the region above a convex function. The reversed inequality in (b) is exactly what breaks it: $\{x^2+y^2\le1\}$ is convex.
W10●●●convexityCobb–Douglas concavity and returns to scale
For $f(x,y)=x^{a}y^{b}$ on $x,y>0$ with $a,b>0$, show that $f$ is concave if and only if $a+b\le1$. Apply it to $a=b=0.3$ and $a=b=0.8$.
Solution
  1. $f_x=ax^{a-1}y^{b}$, $f_y=bx^{a}y^{b-1}$.
  2. $f_{xx}=a(a-1)x^{a-2}y^{b}$,  $f_{yy}=b(b-1)x^{a}y^{b-2}$,  $f_{xy}=ab\,x^{a-1}y^{b-1}$.
  3. $\det H=ab(a-1)(b-1)x^{2a-2}y^{2b-2}-a^2b^2x^{2a-2}y^{2b-2}=ab\,x^{2a-2}y^{2b-2}\big[(a-1)(b-1)-ab\big]$.
  4. Expand the bracket: $(a-1)(b-1)-ab=ab-a-b+1-ab=1-a-b$. So $$\det H=ab\,x^{2a-2}y^{2b-2}\,(1-a-b)$$
  5. Since $a,b>0$ and $x,y>0$, the prefactor is positive, so $\det H>0\iff a+b<1$.
  6. And for $0<a<1$, $f_{xx}=a(a-1)x^{a-2}y^b<0$. So $\det H>0$ with $f_{xx}<0$ ⇒ negative definite ⇒ strictly concave exactly when $a+b<1$ (with $a+b=1$ giving the semidefinite, still-concave, boundary case).
  7. $a=b=0.3$: $a+b=0.6<1$ ⇒ concave. Decreasing returns to scale, and the profit-maximisation problem is well behaved.
  8. $a=b=0.8$: $a+b=1.6>1$ ⇒ $\det H<0$ ⇒ indefinite ⇒ not concave. It remains quasi-concave (its upper contour sets are still convex), so it can represent preferences, but the FOC no longer certifies a maximum.
Insight
The exponent sum controls everything: $a+b$ is the degree of homogeneity, so concavity and decreasing returns to scale are the same condition. With increasing returns the firm's problem has no interior solution — it wants unbounded scale — and the mathematics fails at exactly the point where the economics does.
W11●FOCA quadratic in two variables
Find and classify the stationary point of $f(x,y)=3x^2+2y^2-2xy-8x-4y$, and say whether it is global.
Solution
  1. FOC: $f_x=6x-2y-8=0$ and $f_y=4y-2x-4=0$.
  2. From the second, $x=2y-2$. Substituting: $6(2y-2)-2y-8=0\Rightarrow10y=20\Rightarrow y=2$, then $x=2$.
  3. Hessian: $H=\begin{pmatrix}6&-2\\-2&4\end{pmatrix}$, constant.
  4. $D_1=6>0$ and $D_2=24-4=20>0$ ⇒ positive definite ⇒ strict local minimum.
  5. Global: $H$ is the same at every point, so $f$ is strictly convex on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and $(2,2)$ is the unique global minimum.
  6. $f(2,2)=12+8-8-16-8=-12$.
Insight
For a quadratic objective the Hessian is constant, so a single definiteness check does double duty: it classifies the point and establishes global convexity. That is why quadratic problems — every mean–variance problem in finance — are so much easier than general ones.
W12●●FOCA nonlinear FOC system
Find all stationary points of $f(x,y)=2x^3+xy^2+5x^2+y^2$.
Solution
  1. $f_x=6x^2+y^2+10x$ and $f_y=2xy+2y=2y(x+1)$.
  2. Start with the factorable equation. $f_y=0$ requires $y=0$ or $x=-1$ — two branches, and both must be explored.
  3. Branch 1 ($y=0$): $f_x=6x^2+10x=2x(3x+5)=0$ ⇒ $x=0$ or $x=-\tfrac53$.
    Points: $(0,0)$ and $\left(-\tfrac53,0\right)$.
  4. Branch 2 ($x=-1$): $f_x=6+y^2-10=0\Rightarrow y^2=4\Rightarrow y=\pm2$.
    Points: $(-1,2)$ and $(-1,-2)$.
  5. Four stationary points: $(0,0)$, $\left(-\tfrac53,0\right)$, $(-1,2)$, $(-1,-2)$.
Insight
Always attack the equation that factors first: it splits the problem into cases instead of forcing a substitution. Dropping a branch — here, forgetting $x=-1$ — silently loses half the candidates, and W15 will show that those are the interesting ones.
W13●●FOCProfit maximisation with two inputs
A firm has $F(L,K)=L^{1/4}K^{1/4}$, output price $p=8$, and input prices $w=r=1$. Choose $L,K>0$ to maximise profit, and justify globality.
Solution
  1. Objective: $\pi(L,K)=8L^{1/4}K^{1/4}-L-K$.
  2. FOC: $\pi_L=2L^{-3/4}K^{1/4}-1=0$ and $\pi_K=2L^{1/4}K^{-3/4}-1=0$.
  3. Divide one by the other to eliminate the common factor: $$\frac{L^{-3/4}K^{1/4}}{L^{1/4}K^{-3/4}}=\frac{K}{L}=1\ \Rightarrow\ K=L$$
  4. Substitute back: $2L^{-3/4}L^{1/4}=2L^{-1/2}=1\Rightarrow L^{1/2}=2\Rightarrow L=K=4$.
  5. Global: $F$ has exponents summing to $\tfrac12\le1$, so by W10 it is concave; $\pi=8F-L-K$ is concave plus linear, hence concave. The FOC is therefore sufficient for a global maximum.
  6. $\pi^*=8\cdot4^{1/4}4^{1/4}-8=8\cdot 4^{1/2}-8=16-8=8$.
Insight
Each FOC says value of the marginal product = input price, $p\,\partial F/\partial L=w$. Dividing the two FOCs cancels the messy common term and leaves the cost-minimising input ratio — the standard first move with Cobb–Douglas, and far quicker than substitution.
W14●●SOCDetecting a saddle
Classify the stationary point of $f(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2$ at the origin, and find a direction in which $f$ decreases.
Solution
  1. FOC: $f_x=2x+4y=0$ and $f_y=4x+2y=0$. The only solution is $(0,0)$.
  2. Hessian: $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}$, with $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=4-16=-12<0$.
  3. $D<0$ ⇒ indefinite ⇒ the origin is a saddle point.
  4. A decreasing direction: try $y=-x$. Then $f(x,-x)=x^2-4x^2+x^2=-2x^2<0$ for $x\ne0$ — strictly below $f(0,0)=0$.
  5. Along $y=x$, by contrast, $f(x,x)=6x^2>0$ — increasing. Up one way, down the other: the definition of a saddle.
Insight
Both $f_{xx}=2$ and $f_{yy}=2$ are positive, so the two axis slices are convex and suggest a minimum. The cross term is what creates the downhill direction, and only $D$ detects it. Finding an explicit decreasing direction — here the eigenvector $(1,-1)$, with eigenvalue $2-4=-2$ — turns the classification into a proof.
W15●●●SOCClassify all four (continues W12)
Classify each stationary point of $f(x,y)=2x^3+xy^2+5x^2+y^2$ found in W12, and state whether $f$ has a global maximum on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Solution
  1. Second partials: $f_{xx}=12x+10$,  $f_{yy}=2x+2$,  $f_{xy}=2y$. So $D=(12x+10)(2x+2)-4y^2$.
  2. $(0,0)$: $f_{xx}=10$, $f_{yy}=2$, $f_{xy}=0$. $D=20>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$ ⇒ strict local minimum, $f=0$.
  3. $\left(-\tfrac53,0\right)$: $f_{xx}=-20+10=-10$, $f_{yy}=-\tfrac{10}{3}+2=-\tfrac43$, $f_{xy}=0$. $D=(-10)(-\tfrac43)=\tfrac{40}{3}>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$ ⇒ strict local maximum.
  4. $(-1,2)$: $f_{xx}=-2$, $f_{yy}=0$, $f_{xy}=4$. $D=0-16=-16<0$ ⇒ saddle.
  5. $(-1,-2)$: $f_{xx}=-2$, $f_{yy}=0$, $f_{xy}=-4$. $D=-16<0$ ⇒ saddle.
  6. Global maximum? No. Along the $x$-axis, $f(x,0)=2x^3+5x^2\to+\infty$ as $x\to+\infty$, so $f$ is unbounded above. The local maximum at $\left(-\tfrac53,0\right)$ is emphatically not global.
Insight
All three types in one function, and the final step is the one that matters: a local classification says nothing about global behaviour. The one-line check "what does $f$ do along a ray to infinity?" settles it and costs nothing — do it whenever the domain is unbounded and the objective is not concave.
W16●●SOCA $3\times3$ Hessian
Maximise $f(x,y,z)=-x^2-2y^2-3z^2+2xy+2yz$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Solution
  1. Hessian (constant, since $f$ is quadratic): $$H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2&0\\2&-4&2\\0&2&-6\end{pmatrix}$$
  2. Leading principal minors:
    $D_1=-2<0$
    $D_2=(-2)(-4)-2^2=8-4=4>0$
    $D_3=-2\big[(-4)(-6)-4\big]-2\big[2(-6)-0\big]+0=-2(20)+24=-16<0$
  3. Signs $-,+,-$: alternating and starting negative ⇒ negative definite ⇒ $f$ is strictly concave on $\mathbb{R}^3$.
  4. FOC: $f_x=-2x+2y=0\Rightarrow x=y$;  $f_z=-6z+2y=0\Rightarrow z=\tfrac y3$;  $f_y=-4y+2x+2z=0$.
  5. Substituting: $-4y+2y+\tfrac{2y}{3}=0\Rightarrow-\tfrac{4y}{3}=0\Rightarrow y=0$, hence $x=y=z=0$.
  6. Answer: unique global maximum at the origin, $f^*=0$, by concave programming.
Insight
Establishing concavity before solving the FOC turns the last step into a citation rather than an argument. Remember the negative-definite pattern is alternating from negative, not "all negative" — $D_2$ must be positive.
W17●●●globalFull concave-programming write-up
Maximise $f(x,y)=-x^2-2y^2+2xy+4x+4y$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Give a complete argument.
Solution
  1. Structure first. $H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&2\\2&-4\end{pmatrix}$, constant. $D_1=-2<0$, $D_2=8-4=4>0$ ⇒ negative definite ⇒ $f$ is strictly concave on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. Existence and sufficiency. By module 03 Theorem 2, any stationary point is the unique global maximum; by strict concavity it is unique. No further checks are required.
  3. FOC. $f_x=-2x+2y+4=0\Rightarrow y=x-2$.  $f_y=-4y+2x+4=0\Rightarrow x=2y-2$.
  4. Substituting: $x=2(x-2)-2=2x-6\Rightarrow x=6$, hence $y=4$.
  5. Value. $f(6,4)=-36-32+48+24+16=20$.
  6. Conclusion. Unique global maximum $20$ at $(6,4)$.
Insight
This is the model answer format: structure, then sufficiency, then computation. It is shorter than the usual FOC-then-SOC-then-hand-waving order and it is airtight, because the global claim is made before any candidate is even found.
W18●●●globalCoercive but not concave
Find the global maximum of $f(x,y)=-x^4+2x^2-y^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, and explain why concave programming cannot be used.
Solution
  1. FOC: $f_x=-4x^3+4x=-4x(x^2-1)=0$ ⇒ $x=0,\pm1$;  $f_y=-2y=0$ ⇒ $y=0$.
    Stationary points: $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(-1,0)$.
  2. Hessian: $f_{xx}=-12x^2+4$, $f_{yy}=-2$, $f_{xy}=0$.
  3. $(0,0)$: $H=\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$ — indefinite ⇒ saddle. (This is why $f$ is not concave: a concave function cannot have a positive curvature direction anywhere.)
  4. $(\pm1,0)$: $f_{xx}=-8$, so $D=(-8)(-2)-0=16>0$ with $f_{xx}<0$ ⇒ strict local maxima.
  5. Existence: as $\|(x,y)\|\to\infty$, the $-x^4$ and $-y^2$ terms dominate and $f\to-\infty$ in every direction ⇒ coercive ⇒ a global maximum exists and is among the stationary points.
  6. Compare: $f(0,0)=0$; $f(\pm1,0)=-1+2-0=1$.
  7. Answer: global maximum $=1$, attained at both $(1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$.
Insight
Concavity was unavailable, so coercivity supplied existence and an exhaustive comparison supplied the answer — argument 3 followed by argument 2 from module 06. Non-uniqueness is the expected price: without strict concavity there is nothing forcing a single maximiser.
W19●LagrangeA first Lagrange problem
Maximise $f(x,y)=x^2y$ subject to $x+y=3$, with $x,y>0$. Report $x^*$, $y^*$, $f^*$ and $\lambda^*$.
Solution
  1. $\mathcal L=x^2y-\lambda(x+y-3)$.
  2. $\mathcal L_x=2xy-\lambda=0$;  $\mathcal L_y=x^2-\lambda=0$;  $\mathcal L_\lambda=-(x+y-3)=0$.
  3. Equating the first two: $2xy=x^2$. Since $x>0$ we may divide: $x=2y$.
  4. Constraint: $2y+y=3\Rightarrow y^*=1$, $x^*=2$.
  5. $\lambda^*=x^2=4$, and $f^*=4\cdot1=4$.
  6. Check by substitution: $h(y)=(3-y)^2y$, $h'(y)=-2(3-y)y+(3-y)^2=(3-y)(3-3y)=0$ at $y=1$ ✓, and $h''(1)=-4<0$ ⇒ a maximum ✓.
Insight
Dividing by $x$ was legitimate only because the problem states $x>0$; without that, $x=0$ is a separate branch to check. The substitution cross-check is cheap here and confirms both the point and its type — do it whenever the constraint is easy to solve.
W20●●LagrangeBoth extremes on a circle
Find the maximum and minimum of $f(x,y)=x+2y$ subject to $x^2+y^2=5$.
Solution
  1. Existence: the circle is compact and $f$ continuous ⇒ both a maximum and a minimum exist (Weierstrass). Comparing candidates will therefore settle everything — no second-order test needed.
  2. $\mathcal L=x+2y-\lambda(x^2+y^2-5)$.
  3. $1-2\lambda x=0$ and $2-2\lambda y=0$. Neither allows $\lambda=0$, so $x=\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}$ and $y=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$, hence $y=2x$.
  4. Constraint: $x^2+4x^2=5\Rightarrow x^2=1\Rightarrow x=\pm1$.
    Candidates: $(1,2)$ and $(-1,-2)$.
  5. Values: $f(1,2)=1+4=5$;  $f(-1,-2)=-5$.
  6. Maximum $=5$ at $(1,2)$ with $\lambda=\tfrac12$; minimum $=-5$ at $(-1,-2)$ with $\lambda=-\tfrac12$.
Insight
The Lagrange system finds every constrained extremum at once, so reporting only one is a half-answer. Note the sanity check: $\max=\|\nabla f\|\cdot r=\sqrt5\cdot\sqrt5=5$ ✓ — maximising a linear function on a circle is Cauchy–Schwarz, attained where the point aligns with the gradient $(1,2)$.
W21●●LagrangeConsumer choice, and $\lambda$ verified
Maximise $u(x,y)=x^{1/3}y^{2/3}$ subject to $2x+4y=24$. Find $x^*,y^*,\lambda^*$ and verify that $\lambda^*=\partial V/\partial m$.
Solution
  1. $u_x=\tfrac13x^{-2/3}y^{2/3}$,  $u_y=\tfrac23x^{1/3}y^{-1/3}$.
  2. Eliminate $\lambda$ by dividing the two FOCs: $$\frac{u_x}{u_y}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{y}{x}=\frac{p_x}{p_y}=\frac{2}{4}=\frac12\ \Rightarrow\ y=x$$
  3. Budget: $2x+4x=24\Rightarrow x^*=4$, $y^*=4$.
  4. $u^*=4^{1/3}4^{2/3}=4$.  $\lambda^*=\dfrac{u_x}{p_x}=\dfrac{\tfrac13\cdot4^{-2/3}4^{2/3}}{2}=\dfrac{1/3}{2}=\dfrac16$.
  5. Verification. For general income $m$, the Cobb–Douglas rule gives $p_xx=\tfrac13m$ and $p_yy=\tfrac23m$, so $x=\tfrac{m}{6}$, $y=\tfrac{m}{6}$ and $$V(m)=\left(\tfrac m6\right)^{1/3}\left(\tfrac m6\right)^{2/3}=\frac m6$$
  6. Hence $V'(m)=\tfrac16=\lambda^*$ ✓.
Insight
Dividing the FOCs produces "MRS = price ratio" and removes $\lambda$ from the hard part of the calculation — recover it at the end only if asked. The verification shows $\lambda$ is the marginal utility of income: one extra euro buys $\tfrac16$ of a utility unit, at every income level, because this utility has constant returns to scale.
W22●●●LagrangeTwo constraints
Minimise $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2$ subject to $x+y+z=6$ and $x-y=2$.
Solution
  1. Two constraints ⇒ two multipliers: $$\mathcal L=x^2+y^2+z^2-\lambda(x+y+z-6)-\gamma(x-y-2)$$
  2. FOC: $2x=\lambda+\gamma$,  $2y=\lambda-\gamma$,  $2z=\lambda$.
  3. Add all three: $2(x+y+z)=3\lambda$. Using the first constraint, $12=3\lambda\Rightarrow\lambda=4$, hence $z=2$.
  4. Subtract the second FOC from the first: $2(x-y)=2\gamma\Rightarrow\gamma=x-y=2$ by the second constraint.
  5. Then $x=\dfrac{4+2}{2}=3$ and $y=\dfrac{4-2}{2}=1$.
  6. Check: $3+1+2=6$ ✓ and $3-1=2$ ✓.  $f^*=9+1+4=14$.
  7. Global: $f$ is strictly convex ($H=2I$, positive definite) and the feasible set is the intersection of two hyperplanes, hence affine and convex. So this is the unique global minimum.
Insight
Rather than solving five equations blindly, combine them: summing the FOCs produced $\lambda$ immediately from the first constraint, and differencing produced $\gamma$ from the second. Each multiplier is recovered from the constraint it belongs to. Geometrically the answer is the point of the intersection line closest to the origin — a projection, exactly as in W20's cousin from module 07.
W23●●constrained SOCThe bordered Hessian
For $\max\;x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$ subject to $x+y=10$, find the candidate and confirm it is a maximum using the bordered Hessian.
Solution
  1. $\mathcal L=x^{1/2}y^{1/2}-\lambda(x+y-10)$.
  2. $\tfrac12x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}=\lambda$ and $\tfrac12x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}=\lambda$. Dividing: $\dfrac yx=1\Rightarrow x=y=5$, and $\lambda=\tfrac12$.
  3. Second derivatives at $(5,5)$. Since the constraint is linear, all $g_{ij}=0$ and $\mathcal L_{ij}=f_{ij}$:
    $f_{xx}=-\tfrac14x^{-3/2}y^{1/2}=-\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{1}{5}=-\tfrac1{20}$
    $f_{yy}=-\tfrac1{20}$  (by symmetry)
    $f_{xy}=\tfrac14x^{-1/2}y^{-1/2}=\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac15=\tfrac1{20}$
  4. With $g_x=g_y=1$: $$\bar H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&1\\ 1&-\tfrac1{20}&\tfrac1{20}\\ 1&\tfrac1{20}&-\tfrac1{20}\end{pmatrix}$$
  5. Expanding along the first row: $$\det\bar H=-1\left(1\cdot\left(-\tfrac1{20}\right)-\tfrac1{20}\cdot1\right)+1\left(\tfrac1{20}-\left(-\tfrac1{20}\right)\right)=-1\left(-\tfrac2{20}\right)+\tfrac2{20}=\tfrac15>0$$
  6. $\det\bar H>0$ ⇒ local constrained maximum ✓, with $f^*=5$.
  7. Faster route: $\sqrt{xy}$ is concave on the positive quadrant (W10 with $a=b=\tfrac12$, $a+b=1$) and the constraint is linear ⇒ this is the global maximum, with no determinant needed.
Insight
Both routes are correct, and the second is stronger — it gives a global rather than local conclusion, in one line. Learn the bordered Hessian because it can be examined, but reach for concavity first. Note also that all $g_{ij}=0$ only because the constraint is linear; a nonlinear constraint would require the $-\lambda g_{ij}$ terms.
W24●●shadow priceInterpreting and checking $\lambda$
Maximise $xy$ subject to $2x+3y=12$. Find $\lambda^*$, use it to predict the effect of raising the right-hand side to 13, and compare with the exact answer.
Solution
  1. $\mathcal L=xy-\lambda(2x+3y-12)$ gives $y=2\lambda$ and $x=3\lambda$.
  2. Constraint: $2(3\lambda)+3(2\lambda)=12\Rightarrow12\lambda=12\Rightarrow\lambda^*=1$, so $x^*=3$, $y^*=2$, $f^*=6$.
  3. Prediction: $\Delta V\approx\lambda\,\Delta c=1\times1=1$, so $V(13)\approx7$.
  4. Exact value function. Repeating with a general $c$: $x=3\lambda$, $y=2\lambda$, $12\lambda=c$ so $\lambda=\tfrac{c}{12}$, giving $x=\tfrac c4$, $y=\tfrac c6$ and $$V(c)=\frac{c}{4}\cdot\frac{c}{6}=\frac{c^2}{24}$$
  5. Check: $V'(c)=\dfrac{c}{12}=\lambda$ ✓, and $V(12)=6$ ✓.
  6. Exact: $V(13)=\dfrac{169}{24}\approx7.042$, so the true increase is $0.042$ more than predicted.
Insight
$\lambda$ is a derivative, so $\Delta V\approx\lambda\Delta c$ is a first-order approximation — accurate for small changes, and here erring low because $V$ is convex ($V''=\tfrac1{12}>0$). The convexity of $V$ is itself economic information: each extra unit of the resource is worth more than the last.
W25●●●envelopeHotelling, and a tax
A firm solves $\pi(p)=\max_{q\ge0}\left(pq-\tfrac12q^2-10\right)$. (a) Find $\pi(p)$ and verify $\pi'(p)=q^*(p)$. (b) With a per-unit tax $t$, so profit is $(p-t)q-\tfrac12q^2-10$, find $d\pi/dt$ without recomputing $q^*$.
Solution
  1. (a) FOC: $p-q=0\Rightarrow q^*(p)=p$. SOC: $\pi_{qq}=-1<0$ ⇒ concave in $q$ ⇒ global maximum.
  2. $\pi(p)=p\cdot p-\tfrac12p^2-10=\tfrac12p^2-10$.
  3. Directly: $\pi'(p)=p=q^*(p)$ ✓ — Hotelling's lemma.
  4. Via the envelope theorem, without ever computing $q^*(p)$ as a function: $$\frac{d\pi}{dp}=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\left(pq-\tfrac12q^2-10\right)\bigg|_{q=q^*}=q^*$$
  5. (b) Same move with the parameter $t$: $$\frac{d\pi}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left((p-t)q-\tfrac12q^2-10\right)\bigg|_{q=q^*}=-q^*=-(p-t)$$
  6. So a small tax rise costs the firm approximately its current output in profit, per unit of tax.
Insight
The envelope theorem says the indirect effect — the profit change caused by the firm adjusting $q$ — is zero to first order, because the firm was already at a point where $\partial\pi/\partial q=0$. Only the direct effect of the parameter survives. That is why $d\pi/dt=-q^*$ needs no $dq^*/dt$ at all.
W26●●KKTCase checking with one inequality
Maximise $f(x,y)=-(x-4)^2-(y-2)^2$ subject to $x+y\le3$.
Solution
  1. Case A — assume the constraint is slack ($\lambda=0$). Then solve $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$: $x=4$, $y=2$.
    Feasibility check: $4+2=6>3$ ✗ — infeasible. Discard.
  2. Case B — assume it binds ($x+y=3$). $\mathcal L=-(x-4)^2-(y-2)^2-\lambda(x+y-3)$.
  3. $-2(x-4)-\lambda=0$ and $-2(y-2)-\lambda=0$ ⇒ $x-4=y-2$ ⇒ $x=y+2$.
  4. Constraint: $(y+2)+y=3\Rightarrow y^*=\tfrac12$, $x^*=\tfrac52$.
  5. Sign check: $\lambda=-2\left(\tfrac52-4\right)=3\ge0$ ✓ — valid.
  6. $f^*=-\left(-\tfrac32\right)^2-\left(-\tfrac32\right)^2=-\tfrac94-\tfrac94=-\tfrac92$.
  7. Global: $f$ is concave (Hessian $-2I$) and the constraint function is linear (hence convex), so KKT is sufficient — this is the global maximum.
Insight
The two cases check complementary things: Case A assumes $\lambda=0$ and verifies feasibility; Case B assumes $g=c$ and verifies the sign of $\lambda$. Exactly one survived, as it must for a strictly concave objective. Geometrically the answer is the projection of the unconstrained optimum $(4,2)$ onto the line $x+y=3$.
W27●●●KKTWith non-negativity constraints
Maximise $f(x,y)=4x+3y-x^2-y^2$ subject to $x+y\le2$, $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$.
Solution
  1. Structure. $H=\begin{pmatrix}-2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$ is negative definite ⇒ $f$ concave; all constraints linear ⇒ feasible set convex. KKT will be necessary and sufficient.
  2. Case A — all constraints slack. $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$: $4-2x=0\Rightarrow x=2$; $3-2y=0\Rightarrow y=1.5$.
    Check $x+y=3.5>2$ ✗ — infeasible. So the budget constraint binds.
  3. Case B — budget binds, both variables interior. $\mathcal L=4x+3y-x^2-y^2-\lambda(x+y-2)$.
  4. $\mathcal L_x=4-2x-\lambda=0$ and $\mathcal L_y=3-2y-\lambda=0$.
  5. Subtracting: $4-2x=3-2y\Rightarrow y=x-\tfrac12$.
  6. Constraint: $x+x-\tfrac12=2\Rightarrow x^*=1.25$, $y^*=0.75$.
  7. Checks: $x^*>0$ ✓ and $y^*>0$ ✓ (so the non-negativity constraints are slack and their multipliers are zero); $\lambda=4-2(1.25)=1.5\ge0$ ✓.
  8. $f^*=5+2.25-1.5625-0.5625=5.125$.
  9. By step 1 this is the global maximum.
Insight
Three constraints mean $2^3$ possible patterns, but the work collapses because the concave objective's unconstrained optimum lies in the positive quadrant — only the budget could bind. Always locate the unconstrained optimum first: its position tells you which constraints could possibly be active, and usually eliminates most cases before you write anything down.
W28●●portfolioTwo-asset minimum variance
Assets A and B have $\sigma_A=20\%$, $\sigma_B=30\%$, $\rho=0.2$. Find the minimum-variance weights and the resulting portfolio volatility. Compare with holding A alone.
Solution
  1. $\sigma_A^2=0.04$, $\sigma_B^2=0.09$, $\sigma_{AB}=\rho\sigma_A\sigma_B=0.2(0.2)(0.3)=0.012$.
  2. $$w^*=\frac{\sigma_B^2-\sigma_{AB}}{\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2-2\sigma_{AB}}=\frac{0.09-0.012}{0.04+0.09-0.024}=\frac{0.078}{0.106}=0.736$$
  3. So $73.6\%$ in A and $26.4\%$ in B.
  4. $\sigma_p^2=(0.736)^2(0.04)+(0.264)^2(0.09)+2(0.736)(0.264)(0.012)$
    $=0.021656+0.006272+0.004664=0.032592$
  5. $\sigma_p=\sqrt{0.032592}=0.1805$, i.e. 18.05%.
  6. Comparison: A alone gives $20\%$, B alone $30\%$. The mix beats both — including the safer asset on its own.
  7. Second-order: $\dfrac{d^2\sigma_p^2}{dw^2}=2(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_B^2-2\sigma_{AB})=0.212>0$ ⇒ convex ⇒ this is the global minimum.
Insight
Adding a riskier asset reduced total risk — the central result of diversification, and it is pure convex optimisation. It works because $\rho<1$: the second derivative $2\operatorname{Var}(R_A-R_B)$ is strictly positive whenever the assets are not perfectly correlated, guaranteeing an interior minimum.
W29●●●portfolioThree assets in matrix form
Three uncorrelated assets have volatilities $20\%$, $30\%$, $40\%$. Using $\mathbf w^*=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1/(\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$, find the minimum-variance portfolio and its volatility.
Solution
  1. Uncorrelated ⇒ $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(0.04,\;0.09,\;0.16)$, so $\Sigma^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(25,\;11.111,\;6.25)$.
  2. $\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1=(25,\;11.111,\;6.25)^{\mathsf T}$.
  3. $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1=25+11.111+6.25=42.361$.
  4. $$\mathbf w^*=\frac{1}{42.361}(25,\;11.111,\;6.25)^{\mathsf T}=(0.590,\;0.262,\;0.148)^{\mathsf T}$$
  5. Variance. For this portfolio the formula collapses neatly: $$\sigma_p^2=\mathbf w^{*\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w^*=\frac{1}{\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}=\frac{1}{42.361}=0.02361$$
  6. $\sigma_p=\sqrt{0.02361}=0.1536$, i.e. 15.4% — below even the safest single asset at $20\%$.
  7. Global: $\Sigma$ is positive definite ⇒ the objective is strictly convex; the constraint is linear ⇒ the feasible set is affine. The FOC is therefore sufficient and the solution is unique.
Insight
With uncorrelated assets the weights come out inversely proportional to variance, $w_i\propto1/\sigma_i^2$ — the riskiest asset gets the smallest allocation, but not zero. The clean identity $\sigma_p^2=1/(\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$ follows by substituting $\mathbf w^*$ into $\mathbf w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\mathbf w$ and is worth remembering as a check.
W30●●●estimationLeast squares and maximum likelihood agree
For regression through the origin, $y_i=\beta x_i+\varepsilon_i$ with data $x=(1,2,3)$, $y=(2,5,7)$: (a) find the least-squares $\hat\beta$ and prove it is a global minimum; (b) show that assuming $\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ i.i.d. and maximising the likelihood gives the same estimate.
Solution
  1. (a) Objective: $S(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}(y_i-\beta x_i)^2$.
  2. FOC: $S'(\beta)=-2\sum x_i(y_i-\beta x_i)=0\Rightarrow\sum x_iy_i=\beta\sum x_i^2$, so $$\hat\beta=\frac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2}$$
  3. $\sum x_iy_i=2+10+21=33$ and $\sum x_i^2=1+4+9=14$, so $\hat\beta=\dfrac{33}{14}\approx2.357$.
  4. Global: $S''(\beta)=2\sum x_i^2=28>0$ for all $\beta$ ⇒ $S$ is strictly convex ⇒ the FOC gives the unique global minimum.
  5. (b) Log-likelihood. With normal errors, $$\ell(\beta)=-\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(y_i-\beta x_i)^2$$
  6. The first term does not involve $\beta$, and the second is $-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}S(\beta)$ with $\sigma^2>0$. So maximising $\ell$ is minimising $S$ — a positive rescaling plus a constant, which by module 01 leaves the argmax unchanged.
  7. Hence $\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}}=\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}}=\dfrac{33}{14}$.
  8. $\ell''(\beta)=-\dfrac{\sum x_i^2}{\sigma^2}=-\dfrac{14}{\sigma^2}<0$ ⇒ strictly concave ⇒ global maximum, and $-\ell''$ is the Fisher information: the estimator's variance is $\sigma^2/14$.
Insight
Two apparently different principles — minimise squared error, maximise probability — give the same answer because the normal density is $e^{-(\text{squared error})}$. The equivalence is not a coincidence and it fails for other error distributions. Note also the last line: the curvature at the optimum is not just a classification device, it is the precision of the estimate.
